Cohomology and duality

From the cup product on singular cochains to Poincaré duality, the de Rham theorem, and a first reading of the Serre spectral sequence — the half of algebraic topology that powers algebraic geometry, modular forms, and higher categories.

Reader's note. This page picks up where Algebraic topology §7 left off (where cohomology, the cup product, and Poincaré duality were sketched in a single paragraph). We assume comfort with singular homology and chain complexes, and treat cohomology as a refinement carrying ring structure and powerful duality theorems.

1. Singular cohomology and the cup product

Given a topological space $X$, recall the singular chain complex $(C_\bullet(X;\mathbb{Z}),\partial)$ generated by continuous maps $\sigma\colon\Delta^n\to X$. The singular cochain complex is its $\mathbb{Z}$-linear dual:

$C^n(X;\mathbb{Z}) = \Hom_{\mathbb{Z}}(C_n(X;\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{Z}),\qquad (\delta\varphi)(\sigma) = \varphi(\partial\sigma).$

Then $\delta^2 = 0$ from $\partial^2 = 0$, and we set

$H^n(X;\mathbb{Z}) \;=\; \dfrac{\ker\bigl(\delta\colon C^n\to C^{n+1}\bigr)}{\operatorname{im}\bigl(\delta\colon C^{n-1}\to C^n\bigr)}.$

Why bother dualizing?

Three reasons. First, cohomology is contravariant: $f\colon X\to Y$ induces $f^*\colon H^n(Y)\to H^n(X)$. This matches what geometric objects (forms, cocycles, characteristic classes) actually do under pullback. Second, cohomology has finer torsion behavior than homology — the universal coefficient theorem packs both into one short exact sequence. Third — and this is the important one — cohomology carries a graded-commutative ring structure, the cup product, while homology only has a coalgebra structure that is much harder to use.

The Alexander–Whitney diagonal and the cup product

Given $\varphi\in C^p(X)$ and $\psi\in C^q(X)$, define $\varphi\smile\psi\in C^{p+q}(X)$ by

$(\varphi\smile\psi)(\sigma) = \varphi(\sigma\!\restriction_{[v_0,\ldots,v_p]})\cdot\psi(\sigma\!\restriction_{[v_p,\ldots,v_{p+q}]}).$

The simplex $\sigma\colon\Delta^{p+q}\to X$ is restricted to its front $p$-face and its back $q$-face; the values are multiplied in $\mathbb{Z}$. The Leibniz rule

$\delta(\varphi\smile\psi) = (\delta\varphi)\smile\psi + (-1)^{p}\,\varphi\smile(\delta\psi)$

then ensures that $\smile$ descends to a well-defined operation $\smile\colon H^p(X)\otimes H^q(X)\to H^{p+q}(X)$. On cohomology classes this product is associative, unital (the unit is $1\in H^0(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\#\pi_0}$), and graded-commutative: $[\varphi]\smile[\psi] = (-1)^{pq}[\psi]\smile[\varphi]$.

Why "cup"? The notation $\smile$ appears in Lefschetz; the operation pairs cocycles by chopping a simplex into a front piece and a back piece, then evaluating. The asymmetry of "front vs. back" is what produces the $(-1)^{pq}$ — it is not present at the cochain level, only in cohomology.

Worked example: $H^*(T^2)$

The torus $T^2 = S^1\times S^1$ has CW-structure with one $0$-cell, two $1$-cells $a,b$, and one $2$-cell. Direct computation (or the Künneth theorem of §2) gives

$H^0(T^2;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z},\quad H^1(T^2;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}\alpha\oplus\mathbb{Z}\beta,\quad H^2(T^2;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}(\alpha\smile\beta).$

Graded-commutativity forces $\alpha\smile\alpha = -\alpha\smile\alpha$, hence $2\alpha\smile\alpha = 0$ and (since $H^2$ is torsion-free) $\alpha\smile\alpha = 0$; similarly $\beta\smile\beta = 0$ and $\beta\smile\alpha = -\alpha\smile\beta$. The whole ring is

$H^*(T^2;\mathbb{Z}) \;\cong\; \Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha,\beta] = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha,\beta]/(\alpha^2,\beta^2,\alpha\beta+\beta\alpha),$

the exterior algebra on two degree-1 generators. The class $\alpha\smile\beta$ is detected by integrating over the fundamental $2$-cell: a single non-trivial pairing.

Take-away. The cup product turns the additive cohomology groups $H^n(X;\mathbb{Z})$ into a graded ring $H^*(X;\mathbb{Z})$. Computing this ring is what cohomology calculations are — bare ranks rarely tell topological spaces apart, but ring structures do.

2. Mayer–Vietoris and Künneth

Two ways to compute cohomology by decomposition: cut a space along an open cover (Mayer–Vietoris), or cross two known spaces (Künneth).

The cohomology Mayer–Vietoris sequence

Suppose $X = U\cup V$ is an open cover. The short exact sequence of cochain complexes

$0\to C^*(X) \xrightarrow{} C^*(U)\oplus C^*(V) \xrightarrow{} C^*(U\cap V)\to 0$

induces, by the snake lemma, a long exact sequence in cohomology:

$\cdots\to H^{n-1}(U\cap V)\xrightarrow{\delta}H^n(X)\xrightarrow{(i_U^*,i_V^*)}H^n(U)\oplus H^n(V)\xrightarrow{j_U^*-j_V^*}H^n(U\cap V)\xrightarrow{\delta}H^{n+1}(X)\to\cdots$

The connecting homomorphism $\delta$ raises degree by 1 — opposite to the homological version, where it lowers degree.

Worked example: $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^n)$ by induction. Cover $\mathbb{CP}^n$ by $U = \mathbb{CP}^n\setminus\{[0:\cdots:0:1]\}$ (deformation retracts onto $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$) and $V$ a small open ball around $[0:\cdots:0:1]$ (contractible), with $U\cap V \simeq S^{2n-1}$. The Mayer–Vietoris long exact sequence in cohomology, combined with $H^*(S^{2n-1}) = \mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0, 2n-1$, lets you solve inductively for $$H^k(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z}) = \begin{cases}\mathbb{Z} & 0\le k\le 2n,\;k\text{ even}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$ The cup product turns this into $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{n+1})$ with $|x| = 2$.

Künneth

For two spaces $X$ and $Y$ with finitely-generated cohomology, the Künneth theorem over a field $k$ says

$H^n(X\times Y;k) \;\cong\; \bigoplus_{p+q=n}\,H^p(X;k)\otimes_k H^q(Y;k),$

and the isomorphism is induced by the cross product $\alpha\times\beta = \pi_X^*\alpha\smile\pi_Y^*\beta$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, there is a short exact sequence with a Tor correction:

$0 \to \!\!\!\!\bigoplus_{p+q=n}\!\!H^p(X)\!\otimes\!H^q(Y)\to H^n(X\times Y)\to\!\!\!\!\bigoplus_{p+q=n+1}\!\!\mathrm{Tor}_1^{\mathbb{Z}}(H^p(X),H^q(Y))\to 0,$

which splits (non-canonically). Tor measures the failure of $\otimes$ to preserve injections — it vanishes when one factor is torsion-free.

Worked example. For the torus $T^n = (S^1)^n$, $$H^*(T^n;\mathbb{Z}) \;\cong\; \Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n],$$ the exterior algebra on $n$ degree-1 generators (one per circle factor). $\dim_{\mathbb{Q}} H^k(T^n;\mathbb{Q}) = \binom{n}{k}$. Compare to direct cell-counting: $T^n$ has $\binom{n}{k}$ cells in dimension $k$ — the Künneth and cell decompositions agree as they must.

3. Poincaré duality

Poincaré duality is the deepest "for free" statement in algebraic topology: a closed oriented manifold satisfies a perfect pairing between cohomology and homology in complementary degrees.

Statement

Let $M$ be a closed (compact, no boundary), connected, oriented $n$-manifold. The orientation determines a fundamental class $[M]\in H_n(M;\mathbb{Z})$, the unique generator that restricts to the local orientation at each point. The cap product

$\frown\colon H^k(M;\mathbb{Z})\otimes H_n(M;\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow H_{n-k}(M;\mathbb{Z})$

(an evaluation-then-front/back-cut, dual to the cup product) gives, on capping with $[M]$,

$PD\colon H^k(M;\mathbb{Z})\xrightarrow{\;\;\sim\;\;}H_{n-k}(M;\mathbb{Z}),\qquad \alpha\mapsto\alpha\frown[M].$

Combined with the universal coefficients theorem, this gives a perfect pairing

$H^k(M;\mathbb{Q})\otimes H^{n-k}(M;\mathbb{Q}) \xrightarrow{\smile}H^n(M;\mathbb{Q})\xrightarrow{\int_{[M]}}\mathbb{Q},$

i.e. the cup product followed by integration is non-degenerate. In dimensions where $H^k$ is free, $b_k(M) = b_{n-k}(M)$ (the Betti numbers are palindromic).

What "orientation" is doing. Without orientation the fundamental class lives in $H_n(M;\mathbb{Z}/2)$ and you only get $\mathbb{Z}/2$-coefficient duality. The Möbius band and $\mathbb{RP}^2$ are obstructed in this way: they have integral Poincaré duality only after killing 2-torsion, but $\mathbb{Z}/2$-duality always holds. Lefschetz duality handles manifolds with boundary: $H^k(M,\partial M)\cong H_{n-k}(M)$ — relative cohomology pairs with absolute homology.

Worked example: a closed oriented surface

Let $\Sigma_g$ be the closed orientable surface of genus $g$. Cell-decompose with one $0$-cell, $2g$ one-cells $a_1,b_1,\ldots,a_g,b_g$, and one $2$-cell. Then:

$k$$H_k(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Z})$$H^k(\Sigma_g;\mathbb{Z})$$b_k$
$0$$\mathbb{Z}$$\mathbb{Z}$$1$
$1$$\mathbb{Z}^{2g}$$\mathbb{Z}^{2g}$$2g$
$2$$\mathbb{Z}$$\mathbb{Z}$$1$

Poincaré duality: $b_0 = b_2 = 1$ and $b_1 = b_{n-1} = 2g$ (with $n=2$). The cup product pairing $H^1\otimes H^1\to H^2$ is the symplectic form: $\alpha_i\smile\beta_j = \delta_{ij}\cdot[\Sigma_g]^*$, $\alpha_i\smile\alpha_j = 0$, $\beta_i\smile\beta_j = 0$. The intersection form has signature $0$ (in the symmetric case via $\alpha\smile\beta + \beta\smile\alpha$ on $H^1$, but in dim 2 the natural form is alternating). For 4-manifolds the same construction gives an integral, symmetric, unimodular form whose signature is a deep invariant — the start of the Hirzebruch signature theorem story.

4. De Rham cohomology and the de Rham theorem

On a smooth manifold $M$ we have the differential-form complex $(\Omega^\bullet(M), d)$. The de Rham cohomology $H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ is the quotient closed forms / exact forms.

The de Rham theorem

For any smooth manifold $M$, there is a natural isomorphism of graded $\mathbb{R}$-algebras

$\int\colon H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(M;\mathbb{R})\xrightarrow{\;\;\sim\;\;}H^*(M;\mathbb{R}),$

under which the wedge product on forms corresponds to the cup product on singular cochains. Concretely: a closed $k$-form $\omega$ pairs with a smooth $k$-simplex $\sigma$ via $\int_\sigma\omega$, and Stokes's theorem says this descends to a pairing of cohomology with homology.

Why this is striking: $H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}$ is defined using PDE-theoretic data (smooth forms, the exterior derivative), $H^*$ singular is defined using purely combinatorial data (simplices, cochains). They agree.

Sketch of proof. Both sides are $\delta$-functors satisfying the Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms (Mayer–Vietoris, dimension axiom for a point, homotopy invariance), and they agree on convex open sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (where both are concentrated in degree 0 by the Poincaré lemma). So they agree on every smooth manifold — for instance via the Čech–de Rham double complex applied to a good cover. This is the same machinery that powers the comparison between sheaf cohomology and Čech cohomology on Leray covers (see Sheaf cohomology §5).

Worked example: $H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)=\mathbb{R}$

Parametrize $S^1$ by $\theta\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ and consider the 1-form $d\theta$. It is well-defined (the differential of the multi-valued function $\theta$ patches together because $d$ kills constants), closed (every top-form on a 1-manifold is closed), and not exact: if $d\theta = df$ globally, then by Stokes $$0 = \int_{\partial S^1} f = \int_{S^1} df = \int_{S^1} d\theta = 2\pi,$$ a contradiction. So $[d\theta]\ne 0\in H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)$, and integration $\int_{S^1}\colon H^1_{\mathrm{dR}}(S^1)\to\mathbb{R}$ is an isomorphism (its inverse sends $r\in\mathbb{R}$ to $[(r/2\pi)\,d\theta]$). The corresponding singular cohomology class evaluates to 1 on the fundamental cycle; the de Rham pairing matches.

How de Rham meets Poincaré duality

For a closed oriented $n$-manifold $M$, the de Rham version of Poincaré duality is the statement that the pairing

$H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\otimes H^{n-k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\to\mathbb{R},\qquad ([\omega],[\eta])\mapsto\int_M\omega\wedge\eta$

is non-degenerate. Wedge product on forms corresponds to cup product on cohomology, and the integral is integration of the top-form against the fundamental class. With a Riemannian metric one upgrades this to Hodge theory: every cohomology class has a unique harmonic representative ($\Delta\omega = 0$), and the duality becomes an honest inner product.

5. Spectral sequences (an introduction)

So far we computed cohomology by short exact sequences (Mayer–Vietoris, the LES of a pair) or by tensor products (Künneth). When the geometric input is a fibration $F\to E\to B$ — neither a cover nor a product — there is a third tool.

The general shape

A spectral sequence is a sequence of bigraded pages $(E_r^{p,q}, d_r)$ for $r=1,2,\ldots$, with

  • each $d_r\colon E_r^{p,q}\to E_r^{p+r,\,q-r+1}$ a differential ($d_r^2=0$);
  • $E_{r+1}^{p,q} = H(E_r^{p,q},d_r)$ — the next page is the cohomology of the previous;
  • under finiteness (e.g. boundedness in each total degree), the sequence converges to a graded object $E_\infty^{p,q}$ which is the associated graded of a filtration on the cohomology you wanted.

The clearest mental picture is a $(p,q)$-grid; differentials are arrows of slope $(r, 1-r)$. On the second page they go right by 2, down by 1; on the third, right by 3, down by 2; etc. A class survives to $E_\infty$ iff every $d_r$ entering and leaving it is zero from some page on.

The Serre spectral sequence

For a fibration $F\hookrightarrow E\xrightarrow{\pi}B$ with $B$ simply connected, there is a cohomology spectral sequence with

$E_2^{p,q} = H^p(B;H^q(F))\;\Longrightarrow\; H^{p+q}(E).$

This is the topological cousin of the Leray spectral sequence in sheaf cohomology — the Leray spectral sequence is "Serre for the sheaf of cohomologies along the fibers" (see Sheaf cohomology §4). When $B$ is not simply connected one needs cohomology with coefficients in a local system $\mathcal{H}^q(F)$, but the rough shape is the same.

Worked example: the Hopf fibration

The Hopf fibration is $S^1\hookrightarrow S^3\to S^2$. The base $S^2$ is simply connected, so

$E_2^{p,q} = H^p(S^2)\otimes H^q(S^1).$

Only four entries are non-zero: $(p,q)\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(2,0),(2,1)\}$, each $=\mathbb{Z}$. The only differential of bidegree $(2,-1)$ that can hit anything non-zero is

$d_2\colon E_2^{0,1}\to E_2^{2,0},$

and it must be an isomorphism (sending the generator of $H^1(S^1)$ to the Euler class of the Hopf bundle, which generates $H^2(S^2)$). After taking cohomology, $E_3^{0,1} = E_3^{2,0} = 0$, while $E_3^{0,0} = E_3^{2,1} = \mathbb{Z}$ survive. No further differentials hit, so $E_\infty = E_3$, and the abutment gives

$H^0(S^3) = \mathbb{Z},\quad H^1(S^3) = H^2(S^3) = 0,\quad H^3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z},$

which we already knew. The spectral sequence packages this calculation into an algebraic mechanism that also tells you the Hopf bundle has Euler number 1 — the same number that detects the bundle's non-triviality.

Why spectral sequences feel hard. Three sources of friction. (1) The data is not a single complex but a tower of them, and convergence is a separate axiom you check (boundedness suffices). (2) Differentials $d_r$ for $r\ge 2$ are typically not described by a closed formula — you compute them by reasoning from what they must do (degrees, naturality, multiplicativity). (3) The associated graded $E_\infty$ recovers $H^*$ only up to extensions; resolving the extension problem is a separate task. Despite these caveats, spectral sequences are how 80% of explicit cohomology calculations actually get done in modern algebraic topology, sheaf theory, and arithmetic geometry.

6. Characteristic classes preview

One of the main consumers of cup products and Poincaré duality is the theory of characteristic classes of vector bundles.

The slogan

A real (resp. complex) rank-$n$ vector bundle $\xi\to B$ has cohomology classes

$w_i(\xi)\in H^i(B;\mathbb{Z}/2)\quad(\text{Stiefel–Whitney}),\qquad c_i(\xi)\in H^{2i}(B;\mathbb{Z})\quad(\text{Chern}),$

natural under bundle pullback ($w_i(f^*\xi) = f^* w_i(\xi)$, similarly $c_i$). The classes obstruct triviality: $\xi$ is stably trivial only if all $w_i$ (resp. $c_i$) for $i\ge 1$ vanish. The total class $w(\xi) = 1 + w_1 + w_2 + \cdots$ is multiplicative under direct sum:

$w(\xi\oplus\eta) = w(\xi)\smile w(\eta),\qquad c(\xi\oplus\eta) = c(\xi)\smile c(\eta).$

This Whitney sum formula is one of the few honest applications of the cup product in introductory topology.

Worked example: $c_1$ of the tautological bundle on $\mathbb{CP}^n$

The tautological line bundle $\gamma\to\mathbb{CP}^n$ has fiber over $[\ell]$ equal to the line $\ell\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ itself. Its first Chern class $c_1(\gamma)$ generates $H^2(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$. Cup powers $c_1(\gamma)^k$ generate $H^{2k}$, and the top power $c_1(\gamma)^n$ generates $H^{2n}\cong\mathbb{Z}$ — Poincaré-dual to a point. The whole ring is

$H^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z}) \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}[c_1(\gamma)]/(c_1(\gamma)^{n+1}),$

so cohomology, cup product, and Chern classes all line up into a single statement.

Where this leads: the page Characteristic classes develops Stiefel–Whitney, Chern, Pontryagin, and Euler classes, computes them via classifying spaces, and connects them to integration and the Atiyah–Singer index theorem.

Connections

Cohomology and duality are not "settled" so much as "the lingua franca": every modern theory of invariants — étale cohomology of schemes, Floer cohomology of symplectic manifolds, motivic cohomology, sheaf cohomology of constructible sheaves, equivariant cohomology — is a variation on the themes here, paired with its own duality theorem (Verdier, Grothendieck, Serre, Poincaré–Lefschetz, Atiyah). The frontier is not "what is the cohomology of $X$?" but "for an exotic class of objects, what is the right cohomology, and what duality does it satisfy?"

  • Characteristic classes — Chern classes — built from the cup-product structure surveyed in §6, the workhorses of bundle topology.
  • Atiyah–Singer index theorem — pairs characteristic classes against the fundamental class of a manifold (Poincaré duality at work) to compute analytic indices of elliptic operators.
  • Sheaf cohomology — Leray spectral sequence — the Serre spectral sequence of §5 has a sheaf-theoretic cousin that drives almost every derived-functor cohomology calculation.
  • Hodge theory — refines the de Rham theorem of §4 by decomposing $H^k(X;\mathbb{C})$ of a Kähler manifold into a $(p,q)$-bigrading; Poincaré duality gets enriched to Serre duality and Hodge symmetry.
  • Étale cohomology — the algebraic-geometry analog: Poincaré duality survives (as Poincaré–Lefschetz–Verdier duality), and the Künneth and Leray spectral sequences power Weil-conjecture and Langlands calculations.
  • ∞-categories — the modern home for "cohomology theories" as functors out of stable ∞-categories; spectral sequences and duality become structural rather than ad hoc.
