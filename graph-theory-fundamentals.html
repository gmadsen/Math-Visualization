Graph theory fundamentals

The whole language of networks — degrees, trees, tours, planar drawings, colorings, and matchings — built from one idea: a set of dots and a symmetric relation saying which pairs are joined.

1. Graphs, degrees & the handshake lemma

A (simple, undirected) graph is a pair $G=(V,E)$: a finite set $V$ of vertices and a set $E$ of edges, each edge an unordered pair $\{u,v\}$ of distinct vertices. Equivalently $E$ is a symmetric, irreflexive relation on $V$ — adjacency. We write $u\sim v$ when $\{u,v\}\in E$. The number of vertices $n=|V|$ is the order; $m=|E|$ is the size.

The degree $\deg(v)$ is the number of edges meeting $v$. The richest graph on $n$ vertices is the complete graph $K_n$, with every pair joined: $\binom{n}{2}=\tfrac{n(n-1)}2$ edges and every vertex of degree $n-1$.

The handshake lemma

Sum the degrees over all vertices. Each edge $\{u,v\}$ is counted exactly twice — once at $u$, once at $v$ — so

$\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\deg(v)=2\,|E|.$

The right side is even, so the left side is too. Splitting the sum by parity, the even-degree vertices contribute an even total, hence the odd-degree vertices must also contribute an even total — which forces their count to be even. This is the handshake lemma: in any graph the number of vertices of odd degree is even. (At a party, the number of people who shook hands an odd number of times is even.)

Worked mini-example. Can a graph have degree sequence $(3,3,3,3,3)$? The degrees sum to $15$, which is odd — but $\sum_v\deg(v)=2|E|$ must be even. So no such graph exists: five vertices each of odd degree $3$ would be five odd-degree vertices, an odd count, contradicting the lemma. By contrast $(3,3,2,2,2)$ sums to $12=2\cdot 6$, so it can (and does) describe a $6$-edge graph.

2. Connectivity, trees & spanning trees

A walk is a sequence of vertices each adjacent to the next; a path repeats no vertex. $G$ is connected if every pair of vertices is joined by a path; the maximal connected pieces are its components. Adding an edge can only merge components, never split them — so $k$ edges added to $n$ isolated vertices leave at least $n-k$ components.

A cycle is a closed path of length $\ge 3$. A connected, acyclic graph is a tree. Trees are the leanest connected graphs, and the following are all equivalent for a graph on $n$ vertices:

  • $G$ is a tree (connected and acyclic);
  • $G$ is connected with exactly $n-1$ edges;
  • $G$ is acyclic with exactly $n-1$ edges;
  • any two vertices are joined by a unique path.

Uniqueness of paths is the defining flavor: a second path between two vertices would close a cycle. Removing any edge of a tree disconnects it; adding any edge creates exactly one cycle.

Spanning trees and Cayley's formula

A spanning tree of a connected $G$ is a subgraph that is a tree touching every vertex. Every connected graph has one — keep deleting an edge from any cycle until none remain. How many spanning trees does the complete graph have? Cayley's formula answers it:

$\#\{\text{labeled trees on } n \text{ vertices}\}=n^{\,n-2}.$

So $K_n$ has $n^{n-2}$ spanning trees. (Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem generalizes this to any graph via a Laplacian cofactor.)

Worked mini-example. For $n=4$ Cayley gives $4^{2}=16$ labeled trees, so $K_4$ has $16$ spanning trees: $4$ "stars" (one center joined to the other three) and $12$ "paths" (orderings of the $4$ labels up to reversal, $4!/2=12$). For $n=2$ the formula reads $2^{0}=1$ — a single edge — and for $n=3$, $3^{1}=3$ paths, matching the three ways to pick the middle vertex.

3. Eulerian & Hamiltonian

Two famous traversal questions look superficially alike but have wildly different difficulty. An Eulerian circuit is a closed walk using every edge exactly once; a Hamiltonian cycle is a cycle visiting every vertex exactly once.

Euler's theorem (the easy one)

Euler settled the Königsberg bridges in 1736 with a parity argument. Every time a closed walk passes through a vertex it uses two incident edges — one in, one out — so each vertex's edges must pair up:

A connected graph has an Eulerian circuit $\iff$ every vertex has even degree.

If exactly two vertices are odd, there is no circuit but there is an open Eulerian trail starting at one odd vertex and ending at the other. More than two odd vertices: no Eulerian trail at all. This is a local condition — just read off the degree parities — so Eulerianness is decidable in linear time.

Hamiltonicity (the hard one)

There is no comparable local test. Deciding whether a graph has a Hamiltonian cycle is NP-complete. We have only sufficient conditions, e.g. Dirac's theorem: if $n\ge 3$ and every vertex has degree $\ge n/2$, then $G$ is Hamiltonian. The two notions are independent: a graph can be Eulerian but not Hamiltonian, or vice versa.

Worked mini-example. The Königsberg multigraph has four landmasses of degrees $3,3,3,5$ — four odd vertices, so no Eulerian trail (Euler's negative answer). The complete graph $K_5$ has every vertex of even degree $4$, so it is Eulerian; it is also Hamiltonian (any cyclic order of the $5$ vertices works). But $K_{2,3}$ is Eulerian-free (its degree-$2$ side meets degree-$3$ vertices: degrees $3,3,2,2,2$ give two odd vertices, so it has a trail but no circuit) and not Hamiltonian (any cycle must alternate sides, impossible with unequal parts).

4. Planarity & Euler's formula

A graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane with no edges crossing. Such a drawing carves the plane into faces (regions), counting the unbounded outer region as one face. Euler discovered that for any connected plane graph the alternating count is invariant:

$V - E + F = 2.$

This is Euler's polyhedron formula in disguise (cube: $8-12+6=2$). The proof is induction on edges: a tree has $F=1$ and $E=V-1$, giving $V-(V-1)+1=2$; each added edge either closes a new face ($F{+}1$) or links a new vertex ($V{+}1$), and both moves preserve $V-E+F$.

The edge bound and forbidden minors

Euler's formula caps how dense a planar graph can be. In a simple planar graph with $V\ge 3$, every face is bounded by at least $3$ edges and every edge borders $2$ faces, so $3F\le 2E$. Substituting into $F=2-V+E$ gives the workhorse corollary:

$E \le 3V-6\qquad\text{(and } E\le 2V-4 \text{ if triangle-free).}$

Violating the bound certifies non-planarity. The complete characterization is Kuratowski–Wagner: a graph is planar iff it contains no $K_5$ and no $K_{3,3}$ as a minor (equivalently no subdivision of either).

Worked mini-example. $K_5$: $V=5$, $E=\binom 52=10$, and $3V-6=9\lt 10$ — the bound fails, so $K_5$ is non-planar. $K_{3,3}$: it is triangle-free (bipartite) with $V=6$, $E=9$, and the sharper $2V-4=8\lt 9$ — non-planar too. Both are minimal obstructions: delete any edge of either and the result becomes planar. The octahedron ($V=6$, $E=12$, $F=8$) sits right at $E=3V-6=12$ — a maximal planar (triangulated) graph.

5. Graph coloring

A proper $k$-coloring assigns one of $k$ colors to each vertex so that adjacent vertices differ. The chromatic number $\chi(G)$ is the smallest such $k$. Coloring models conflict-free scheduling: vertices are tasks, edges are conflicts, colors are time slots. Bipartite graphs are exactly the $2$-colorable ones (no odd cycle); $\chi(K_n)=n$; $\chi(C_n)=2$ for even $n$ and $3$ for odd $n$.

Greedy and Brooks

Color vertices one at a time in some order, always picking the smallest color not yet used by an already-colored neighbor. A vertex has at most $\Delta$ neighbors, so some color in $\{1,\dots,\Delta+1\}$ is always free:

$\chi(G)\le \Delta+1.$

The order matters — a clever order can do far better than a bad one, though finding the optimum is NP-hard. Brooks' theorem sharpens the greedy bound: a connected graph has $\chi(G)\le\Delta$ unless it is a complete graph (then $\chi=\Delta+1=n$) or an odd cycle (then $\chi=3=\Delta+1$). Those two families are the only ones needing the extra color.

Planar graphs

Planar graphs are special: the Five Color Theorem has a clean discharging/recoloring proof, while the Four Color Theorem (Appel–Haken, 1976) — that four colors always suffice — is the deep result, proved by reducing to thousands of computer-checked configurations.

Worked mini-example. The $5$-cycle $C_5$ has $\Delta=2$, so greedy promises $\chi\le 3$. It is an odd cycle, so it is not bipartite ($\chi\ne 2$) and indeed $\chi(C_5)=3$ — a Brooks exception. The Petersen graph has $\Delta=3$ but is neither complete nor an odd cycle, so Brooks gives $\chi\le 3$, and in fact $\chi=3$. Meanwhile $K_4$ (which is planar) needs all $4$ colors, saturating the Four Color bound.

6. Matchings & Hall's theorem

A matching $M\subseteq E$ is a set of edges no two of which share a vertex. A vertex in some edge of $M$ is matched; a matching is perfect if it covers all vertices. The bipartite case — vertices split into parts $X$ and $Y$, all edges crossing — is the cleanest and most useful (job assignments, the "marriage problem").

Hall's marriage theorem

When can we match every vertex of $X$ into $Y$? Write $N(S)$ for the set of all neighbors of a subset $S\subseteq X$. If some $S$ has fewer neighbors than members, those members cannot all be matched. Hall (1935) proved this obvious obstruction is the only one:

$X$ can be matched into $Y$ $\iff$ $|N(S)|\ge |S|$ for every $S\subseteq X$.

Augmenting paths and König

Matchings are built by augmenting paths: a path that alternates non-matching/matching edges and starts and ends at unmatched vertices. Flipping every edge along it grows $M$ by one. Berge's theorem says $M$ is maximum iff no augmenting path exists — the algorithmic heart of bipartite matching. The min-max dual is König's theorem: in a bipartite graph,

$\max\ \text{matching size}=\min\ \text{vertex-cover size},$

the bipartite shadow of LP duality / max-flow–min-cut. (For general graphs the analogue is the Tutte–Berge formula.)

Worked mini-example. Three applicants $x_1,x_2,x_3$ all qualified only for the same two jobs $y_1,y_2$: take $S=\{x_1,x_2,x_3\}$, then $N(S)=\{y_1,y_2\}$ so $|N(S)|=2\lt 3=|S|$ — Hall fails, no full assignment exists, and a maximum matching has size $2$. By König a minimum vertex cover also has size $2$ (the two jobs cover every edge). Add a third job $y_3$ adjacent to $x_3$ and Hall's condition is restored: a perfect matching $x_1y_1,x_2y_2,x_3y_3$ appears.

Connections

These six ideas are the trunk from which the rest of the section grows. Connectivity becomes a spectral question once you encode a graph as a matrix; the handshake lemma and planar face counts are the entry point to topological combinatorics; matching's min-max duality is the combinatorial face of linear programming. The frontier here is less about settling questions than about how hard they are: Eulerian is trivial, Hamiltonian is NP-complete, and the gap between them is a microcosm of complexity theory.

  • Spectral graph theory — adjacency & Laplacian — connectivity and components reappear as the spectrum and kernel of $L=D-A$.
  • Extremal combinatorics — Turán's theorem — once you can count edges and forbid subgraphs, you ask how many edges force a $K_{r+1}$.
  • Simplicial complexes — the f-vector & Euler characteristic — Euler's $V-E+F=2$ is the genus-$0$ case of the topological invariant $\chi$.
  • Combinatorial optimization — LP duality — König's max-matching = min-cover is the integral shadow of LP duality and max-flow–min-cut.
  • Probabilistic method — linearity of expectation — random graphs and colorings turn these existence questions into expectation calculations.
