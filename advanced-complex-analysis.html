Advanced complex analysis

Picard's theorems, Weierstrass and Mittag-Leffler construction, Phragmén-Lindelöf, Hadamard three-circles, Hardy-space boundary behavior, Bloch and Nevanlinna value distribution, Bergman kernels, quasiconformal maps with the Beltrami equation, and several complex variables — the graduate sequel to complex analysis.

1. The graduate landscape

Undergraduate complex analysis stops once Cauchy's theorem, the residue calculus, and conformal mapping are in hand. The graduate sequence picks up four threads that classical textbooks leave dangling, all asking sharper versions of the same question: what makes entire and meromorphic functions special, and how do they behave at the edges of their domain — at infinity, at an essential singularity, on the boundary of a disk?

The four threads are:

  • Value distribution. Liouville says a bounded entire function is constant. Little Picard sharpens this to: a non-constant entire function omits at most one value. Great Picard upgrades Casorati-Weierstrass at an essential singularity from "dense image" to "every value, infinitely often, with at most one exception." Bloch's theorem (a cousin we touch on only in passing) gives a quantitative geometric statement: a holomorphic function on the disk with $|f'(0)|=1$ contains a disk of universal radius in its image.
  • Construction theorems. Polynomials factor as $\prod(z-a_k)$. Weierstrass generalizes this to entire functions with prescribed zero set, paying for convergence with exponential corrections. Mittag-Leffler is the dual statement on the meromorphic side: prescribe poles and principal parts, get a meromorphic function via convergent partial fractions.
  • Growth and maximum-modulus. The maximum modulus principle (an undergraduate result) needs an unbounded analog: Phragmén-Lindelöf says max modulus survives on an unbounded sector if you bound growth at infinity, and Hadamard's three-circles theorem upgrades this to a precise convexity statement on annuli — the workhorse behind the Riesz-Thorin and Hadamard interpolation theorems.
  • Boundary behavior. A holomorphic function on the disk needs no boundary values in general — but if you control the $L^p$ norm on circles $|z|=r$, you land in the Hardy space $H^p(\mathbb{D})$, and Fatou's theorem produces non-tangential boundary values almost everywhere. The inner-outer factorization $f = B\cdot S\cdot O$ is the analog of Weierstrass on the disk side.

Each thread is internally tight (the theorems chain) and the threads cross-link: Picard's proof uses the modular function $\lambda$, which lives on the upper half-plane; the Hardy-space inner-outer factorization is a Weierstrass theorem on the disk; Phragmén-Lindelöf is the engine inside Hadamard three-circles. The remainder of this page walks the four threads in order.

2. Little Picard theorem

Liouville's theorem — bounded entire functions are constant — is a striking constraint, but it leaves the door wide open: an unbounded entire function might still avoid huge swaths of $\mathbb{C}$. The exponential $e^z$ is unbounded, surely, but it never equals zero. Could a non-constant entire function omit two values? Three? An open set?

Little Picard theorem. If $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ is entire and omits two distinct values, then $f$ is constant. Equivalently, a non-constant entire function takes every complex value with at most one exception.

The bound 'at most one' is sharp: $e^z$ omits $0$ and only $0$. Translating, $a + e^z$ omits $a$ and only $a$, so any single value can be the omitted one. But two omitted values — never.

Why the proof is hard

Liouville is one line: differentiate Cauchy's integral formula, take radius to infinity. Little Picard is dramatically harder, because the conclusion is essentially topological while the natural data are analytic. The standard proof goes through the elliptic modular function $\lambda:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$, a holomorphic surjection from the upper half-plane to the twice-punctured plane that is a covering map.

If $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$ omits both $0$ and $1$, then by post-composing with a Möbius transformation we may assume the two omitted values are exactly $0$ and $1$. Now $f$ lifts through the universal cover of $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$, which is the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}$. The lift $\tilde f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{H}$ is then a holomorphic map from $\mathbb{C}$ to a bounded region (the disk, after Cayley) — bounded entire — hence constant by Liouville. So $f$ itself is constant.

Concept check. The proof effectively converts the topological obstruction (the fundamental group $\pi_1(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\})$ is free on two generators, while $\pi_1(\mathbb{C})$ is trivial, so $f$ must lift) into a Liouville-type analytic statement on the cover. This pattern — descend to a cover, win by Liouville on the cover — recurs throughout value-distribution theory.

Examples and non-examples

omits one $e^z$ omits $0$; $\cos z + e^z$ is non-constant entire, hence by little Picard omits at most one value.

omits zero Polynomials of degree $\ge 1$, $\sin z$, $\cos z$ — all surjective onto $\mathbb{C}$ by FTA-like arguments.

forbidden Any non-constant entire function omitting two values. There is none, by little Picard.

Little Picard generalizes immediately: an entire function whose image lies in a Riemann surface that admits a holomorphic universal cover by the disk (a hyperbolic Riemann surface) must be constant. The omitted-pair theorem is the special case of $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$.

3. Great Picard theorem

Little Picard controls entire functions globally. Great Picard does the same job locally, near an essential singularity — and the conclusion is even more dramatic.

Recall the trichotomy from the classification of singularities: an isolated singularity is either removable (bounded), a pole (Laurent has finitely many negative terms), or essential (Laurent has infinitely many). Casorati-Weierstrass already gave us a striking statement at an essential singularity: the image of any punctured neighborhood is dense in $\mathbb{C}$. Great Picard sharpens 'dense' to 'every value, infinitely often.'

Great Picard theorem. Suppose $f$ has an essential singularity at $z_0$. Then in every punctured neighborhood of $z_0$, $f$ takes every complex value infinitely often, with at most one exception.

The witness is, again, the exponential. Consider $f(z) = e^{1/z}$ near $z=0$. Substitute $w = 1/z$: as $z$ ranges over the punctured disk $0<|z|<\varepsilon$, $w$ ranges over the exterior $|w|>1/\varepsilon$. The image $e^w$ on this exterior misses only $0$ (because $e^w$ never vanishes), and hits every other complex value with infinitely many preimages — the preimages of $w_0\ne 0$ are $\log w_0 + 2\pi i\mathbb{Z}$, all of which eventually fall in the exterior. So $e^{1/z}$ near $0$ omits exactly $\{0\}$, and that's the one allowed exceptional value.

Compatibility with little Picard

The two theorems are consistent because every non-constant entire function $f$ that is not a polynomial has an essential singularity at $\infty$ (in the sense that $f(1/w)$ has an essential singularity at $w=0$). Applying great Picard near $\infty$ then says $f$ takes every value infinitely often except possibly one — and outside a large compact set this is enough to conclude that globally on $\mathbb{C}$, $f$ omits at most one value (by the identity principle: if it hit a value finitely often globally, it would hit it only inside the compact set, but Picard says it must hit it infinitely often outside).

For polynomials, $\infty$ is a pole rather than essential, and the omitted-value bound is replaced by 'surjective' — a polynomial of positive degree hits every value $w$ exactly $\deg(p)$ times.

Sharpness, again

The 'at most one exception' bound is sharp: $e^{1/z}$ near $0$ omits exactly $\{0\}$. Translating gives essential singularities omitting any single prescribed value. But omitting two near an essential singularity — impossible.

The proofs of little and great Picard are essentially the same: in both cases one lifts through the universal cover $\lambda:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$ and applies Liouville (or its local analog, the Schwarz lemma). Modern proofs via Schottky's theorem and Montel's theorem give a quantitative form: there is an explicit bound on $|f|$ on a disk, depending only on the omitted values and the disk radius, which forces the singularity to be removable or polar — incompatible with 'essential.'

4. Weierstrass factorization

A polynomial of degree $n$ factors as $c\prod_{k=1}^n (z-a_k)$, the product running over its zero set. Weierstrass factorization generalizes this to entire functions: prescribe a zero set, get an entire function vanishing on it.

The naïve product $\prod_n(1 - z/a_n)$ over an infinite zero sequence $a_n\to\infty$ generally diverges — the terms approach $1$, but not fast enough. Weierstrass's idea is to multiply each factor by an exponential correction so the corrected factor approaches $1$ rapidly enough for the product to converge.

Elementary factors

Elementary factor of order $p$. $$E_p(z) = (1-z)\exp\!\left(z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \cdots + \tfrac{z^p}{p}\right),\qquad E_0(z) = 1-z.$$ The exponential is precisely the truncated Taylor series of $-\log(1-z)$. Consequently $\log E_p(z) = -\sum_{k>p} z^k/k = O(z^{p+1})$ near $z=0$, and one shows $|E_p(z) - 1|\le |z|^{p+1}$ for $|z|\le 1$.

The elementary factor still has a simple zero at $z=1$ (from the $(1-z)$ piece) but is much closer to $1$ on a disk than $(1-z)$ alone — exactly the rate of decay needed to make an infinite product convergent.

Weierstrass factorization theorem. Let $\{a_n\}$ be a discrete sequence in $\mathbb{C}$ with $|a_n|\to\infty$, and let $m\ge 0$ be an integer. There exist integers $p_n\ge 0$ such that $$f(z) = z^m \prod_{n=1}^\infty E_{p_n}\!\left(\frac{z}{a_n}\right)$$ converges to an entire function with a zero of order $m$ at $0$, simple zeros at each $a_n$ (with prescribed multiplicities by repeating $a_n$), and no other zeros. Moreover any entire function $g$ with this prescribed zero set has the form $g(z) = e^{h(z)} f(z)$ for some entire $h$.

The $e^{h(z)}$ is the slack: two entire functions with the same zeros differ by a nowhere-vanishing entire function, and a nowhere-vanishing entire function is the exponential of an entire function (lift $\log f$ to the simply connected $\mathbb{C}$).

The sine product

The classical witness: $\sin(\pi z)$ has zeros exactly at $\mathbb{Z}$. Pairing $\pm n$ in the Weierstrass product gives the celebrated identity

$$\sin(\pi z) \;=\; \pi z \prod_{n=1}^\infty \left(1 - \frac{z^2}{n^2}\right).$$

Pairing was needed: the unpaired product $\prod (1 - z/n)$ diverges, but $\prod(1 - z^2/n^2) = \prod(1-z/n)(1+z/n)$ does not — the two divergences cancel. Equivalently, the elementary factor $E_1(z/n)$ already includes the linear correction; the sine product is the result of doing this correctly.

Setting $z = 1/2$ rearranges into the Wallis product $\prod_{n\ge 1}(1 - 1/(4n^2)) = 2/\pi$. Setting $z = 1/3$ gives $\prod(1 - 1/(9n^2)) = 3\sqrt{3}/(2\pi)$. The Weierstrass product is a vending machine for these identities.

The Gamma function

The other canonical Weierstrass product is for $1/\Gamma(z)$, an entire function with simple zeros at $0,-1,-2,\ldots$:

$$\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)} \;=\; z e^{\gamma z}\prod_{n=1}^\infty\left(1+\frac{z}{n}\right)e^{-z/n},$$

where $\gamma$ is the Euler-Mascheroni constant. The factor $e^{-z/n}$ is the exponential correction in $E_1(-z/n)$; the prefactor $e^{\gamma z}$ comes from the freedom to multiply by $e^{h(z)}$. Hadamard's later refinement (Hadamard factorization) sharpens Weierstrass for entire functions of finite order: the correction $h(z)$ is then a polynomial whose degree is bounded by the order.

5. Mittag-Leffler theorem

Weierstrass tells us we can prescribe a zero set on the entire-function side. Mittag-Leffler is the dual statement on the meromorphic side: we can prescribe a pole set together with the principal parts at those poles.

A principal part at $a$ is the polar piece of the Laurent expansion: a finite sum $P_n(1/(z-a)) = c_{-k}/(z-a)^k + \cdots + c_{-1}/(z-a)$. The principal part is exactly the obstruction to extending holomorphically across $a$: $f$ minus its principal part is holomorphic at $a$.

Mittag-Leffler theorem. Let $\{a_n\}$ be a discrete sequence in $\mathbb{C}$ with $|a_n|\to\infty$, and let $P_n(w)$ be a polynomial in $w$ with $P_n(0) = 0$ for each $n$. Then there exists a meromorphic function on $\mathbb{C}$ with principal part $P_n(1/(z-a_n))$ at each $a_n$ and no other poles. It can be written as $$f(z) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \Bigl[P_n\!\left(\tfrac{1}{z-a_n}\right) - p_n(z)\Bigr] + g(z),$$ where $p_n$ is a polynomial chosen as a convergence-correction (a partial Taylor series of $P_n(1/(z-a_n))$ at $z=0$, truncated to depth depending on $|a_n|$) and $g$ is any entire function.

The convergence correction is the analog of the exponential correction in Weierstrass: each individual term $P_n(1/(z-a_n))$ produces a divergent series in general (think of harmonic-like decay), so we subtract the leading Taylor terms of each principal part at $0$ to get a convergent series. The corrections $p_n$ are entire (in fact polynomial), so they do not change the principal parts.

The cotangent expansion

The classical witness: $\pi\cot(\pi z)$ has simple poles at every integer $n$, each with residue $1$. The principal part at $n$ is $1/(z-n)$. The Mittag-Leffler series is

$$\pi\cot(\pi z) \;=\; \frac{1}{z} \;+\; \sum_{n\ne 0}\!\left(\frac{1}{z-n} + \frac{1}{n}\right).$$

The corrections $+1/n$ are exactly what's needed: $1/(z-n) + 1/n = z/(n(z-n))$, which decays like $1/n^2$ — convergent. Without the corrections, the bare series $\sum 1/(z-n)$ diverges (compare $\sum 1/n$).

This identity is the analytic engine behind several gems. Differentiating gives

$$\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)} \;=\; \sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{(z-n)^2},$$

a (now absolutely convergent) Mittag-Leffler expansion for the squared-cosecant. Setting $z=1/2$ recovers $\sum_{n\ge 0}1/(2n+1)^2 = \pi^2/8$, hence Euler's $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$.

6. Phragmén-Lindelöf principle

The maximum modulus principle says: if $f$ is holomorphic on a bounded domain $D$ and continuous on $\overline D$, then $|f|$ attains its max on the boundary $\partial D$. The hypothesis 'bounded' is essential — on an unbounded domain there is a 'missing arc at infinity' to which the boundary statement does not extend.

Counterexample: on the right half-plane $\{\operatorname{Re} z>0\}$, the function $f(z) = e^z$ is bounded by $1$ on the imaginary boundary ($|e^{iy}|=1$) but blows up exponentially as $\operatorname{Re} z\to\infty$. So plain max modulus is false on unbounded domains.

The Phragmén-Lindelöf principle rescues max modulus on a sector by trading boundedness on the boundary for a controlled growth condition: as long as $f$ doesn't grow too fast compared to the opening of the sector, the boundary estimate persists in the interior.

Phragmén-Lindelöf, sector form. Let $S = \{z = re^{i\theta} : 0
  • $|f(z)|\le M$ on $\partial S$ (the two boundary rays).
  • There exist constants $C, \beta$ with $0\le \beta < \alpha$ such that $|f(z)|\le C e^{|z|^\beta}$ on $S$.
    • Then $|f(z)|\le M$ throughout $S$.

    The key constraint is $\beta < \alpha$, with strict inequality. The exponent $\alpha$ measures the 'narrowness' of the sector — narrower sectors permit more aggressive growth — and the function is allowed to grow no faster than $e^{|z|^\beta}$ for some $\beta$ strictly below the threshold.

    Why the example $e^z$ on the right half-plane is critical

    The right half-plane has opening $\pi$, so $\alpha = 1$. The function $e^z$ has $|e^z| = e^{\operatorname{Re} z} \le e^{|z|}$, growth at exponent $\beta = 1 = \alpha$ exactly. This sits on the boundary of the Phragmén-Lindelöf condition: $\beta < \alpha$ fails (with strict inequality), so the conclusion need not hold. And indeed $|e^z|$ is bounded by $1$ on the imaginary axis but blows up in the half-plane — exhibiting failure at the critical exponent.

    If we had $\beta < 1$ (any sub-exponential growth) on the right half-plane, the conclusion holds. The principle is sharp; the threshold is fully optimal.

    Proof sketch via auxiliary growth

    Choose $\gamma$ with $\beta < \gamma < \alpha$. Multiply $f$ by an auxiliary $g_\varepsilon(z) = e^{-\varepsilon z^\gamma}$ (with a fixed branch on the sector). On the boundary, $|f g_\varepsilon|\le M\cdot 1 = M$ since $|g_\varepsilon|\le 1$ on the rays. The growth of $f g_\varepsilon$ is at most $e^{|z|^\beta - \varepsilon|z|^\gamma\cos(\gamma\theta)}$. The cosine factor stays bounded below away from $0$ on the sector (this is where $\gamma<\alpha$ is used), so $|f g_\varepsilon|\to 0$ as $|z|\to\infty$. Now apply ordinary max modulus: $|f g_\varepsilon|\le M$ on the bounded subdomain, and taking $\varepsilon\to 0$ gives $|f|\le M$ on the sector.

    Three-lines theorem and applications

    The strip version (between two parallel lines) is the Hadamard three-lines theorem: if $f$ is bounded and holomorphic on the strip $a\le\operatorname{Re} z\le b$, then $\log\sup_{\operatorname{Re} z = x}|f|$ is convex in $x$. This is the engine inside the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem: bounding an operator on $L^{p_0}$ and $L^{p_1}$ gives a bound on $L^{p_\theta}$ via a holomorphic family of operators living on a strip, with norm controlled by Phragmén-Lindelöf.

    7. Hadamard's three-circles theorem

    Phragmén-Lindelöf gives qualitative max-modulus on unbounded domains. Hadamard's three-circles theorem sharpens this on annuli to a precise convexity statement, and it is the workhorse behind interpolation theorems and growth bounds throughout analysis.

    Let $f$ be holomorphic on the annulus $r_1 \le |z| \le r_3$, and define $M(r) = \max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|$ — the supremum of $|f|$ on the circle of radius $r$.

    Hadamard's three-circles theorem. $\log M(r)$ is a convex function of $\log r$ on $[\log r_1, \log r_3]$. Equivalently, for $r_1 < r_2 < r_3$, $$M(r_2)^{\log(r_3/r_1)} \;\le\; M(r_1)^{\log(r_3/r_2)}\,M(r_3)^{\log(r_2/r_1)}.$$

    Why convexity of $\log M(r)$ in $\log r$?

    The function $r^a$ has $\log M(r) = a\log r$, exactly linear in $\log r$. The three-circles theorem says: among all holomorphic functions, the power functions $z^a$ are extremal — they make $\log M$ exactly linear, and every other holomorphic function gives at most the linear interpolation, not more. This is convexity (in the $\log\log$ sense), and convex functions are exactly those with this 'no chord goes above' property.

    The proof is a clean application of the maximum modulus principle to an auxiliary function. Fix $r_1 < r_2 < r_3$. Choose a real exponent $a$ such that $M(r_1) r_1^a = M(r_3) r_3^a$ — this is the slope that makes the bounds equal at the endpoints. Now consider $g(z) = z^a f(z)$ (with a branch of $z^a$ on a slit annulus). On both the inner and outer boundary, $|g| = M(r_1)r_1^a = M(r_3)r_3^a$. By max modulus, $|g|\le$ this same value throughout, in particular on $|z|=r_2$. Rearranging gives the inequality.

    (The branch issue is real: $z^a$ for non-integer $a$ is multi-valued. The standard fix is to argue with $|z|^a |f(z)|$, which is the well-defined modulus of any branch, or to slit the annulus and use Phragmén-Lindelöf-type arguments.)

    Worked example

    Suppose $f$ is holomorphic on $1\le|z|\le 4$ with $M(1) = 1$ and $M(4) = 16$. The convexity bound on $M(2)$ comes from interpolating $\log M$ between $\log 1 = 0$ and $\log 4$:

    $$\log M(2) \;\le\; \frac{\log 4 - \log 2}{\log 4}\log M(1) \;+\; \frac{\log 2}{\log 4}\log M(4) \;=\; 0 + \tfrac12\log 16 \;=\; \log 4.$$

    So $M(2)\le 4$. The bound is achieved by $f(z) = z^2$: $M(1) = 1$, $M(2) = 4$, $M(4) = 16$, so $\log M$ is exactly linear in $\log r$.

    Three-lines, three-circles, three-spheres

    The statement has cousins for strips (three-lines, where $\log M$ is convex in $\operatorname{Re} z$), for harmonic functions (three-spheres, where the maximum on a sphere is convex in $\log r$), and for $L^p$ norms (the Hadamard interpolation theorem, where $\log\|f\|_p$ is a convex function of $1/p$). All three sit on the same foundation: a maximum-modulus or sub-mean-value principle plus a clever auxiliary function. Phragmén-Lindelöf enters when the strip or sector is unbounded; on a closed annulus, plain max modulus suffices.

    8. Hardy spaces and Fatou boundary

    A holomorphic function on the unit disk $\mathbb{D}$ has no obligation to extend to the boundary $\partial\mathbb{D}$. The function $\exp\bigl(\frac{z+1}{z-1}\bigr)$ is bounded and holomorphic on $\mathbb{D}$ but oscillates wildly as $z\to 1$ — no continuous boundary extension exists. Yet bounded holomorphic functions on $\mathbb{D}$ do have almost everywhere boundary values, and this is the gateway to a rich theory.

    The Hardy space $H^p(\mathbb{D})$ is defined for $0 < p \le \infty$ as the holomorphic functions on $\mathbb{D}$ whose $L^p$ norms on circles $|z|=r$ stay uniformly bounded:

    $$\|f\|_{H^p}^p \;=\; \sup_{0

    and $\|f\|_{H^\infty} = \sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|$. The norms are nested: $H^\infty\subset H^2\subset H^p\subset H^{p'}$ for $p>p'$.

    Fatou's boundary theorem

    Fatou's theorem. If $f\in H^p(\mathbb{D})$ for some $p\ge 1$, then for almost every $\theta\in [0,2\pi)$, the non-tangential limit $$f^*(e^{i\theta}) \;=\; \lim_{z\to e^{i\theta},\;z\in\Gamma_\theta} f(z)$$ exists, where $\Gamma_\theta$ is any Stolz cone with vertex at $e^{i\theta}$. Moreover $f^*\in L^p(\partial\mathbb{D})$ and $\|f^*\|_{L^p(\partial\mathbb{D})} = \|f\|_{H^p}$.

    The 'non-tangential' qualifier is sharp: there exist bounded holomorphic functions on $\mathbb{D}$ with no tangential limit at certain boundary points. But within a Stolz cone — a cone of fixed aperture pointing into the disk — the limit exists almost everywhere.

    Inner-outer factorization

    The structural theorem of Hardy spaces is the inner-outer factorization, the disk-side analog of Weierstrass:

    Inner-outer factorization. Every $f\in H^p(\mathbb{D})$ with $f\not\equiv 0$ factors uniquely (up to a unimodular constant) as $f = B\cdot S\cdot O$, where:
    • $B$ Blaschke product $B(z) = z^m\prod_n \frac{|a_n|}{a_n}\cdot\frac{a_n - z}{1 - \overline{a_n}z}$, encoding the zeros $\{a_n\}$ of $f$ (which must satisfy $\sum (1-|a_n|)<\infty$ for the product to converge).
    • $S$ singular inner factor $S(z) = \exp\!\bigl(-\int\frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z}\,d\mu(\theta)\bigr)$ for a positive singular measure $\mu$ on $\partial\mathbb{D}$ — captures the boundary 'mass' of $f$ that is not absolutely continuous.
    • $O$ outer function $O(z) = \exp\!\bigl(\int\frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z}\log|f^*(e^{i\theta})|\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\bigr)$ — the unique zero-free, non-singular factor whose log-modulus is the Poisson integral of $\log|f^*|$.
    $B$ and $S$ are inner functions: holomorphic on $\mathbb{D}$ with $|f^*(e^{i\theta})| = 1$ a.e. on $\partial\mathbb{D}$.

    The Blaschke product is the disk analog of $\prod E_p(z/a_n)$ in Weierstrass — a canonical factor encoding the zero set with the right convergence corrections. Each Blaschke factor $\phi_a(z) = (a-z)/(1-\overline a z)$ is a disk automorphism, with $|\phi_a(e^{i\theta})| = 1$.

    The Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality

    Behind Fatou's theorem is a real-variable lemma: the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function $Mf(\theta) = \sup_{r}\frac{1}{2r}\int_{\theta-r}^{\theta+r}|f^*(e^{i\phi})|\,d\phi$ controls the radial limit. The maximal-function inequality $\|Mf\|_{L^p}\lesssim \|f\|_{L^p}$ for $1

    Why $H^p$ matters

    $H^p$ spaces are the natural setting for boundary-value problems on the disk, for moment problems, and for one-sided Fourier analysis. The shift operator $T:f(z)\mapsto z f(z)$ on $H^2(\mathbb{D})$ has a complete spectral theory in terms of the inner-outer factorization (Beurling's theorem on invariant subspaces). On the half-plane side, $H^p(\mathbb{H})$ governs the Paley-Wiener theorem connecting analytic continuation to band-limited Fourier transforms. The boundary trace map $H^p\to L^p$ is continuous and its image is exactly the $L^p$ functions whose Fourier coefficients vanish for negative frequencies — the analog of holomorphy in the frequency domain.

    9. Bloch's theorem

    Picard's theorems are qualitative: a non-constant entire map omits at most one value, an essential singularity attains every value (with one exception) infinitely often. Bloch's theorem upgrades the value-distribution thread to a quantitative geometric statement on the disk: every normalized holomorphic function contains a univalent disk of universal radius in its image.

    Bloch's theorem. Let $f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}$ be holomorphic with $f(0)=0$ and $f'(0)=1$. Then $f(\mathbb{D})$ contains an open disk on which $f$ is one-to-one (a univalent sub-disk), of radius at least the Bloch constant $B>0$. The known lower bound is $B\ge \sqrt{3}/4 \approx 0.4330$; the conjectured exact value (Ahlfors-Grunsky) is $B \approx 0.4719$.

    Two clauses of the conclusion are independent and worth separating:

    • The image $f(\mathbb{D})$ is large. The Landau constant $L$ is the analogous universal lower bound on the radius of any open disk in $f(\mathbb{D})$ (not necessarily univalent). $L\ge B$, with the sharp lower bound $L\ge 1/2$.
    • $f$ is univalent on a sub-disk. Bloch's actual statement is the stronger: there is a sub-disk of $\mathbb{D}$ on which $f$ is one-to-one, mapping it bijectively onto a disk of radius $\ge B$ in the image. The univalent sub-disk is what makes the theorem nontrivial — without it, an injective sub-region of the image is not guaranteed.

    Why this is in the value-distribution thread

    Picard says that an unbounded entire function cannot omit two values; effectively, the image must be 'almost all' of $\mathbb{C}$. Bloch on the disk says that even with the unit-disk domain restriction, the image must contain at least a disk of universal radius — the holomorphic function is forced to 'cover' a chunk of the plane. The same theme: holomorphic constraints + a normalization make the image geometrically large. Bloch is the disk-side, quantitative cousin of Picard.

    The Bloch constant has a beautiful extremal characterization. Any holomorphic $f$ with $f(0)=0, f'(0)=1$ defines a Riemann surface $f(\mathbb{D})$ (with branch points where $f'=0$), and the question is: what is the largest disk that fits as a univalent sheet of this surface? The Bloch lower bound $\sqrt{3}/4$ was proved by Ahlfors (1938) using the Schwarz-Pick lemma in the metric of constant negative curvature; the upper bound $0.4719$ comes from a candidate extremal function with hexagonal (equilateral-triangular) lattice symmetry.

    The Schottky-Landau-Bloch chain

    A family of related universal bounds organizes the quantitative side of value distribution. Schottky's theorem: any holomorphic $f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$ has $|f(z)|$ bounded above by a universal function of $|z|$ and $|f(0)|$, even though $f$ depends on infinitely many parameters. Schottky implies Montel's theorem: holomorphic functions $\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$ form a normal family. Both feed into the modern proof of Picard's theorems via normal families. Bloch sits at the geometric end of the same arsenal — a universal lower bound on image size rather than an upper bound on $|f|$.

    Quantitative Picard (Bloch) and qualitative Picard (omitting two values) are now both consequences of the same circle of ideas: normalization + holomorphy + the modular function $\lambda$ on $\mathbb{H}$ produce universal bounds.

    10. Nevanlinna theory

    Picard's theorems are sharp in count: a meromorphic function on $\mathbb{C}$ omits at most two values from $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$. But Picard says nothing about which values are almost omitted, or how the rate at which $f$ takes a value $a$ compares to the rate at which it grows. Nevanlinna theory (Rolf Nevanlinna, 1925) supplies the missing quantitative theory of value distribution for meromorphic functions.

    The characteristic function $T(r,f)$

    The central object is the Nevanlinna characteristic

    $$T(r,f) \;=\; m(r,\infty) \;+\; N(r,\infty),$$

    a sum of two pieces:

    • proximity $m(r,a) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} \log^+\bigl|\frac{1}{f(re^{i\theta}) - a}\bigr|\,d\theta$ — the average of $\log^+ \frac{1}{|f-a|}$ on the circle $|z|=r$, measuring how often and how closely $f$ approaches $a$.
    • counting $N(r,a) = \int_0^r \frac{n(t,a) - n(0,a)}{t}\,dt + n(0,a)\log r$, the integrated number of times $f$ takes the value $a$ in $\{|z|\le t\}$ (with multiplicity), where $n(t,a)$ is the unweighted count.

    For $a = \infty$ the proximity uses $\log^+|f|$ instead of $\log^+ 1/|f-a|$; the counting counts poles. The convention $\log^+ x = \max(\log x, 0)$ keeps the integrand nonnegative.

    First main theorem

    First main theorem. For every $a \in \hat{\mathbb{C}}$ and every meromorphic $f$, $$m(r,a) + N(r,a) \;=\; T(r,f) + O(1)\quad\text{as } r \to \infty.$$

    This is a remarkable conservation law: the total 'mass' $m + N$ for any value $a$ equals $T(r,f) + O(1)$, independent of $a$. In particular, the order $\rho(f) = \limsup_{r\to\infty}\log T(r,f)/\log r$ is intrinsic to $f$ and measures growth on a logarithmic scale.

    Second main theorem and the defect relation

    Picard says $f$ omits at most two values. Nevanlinna's second main theorem upgrades this to a quantitative inequality involving any finite set of distinct values $a_1,\ldots,a_q\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}$:

    $$(q-2)\,T(r,f) \;\le\; \sum_{j=1}^q N_1(r,a_j) \;+\; S(r,f),$$

    where $N_1$ counts $a_j$-points without multiplicity and $S(r,f)$ is a small remainder ($S(r,f) = O(\log T(r,f) + \log r)$ outside an exceptional set of finite Lebesgue measure). Defining the defect $\delta(a) = \liminf_{r\to\infty} m(r,a)/T(r,f)$ — the asymptotic share of the characteristic concentrated in the proximity term at $a$ — the second main theorem gives the defect relation:

    Defect relation. $\sum_{a\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}} \delta(a) \;\le\; 2$, with summation over all values $a$ at which $\delta$ is nonzero.

    Picard recovered as a corollary

    If $f$ omits $a$, then $N(r,a) = 0$, and the first main theorem forces $m(r,a) = T(r,f) + O(1)$, so $\delta(a) = 1$. The defect relation $\sum \delta(a) \le 2$ then says at most two values can have $\delta(a) = 1$ — at most two values can be omitted by a meromorphic function. For entire $f$ (no poles), $\delta(\infty) = 1$ already, leaving at most one more omitted value: little Picard.

    The same defect relation gives finer information than Picard. For example, for $f(z) = e^z$ the defects are $\delta(0) = 1, \delta(\infty) = 1$, summing exactly to $2$ — the bound is saturated. For $f = \wp$ (the Weierstrass $\wp$-function), no value has $\delta(a) = 1$, but the values where $\wp$ takes critical points have $\delta(a) = 1/2$, and the four such values sum to $2$. The defect relation organizes all these examples into one principle.

    11. Bergman kernels

    For a bounded domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{C}$, the Bergman space $A^2(\Omega)$ is the Hilbert space of holomorphic functions on $\Omega$ that are square-integrable for Lebesgue area measure $dA$:

    $$A^2(\Omega) \;=\; \bigl\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}\ \text{holomorphic}\ \big|\ \int_\Omega |f|^2\,dA < \infty\bigr\},\qquad \langle f,g\rangle = \int_\Omega f\bar g\,dA.$$

    Boundedness of $\Omega$ is essential: on $\mathbb{C}$, the only $A^2$ function is $0$ (any $L^2$ holomorphic function on $\mathbb{C}$ vanishes by the mean-value property: $|f(0)|^2 \le \frac{1}{\pi R^2}\int_{|z|

    Reproducing kernel

    The deciding feature: point evaluation $f \mapsto f(w)$ is bounded on $A^2(\Omega)$. To see this, the mean-value property gives $|f(w)| \le \frac{1}{\pi r^2} \int_{D_r(w)}|f|\,dA \le C_w \|f\|_{A^2}$ for any disk $D_r(w)\subset\Omega$. Boundedness of point evaluation is the defining feature of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS); by Riesz representation there is a unique $K_w\in A^2(\Omega)$ with $f(w) = \langle f, K_w\rangle$ for all $f\in A^2(\Omega)$.

    Bergman kernel. Set $K_\Omega(z,w) = \overline{K_w(z)}$. It is holomorphic in $z$, anti-holomorphic in $w$, conjugate-symmetric ($\overline{K(z,w)} = K(w,z)$), and satisfies the reproducing identity $$f(w) \;=\; \int_\Omega K_\Omega(z,w)\, f(z)\,dA(z)\qquad \forall f\in A^2(\Omega).$$ In an orthonormal basis $\{\phi_n\}$ of $A^2(\Omega)$, $K_\Omega(z,w) = \sum_n \phi_n(z)\overline{\phi_n(w)}$.

    The disk kernel

    On the unit disk $\mathbb{D}$, the orthonormal basis is $\phi_n(z) = \sqrt{(n+1)/\pi}\, z^n$ ($n\ge 0$). The kernel sums to a closed form:

    $$K_{\mathbb{D}}(z,w) \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+1)(z\bar w)^n \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi(1-z\bar w)^2}.$$

    The diagonal $K(z,z) = \frac{1}{\pi(1-|z|^2)^2}$ blows up as $z\to\partial\mathbb{D}$ — the kernel encodes the geometry of the disk's boundary.

    Transformation under biholomorphisms

    The pull-back map $f\mapsto (f\circ\phi)\phi'$ is a unitary isomorphism $A^2(\Omega_2)\to A^2(\Omega_1)$ for any biholomorphism $\phi:\Omega_1\to\Omega_2$ — the Jacobian factor $|\phi'|^2$ is what makes the area integral transform correctly. From this:

    Transformation rule. $K_{\Omega_2}\bigl(\phi(z),\phi(w)\bigr)\,\phi'(z)\,\overline{\phi'(w)} \;=\; K_{\Omega_1}(z,w).$

    The kernel itself is not biholomorphism-invariant; the Jacobian factors carry the conformal scaling. But $\partial\bar\partial \log K(z,z)$ is invariant, because the extra factors $\log\phi'(z) + \log\overline{\phi'(w)}$ are pluriharmonic (in one complex variable, simply harmonic in $z$ and harmonic in $w$ separately) and $\partial\bar\partial$ kills them.

    The Bergman metric

    The Bergman metric on $\Omega$ is the Kähler metric (a Hermitian metric whose imaginary form is closed; in one complex variable, just any positive Hermitian metric written $g_{z\bar z}\,dz\,d\bar z$) with potential $\log K(z,z)$:

    $$g_{\Omega} \;=\; \partial_z\partial_{\bar z}\log K(z,z)\, dz\,d\bar z.$$

    By the transformation rule, this is biholomorphism-invariant. On $\mathbb{D}$:

    $$\log K_{\mathbb{D}}(z,z) \;=\; -\log\pi - 2\log(1-|z|^2),\qquad g_{\mathbb{D}} \;=\; \frac{2}{(1-|z|^2)^2}\,dz\,d\bar z,$$

    which is the standard Poincaré metric of constant Gaussian curvature $-1$ on $\mathbb{D}$. (Some texts use the normalization $K = (n+1)/(\pi(1-|z|^2)^{n+2})$ on the unit ball in $\mathbb{C}^n$, giving curvature $-2/(n+1)$; here we stick with $K = 1/(\pi(1-z\bar w)^2)$ and recover curvature $-1$ on $\mathbb{D}$.)

    This yields a slogan: the Bergman kernel encodes the conformal geometry of $\Omega$. On the disk, the kernel pins down the hyperbolic metric. On a more complicated bounded domain, the Bergman metric is a canonical invariant Kähler metric — well-defined, biholomorphism-invariant, and computable in principle from the kernel. Bergman kernels are also the prototype of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces in functional analysis and the source of techniques in harmonic analysis on domains, signal processing, and machine learning.

    12. Quasiconformal maps and the Beltrami equation

    A conformal map preserves angles infinitesimally — its derivative is a positive multiple of a rotation, so round disks go to round disks under $df$. Quasiconformal maps relax this to a controlled bounded eccentricity: round disks go to ellipses of bounded axis ratio, the bound being uniform across the domain.

    The dilatation and the Beltrami coefficient

    Write $\partial_z = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_x - i\partial_y)$ and $\partial_{\bar z} = \frac{1}{2}(\partial_x + i\partial_y)$. For a smooth function $f$, the Cauchy-Riemann equation is $\partial_{\bar z}f = 0$ — exactly the holomorphy condition. The amount by which $\partial_{\bar z}f$ fails to vanish is the Beltrami coefficient

    $$\mu_f \;=\; \frac{\partial_{\bar z} f}{\partial_z f}\qquad\text{wherever }\partial_z f\ne 0.$$

    The infinitesimal map $df$ sends a unit disk to an ellipse with eccentricity $K = \frac{1+|\mu_f|}{1 - |\mu_f|}$, the dilatation. Conformal $\Leftrightarrow \mu = 0 \Leftrightarrow K = 1$.

    Quasiconformal map. A homeomorphism $f:\Omega\to\Omega'$ between planar domains is $K$-quasiconformal if $f \in W^{1,2}_{\mathrm loc}$ (i.e., its first distributional partial derivatives are locally $L^2$) and $\|\mu_f\|_\infty \le k = (K-1)/(K+1) < 1$, equivalently the dilatation is essentially bounded by $K$.

    Because the bound is on $\|\mu_f\|_\infty$ — not on $\mu_f$ pointwise everywhere — quasiconformal maps need not be smooth. The right setting is Sobolev: $f\in W_{\mathrm loc}^{1,2}$ with the Beltrami equation holding distributionally.

    The Beltrami equation

    Solving $\partial_{\bar z}f = \mu\,\partial_z f$ for a given measurable $\mu$ is the Beltrami equation. The pioneering result is:

    Measurable Riemann mapping theorem (Morrey 1938; Ahlfors-Bers 1960). For any measurable $\mu:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ with $\|\mu\|_\infty < 1$, there is a unique homeomorphism $f^\mu:\hat{\mathbb{C}}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}$, normalized to fix $0,1,\infty$, with $\partial_{\bar z}f^\mu = \mu\,\partial_z f^\mu$ holding distributionally. $f^\mu$ is quasiconformal, depends holomorphically on $\mu$ in a precise sense, and reduces to the Riemann mapping theorem when $\mu = 0$ on a domain.

    The 'measurable' in the name is the killer feature: $\mu$ need only be a bounded measurable function, with no continuity. The proof goes through the Beurling transform $\mathcal{T}: L^p \to L^p$ (a singular integral operator that intertwines $\partial_z$ and $\partial_{\bar z}$) and a Neumann series, all on Sobolev spaces. The output $f^\mu$ is automatically Hölder continuous.

    Why this is structurally useful

    Conformal maps are rigid: a holomorphic homeomorphism is determined by three boundary points. Quasiconformal maps are the right setting for deformation:

    • Teichmüller space. The space of marked complex structures on a compact surface $\Sigma_g$ ($g\ge 1$) is parametrized by Beltrami coefficients modulo the action of the diffeomorphism group. The measurable Riemann mapping theorem produces, for each $\mu$ with $\|\mu\|_\infty < 1$, a quasiconformal deformation of the standard structure on $\Sigma_g$. The Teichmüller space $\mathcal{T}_g$ has dimension $6g - 6$ for $g\ge 2$.
    • Mostow rigidity. The proof of Mostow rigidity for closed hyperbolic 3-manifolds runs through quasiconformal maps on the boundary sphere $S^2 = \partial \mathbb{H}^3$: ergodicity forces the dilatation of the boundary extension to be constant a.e., and a quasiconformal map with constant dilatation $0$ is conformal — hence Möbius — hence comes from an ambient isometry.
    • Thurston's geometrization. Quasiconformal surgery is the technical engine in Thurston's geometrization program for 3-manifolds and the parametrization of Kleinian groups.

    Conformal-and-rigid $\to$ quasiconformal-and-flexible is the theme. The Beltrami equation is the bookkeeping device that makes 'small angle distortion' a precise and computable invariant.

    13. Several complex variables

    Holomorphic functions of one variable are governed by Cauchy's integral formula, the residue calculus, and the Riemann mapping theorem. In several complex variables — $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $n\ge 2$ — every one of these results either fails outright or holds in a much more delicate form. The reason is essentially topological: the unit ball and the bidisk in $\mathbb{C}^2$ are not biholomorphic (Poincaré 1907), so the Riemann mapping theorem fails. New phenomena take its place.

    The Hartogs phenomenon: extension is forced

    In one variable, an isolated singularity can be removable, a pole, or essential — and the third option is genuinely there ($e^{1/z}$ at $0$). In several variables, isolated singularities cannot exist:

    Hartogs's extension theorem. Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ ($n\ge 2$) be a domain and $K\subset\Omega$ a compact set such that $\Omega\setminus K$ is connected. Then every holomorphic function on $\Omega\setminus K$ extends uniquely to a holomorphic function on all of $\Omega$.

    The standard test case is the Hartogs figure: a polydisk $\mathbb{D}^2$ minus a smaller inner closed polydisk. Hartogs proves any holomorphic $f$ on the shell extends to the full polydisk. Iterating across nested compact 'holes' rules out isolated singularities for any $n\ge 2$. The proof is concrete: write $f$ as a Laurent series in one variable at fixed values of the other; the Hartogs hypothesis on connectivity forces all negative-power coefficients to vanish.

    Domains of holomorphy and pseudoconvexity

    Some domains $\Omega \subset \mathbb{C}^n$ are 'maximal' for holomorphy: there is a holomorphic function on $\Omega$ that does not extend to any larger domain. These are domains of holomorphy. The Hartogs phenomenon shows that not every domain is one — most polydisks-with-holes are not.

    The intrinsic geometric condition turns out to be pseudoconvexity: $\Omega$ admits a plurisubharmonic exhaustion $\rho:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$, $\rho\to+\infty$ at $\partial\Omega$. Plurisubharmonicity (PSH) is the right $n$-variable analog of subharmonicity — pulling $\rho$ back to a complex line gives a subharmonic function. Pseudoconvexity at the boundary level says the Levi form of $\partial\Omega$ (the complex-Hessian of a defining function, restricted to the holomorphic tangent directions $T^{1,0}\partial\Omega$) is positive semi-definite.

    Levi's problem (Oka 1942 + Bremermann/Norguet 1954). A domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{C}^n$ is a domain of holomorphy if and only if it is pseudoconvex.

    This is the SCV analog of the Riemann mapping theorem: every domain of holomorphy is pseudoconvex, and conversely. The 'shape' of the boundary determines whether it can host a maximally-extended holomorphic function. The disk in one variable is automatically a domain of holomorphy because every $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ is.

    The $\bar\partial$-problem and the $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem

    The constraint $\bar\partial f = 0$ defines holomorphy. To probe the structure, ask the inhomogeneous question: given a $\bar\partial$-closed $(0,1)$-form $\alpha = \sum \alpha_j\, d\bar z_j$ on $\Omega$, when can we solve $\bar\partial u = \alpha$ for some scalar $u$?

    • On a domain of holomorphy (pseudoconvex), the answer is always — and one can choose $u$ with explicit $L^2$ bounds via the Hörmander estimate $\int_\Omega |u|^2 e^{-\phi}\,dV \le C \int_\Omega |\alpha|^2 e^{-\phi}\,dV$ for any plurisubharmonic weight $\phi$.
    • The associated minimization problem (find the $u$ of minimum norm orthogonal to holomorphic functions) is the $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem: solve $\Box u = (\bar\partial \bar\partial^* + \bar\partial^*\bar\partial)u = \alpha$ with prescribed boundary conditions. Its analytic regularity theory (Kohn-Spencer) is the engine driving SCV at the technical level.

    The $\bar\partial$-Neumann theory connects SCV to elliptic PDE theory, harmonic analysis, and the index theorem (the $\bar\partial$-complex on a complex manifold computes Dolbeault cohomology, and its index is a topological invariant by Atiyah-Singer).

    What's lost in passing to $n\ge 2$

    The slogan for SCV: topology constrains analysis. The disk-and-bidisk inequivalence in $\mathbb{C}^2$, Hartogs's extension, the equivalence of holomorphy-domains with pseudoconvexity — all reflect the much richer interplay between holomorphy and the underlying $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ geometry. SCV connects to algebraic geometry (Stein manifolds, GAGA), CR geometry (boundaries of domains), and complex differential geometry (Kähler manifolds, the $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma).

    Connections

    The graduate complex-analysis canon doesn't end here — it spreads outward. Quasiconformal maps and the Beltrami equation generalize conformal maps to allow controlled angle distortion, opening the door to Teichmüller theory and Thurston's hyperbolic geometrization. Several complex variables (SCV) replaces the open mapping and Riemann mapping theorems with much subtler statements (the disk and the bidisk are not biholomorphic) and Hartogs's extension phenomenon. Bergman kernels and reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces bridge complex analysis with operator theory and machine learning. Nevanlinna theory measures the value distribution of meromorphic functions much more finely than Picard, with characteristic functions $T(r,f)$ and the second main theorem.

    • Complex analysis — Liouville's theorem — the bounded-entire-is-constant baseline that little Picard sharpens.
    • Complex analysis — classification of singularities — the trichotomy (removable, pole, essential) whose essential case is upgraded by great Picard.
    • Complex analysis — maximum modulus principle — the bounded-domain ancestor of Phragmén-Lindelöf.
    • Harmonic functions — the three-spheres / three-circles convexity statements live equally for harmonic functions, and outer functions in $H^p$ are Poisson integrals of their boundary log-modulus.
    • Functional analysis — the Hadamard three-lines theorem is the engine inside the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem and complex interpolation between Banach spaces.
    • Harmonic analysis & Fourier theory — Hardy spaces and the Hardy-Littlewood maximal inequality sit at the intersection of complex analysis and the Calderón-Zygmund theory.
    • Upper half-plane & hyperbolic geometry — Picard's theorems lift through the modular function $\lambda:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}$, where the upper half-plane plays the role of universal cover.
