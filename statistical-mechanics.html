Statistical mechanics

From a Hamiltonian to a probability measure on phase space: microstates, the Boltzmann distribution, and the partition function. Entropy as the variational principle that picks out canonical equilibrium; the Ising model and the renormalization group as the route from microscopic spins to universal critical exponents; large deviations as the modern probabilistic skeleton of equilibrium thermodynamics.

1. Microstates and ensembles

A classical mechanical system with $N$ particles in three dimensions has phase space $\Gamma = \mathbb{R}^{6N}$ — three position and three momentum coordinates per particle. A microstate is a single point $(q, p)\in\Gamma$. The Hamiltonian $H(q, p)$ generates the deterministic flow

$$\dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}.$$

For one mole of gas $N \approx 6\times 10^{23}$, so $\Gamma$ has $\sim 10^{24}$ dimensions. Direct integration of the trajectory is hopeless — and unnecessary, because the macroscopic observables we measure (pressure, temperature, density) are averages over enormous numbers of microstates. Statistical mechanics replaces the deterministic trajectory by a probability measure on $\Gamma$, called an ensemble.

Liouville's theorem. The Hamiltonian flow on $\Gamma$ preserves the symplectic volume $d\Gamma = \prod_i dq_i\,dp_i$. Equivalently, the velocity field $V = (\partial H/\partial p, -\partial H/\partial q)$ is divergence-free: $$\nabla\cdot V \;=\; \sum_i\!\left(\frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i\partial q_i}\right) \;=\; 0.$$ A probability density $\rho(q, p, t)$ carried by the flow satisfies the Liouville equation $\partial_t\rho + \{\rho, H\} = 0$; in particular, any density $\rho = \rho(H)$ that depends only on conserved quantities is stationary.

The simplest stationary density is the one supported on a single energy shell — the microcanonical ensemble. Fix energy $E$ (and possibly other conserved quantities like total momentum) and put the uniform Liouville measure on a thin shell $\{E \le H(q,p) \le E + \Delta E\}$:

$$\rho_{\mathrm mc}(q, p) \;=\; \frac{\mathbf{1}\{E \le H \le E + \Delta E\}}{\Omega(E)\,\Delta E}, \qquad \Omega(E) = \int_\Gamma \delta(H(q, p) - E)\, d\Gamma.$$

Boltzmann's tombstone formula defines entropy from this microstate count:

$$S(E) \;=\; k_B \log\Omega(E),$$

and inverse temperature emerges as the conjugate variable

$$\frac{1}{T} \;=\; \frac{\partial S}{\partial E}.$$

This is a derivation, not a postulate: temperature is the rate at which microstate count grows with energy, and two systems brought into thermal contact equilibrate at common $\partial S/\partial E$ because the joint $\Omega_1(E_1)\Omega_2(E - E_1)$ is dominated by the saddle point that equates the two slopes.

Ergodic hypothesis. The microcanonical ensemble is the right ensemble at fixed $E$ if the dynamics is ergodic: time averages along a typical trajectory converge to ensemble averages over the energy shell, $$\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f(\Phi_t(q_0, p_0))\,dt \;=\; \int_{\{H=E\}} f\, d\rho_{\mathrm mc} \quad \text{for $\rho_{\mathrm mc}$-a.e. $(q_0, p_0)$}.$$ Ergodicity is delicate (KAM theory shows that integrable Hamiltonians are not ergodic — invariant tori block phase-space mixing), but the laboratory observation that macroscopic systems do reach thermal equilibrium suggests it is generically a good approximation, and the maximum-entropy derivation in §3 sidesteps this issue entirely.

2. Boltzmann distribution and the canonical ensemble

The microcanonical ensemble fixes the total energy of an isolated system. In the laboratory we more often hold the temperature fixed by coupling the system to a much larger heat bath. The corresponding ensemble is the canonical ensemble, and its derivation is one of the cleanest arguments in physics.

Take a system $S$ with Hamiltonian $H_S$ in contact with a bath $B$ with Hamiltonian $H_B$, the combined system isolated and at total energy $E_{\mathrm tot}$. Assume the coupling is weak so the joint Hamiltonian decomposes as $H_S + H_B$. The probability that $S$ is in microstate $i$ with energy $E_i$ is proportional to the number of bath microstates compatible with that condition:

$$P_i \;\propto\; \Omega_B(E_{\mathrm tot} - E_i) \;=\; \exp\!\bigl(S_B(E_{\mathrm tot} - E_i)/k_B\bigr).$$

For a large bath, $E_i \ll E_{\mathrm tot}$ and we can Taylor-expand:

$$S_B(E_{\mathrm tot} - E_i) \;\approx\; S_B(E_{\mathrm tot}) - E_i\,\frac{\partial S_B}{\partial E}\bigg|_{E_{\mathrm tot}} \;=\; S_B(E_{\mathrm tot}) - E_i/T.$$

Higher-order corrections are suppressed by powers of $1/N_{\mathrm bath}$, so they vanish exactly in the thermodynamic limit. Substituting and absorbing the bath-only term into the normalization:

Boltzmann distribution. A system in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T = 1/(k_B\beta)$ is described by the canonical density operator $$\rho \;=\; \frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad Z(\beta) = \tr\,e^{-\beta H},$$ where the partition function $Z$ is the normalization. For a discrete spectrum, $p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$.

The partition function $Z(\beta)$ is the central object — its logarithm is a generating function for thermodynamic averages. Direct differentiation gives:

quantityformula
mean energy$\langle E\rangle = -\partial_\beta\log Z$
energy variance$\mathrm{Var}(E) = \partial_\beta^2\log Z$
Helmholtz free energy$F = -k_B T\log Z$
entropy$S = -\partial F/\partial T$
heat capacity$C_V = k_B\beta^2\,\mathrm{Var}(E)$

The last identity is the first fluctuation-response relation we meet: the heat capacity, an apparently dynamical quantity (how much energy you need to add to raise temperature by a degree), equals an equilibrium fluctuation (the variance of energy). All linear-response coefficients have this structure (§7).

Worked example: two-level system. A spin in a magnetic field has energies $E_\pm = \pm\Delta/2$. Then $Z = e^{\beta\Delta/2} + e^{-\beta\Delta/2} = 2\cosh(\beta\Delta/2)$, $\langle E\rangle = -\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\tanh(\beta\Delta/2)$, $C_V = k_B(\beta\Delta/2)^2\mathrm{sech}^2(\beta\Delta/2)$ — the Schottky anomaly: a peak in $C_V$ at $k_BT \approx \Delta/2.4$, with $C_V\to 0$ in both the low-$T$ (gap freezes out fluctuations) and high-$T$ (Boltzmann factors saturate at $1/2$) limits.

3. Entropy and the Gibbs distribution

The Boltzmann distribution can also be derived without ever mentioning a heat bath. It is the unique distribution that maximizes entropy at fixed mean energy — a variational characterization that makes statistical mechanics a special case of information theory.

Gibbs entropy. For a probability distribution $\{p_i\}$ on a discrete set of microstates, $$S(\{p_i\}) \;=\; -k_B \sum_i p_i \log p_i.$$ For a continuous density $\rho(q,p)$ on phase space (with $h$ a reference scale, classically the Planck constant), $S = -k_B\int \rho\log(\rho h^{3N}/N!)\, d\Gamma$ — the same formula relative to a measure.

The entropy is concave in $\{p_i\}$, vanishes for any deterministic $p_i = \delta_{i,k}$, and achieves its global maximum $k_B\log N$ at the uniform distribution. To find the maximum subject to constraints we use Lagrange multipliers.

Theorem (Gibbs/Jaynes). Among all distributions $\{p_i\}$ on $\{E_i\}$ satisfying $\sum p_i = 1$ and $\sum p_i E_i = U$, the unique maximizer of Gibbs entropy is the Boltzmann distribution $$p_i^* \;=\; \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z(\beta)},$$ where $\beta$ is the Lagrange multiplier conjugate to $U$, determined implicitly by $-\partial_\beta\log Z = U$.

Proof sketch. The Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = -k_B\sum p_i\log p_i - k_B\alpha(\sum p_i - 1) - k_B\beta(\sum p_i E_i - U)$ has stationary points where $-\log p_i - 1 - \alpha - \beta E_i = 0$, so $p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}$; normalization fixes the proportionality constant. Concavity of $-x\log x$ guarantees this is the unique global maximum. $\square$

This derivation is operationally identical to the heat-bath argument of §2 but conceptually different: it does not require physical contact with anything. The Boltzmann distribution is the least biased distribution consistent with knowledge of the mean energy alone — Jaynes's maximum-entropy principle. The same principle, applied to other constraints, generates the grand canonical ensemble (§4), the maximum-entropy distributions of mathematical statistics, and prior selection in Bayesian inference.

Boltzmann's $S = k_B\log\Omega$ as a special case. The microcanonical ensemble is uniform on $\Omega$ accessible states, $p_i = 1/\Omega$. Then $S = -k_B\sum (1/\Omega)\log(1/\Omega) = k_B\log\Omega$ — the tombstone formula recovered. Boltzmann and Gibbs entropies agree whenever the microcanonical distribution is approximately uniform on the energy shell, which is exactly the ergodic regime.

The second law, in the form most relevant to statistical mechanics, becomes a precise statement: when two systems initially in canonical equilibrium at different $\beta$ are coupled and allowed to relax to a new equilibrium, the total Gibbs entropy is non-decreasing. The proof uses the Klein inequality for relative entropy: $D(\rho\|\sigma) = \tr\,\rho(\log\rho - \log\sigma)\ge 0$ with equality iff $\rho = \sigma$. Setting $\sigma$ equal to the equilibrium state of the joint system gives entropy non-decrease. This is the same Kullback-Leibler divergence that drives information theory and statistical inference — yet another bridge.

4. Grand canonical ensemble and chemical potential

The canonical ensemble fixes the particle number $N$. To describe systems that exchange particles with a reservoir — gas in contact with a vapor, electrons in a wire connected to a battery — we relax that constraint. The reservoir fixes both temperature and a new intensive variable, the chemical potential $\mu$, which acts as a Lagrange multiplier conjugate to $N$.

Repeating the heat-bath argument (or the maximum-entropy derivation) at fixed $\langle E\rangle$ and fixed $\langle N\rangle$ gives the grand canonical density operator

$$\rho \;=\; \frac{e^{-\beta(H - \mu N)}}{\Xi(\beta, \mu)}, \qquad \Xi(\beta, \mu) = \tr\,e^{-\beta(H - \mu N)}.$$

The grand partition function $\Xi$ generates a new free energy, the grand potential

$$\Omega(\beta, \mu) \;=\; -k_B T\log\Xi \;=\; F - \mu N \;=\; -PV.$$

The last equality $\Omega = -PV$ is a thermodynamic identity for a homogeneous system, derived by Euler's relation: in the thermodynamic limit, $\Omega$ is extensive and depends only on the intensive variables $T, \mu$ (and $V$ as the size), so $\Omega = -PV$ where $P$ is the pressure.

For a single-species ideal gas of non-interacting particles in single-particle eigenstates of energy $\epsilon_k$, the grand partition function factorizes over modes:

$$\Xi \;=\; \prod_k \Xi_k, \qquad \Xi_k = \sum_{n=0}^{n_{\max}} e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)n}.$$

The sum depends on quantum statistics:

statistics$n_{\max}$$\Xi_k$$\bar n_k$
Fermi-Dirac (fermions)$1$$1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}$$\dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}+1}$
Bose-Einstein (bosons)$\infty$$\dfrac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}}$$\dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}-1}$
Maxwell-Boltzmann (classical)—$e^{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}}$$e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k-\mu)}$

For bosons, $\mu < \min_k \epsilon_k$ is required for convergence; pushing $\mu\to\min_k\epsilon_k$ from below at low $T$ produces Bose-Einstein condensation — a macroscopic occupancy of the ground state. For fermions, the low-$T$ limit forces $\bar n_k \to \mathbf{1}\{\epsilon_k < \mu\}$ — the Fermi sea, with $\mu_{T=0} = \epsilon_F$ (the Fermi energy). The classical limit recovers Maxwell-Boltzmann when $e^{\beta\mu}\ll 1$ (low density / high temperature).

Ensemble equivalence. Microcanonical, canonical, and grand canonical descriptions differ by which extensive variables they hold strictly fixed. In the thermodynamic limit, intensive thermodynamic quantities (energy density, pressure, magnetization per spin) computed from any of the three coincide — for short-range interactions. Long-range interactions (3D self-gravitating gas, mean-field models) violate equivalence, so canonical and microcanonical specific heats can have opposite signs. The reason: ensemble equivalence requires concavity of $S(E)$, which can fail when the energy is non-extensive.

5. The Ising model and phase transitions

So far the canonical formalism has been general but abstract. The Ising model is the simplest concrete arena where the canonical machinery produces a sharp phase transition — a discontinuity (or non-analyticity) in thermodynamic quantities as a function of intensive variables.

Place a spin $\sigma_i\in\{+1, -1\}$ at each site $i$ of a $d$-dimensional lattice. The Hamiltonian is

$$H \;=\; -J\sum_{\langle i, j\rangle}\sigma_i\sigma_j \;-\; h\sum_i \sigma_i,$$

where $\langle i, j\rangle$ runs over nearest-neighbor pairs, $J > 0$ is the ferromagnetic coupling (parallel spins lower energy), and $h$ is an external field. The partition function is a sum over all $2^N$ spin configurations:

$$Z(\beta, h) \;=\; \sum_{\{\sigma\}} e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}.$$

The order parameter is the magnetization per spin $m = \langle\sigma_i\rangle$. At high temperature ($\beta J\ll 1$), thermal fluctuations destroy alignment and $m\to 0$; at low temperature ($\beta J\gg 1$), the energetic preference for aligned spins wins and $|m|\to 1$. Somewhere in between is a critical point $T_c$ separating the two regimes.

Mean-field theory. Replace each neighbor of $\sigma_i$ by its average $\langle\sigma\rangle = m$. The effective single-spin Hamiltonian is $H_i = -(qJm + h)\sigma_i$ with $q$ the coordination number; thermal averaging gives the self-consistency $$m \;=\; \tanh\bigl(\beta(qJm + h)\bigr).$$ At $h = 0$, this has only the trivial solution $m = 0$ for $\beta qJ \le 1$, but bifurcates to $m = \pm m_0(\beta)\ne 0$ for $\beta qJ > 1$. The mean-field critical temperature is $k_BT_c^{\mathrm MF} = qJ$, with critical exponent $m\sim(T_c-T)^{1/2}$ near the transition.

Mean-field theory is exact in infinite dimensions and increasingly accurate as $d$ grows, but in low dimensions it overestimates $T_c$ and gets the critical exponents wrong because it ignores fluctuations. The classical results:

dimension$T_c/J$magnetization exponent $\beta$method
$d = 1$$0$ (no transition)—Peierls argument
$d = 2$ (square)$2/\log(1+\sqrt 2)\approx 2.27$$1/8$Onsager 1944
$d = 3$$\approx 4.51$$\approx 0.326$Monte Carlo, $\epsilon$-expansion
$d \ge 4$—$1/2$ (mean-field)Wilson-Fisher trivial above $4D$

The 1D Ising model has no phase transition at any positive $T$: a single domain wall costs constant energy $2J$ but contributes $k_B\log L$ entropy from its position, so $\Delta F = 2J - k_BT\log L\to -\infty$ for any $T > 0$ as $L\to\infty$. Domain walls always proliferate (Peierls). In $d\ge 2$ the wall energy scales with the wall length, beating the entropy at low temperature — and a phase transition is possible.

Onsager's 1944 exact solution of the 2D Ising model on the square lattice was a tour de force: the partition function reduces to a free-fermion problem, and Kramers-Wannier duality $\sinh(2\beta J)\sinh(2\beta J^*) = 1$ pins the critical temperature self-dually. The exact magnetization $m\sim(T_c-T)^{1/8}$ is one of the most-quoted critical exponents in physics, and 2D Ising is the prototype of the broader class of two-dimensional conformal field theories classified by Belavin-Polyakov-Zamolodchikov.

6. The renormalization group

How can two systems with completely different microscopic Hamiltonians — say, the 3D Ising model and the liquid-vapor transition in water at the critical density — share identical critical exponents? This empirical fact, called universality, is the central puzzle of critical phenomena. The answer is the renormalization group (RG), Kadanoff and Wilson's Nobel-winning reformulation of statistical mechanics from a flow on the space of theories.

The basic move is coarse-graining followed by rescaling. Given a lattice theory at scale $a$ with couplings $K = (K_1, K_2, \dots)$:

  1. Coarse-grain. Group spins into blocks of side $b$, replacing each block with a representative (e.g. majority spin, sum, average — the choice doesn't matter for the universal output). The effective theory describes the block variables.
  2. Rescale. Measure all distances in units of the new lattice spacing $a' = ba$, restoring the original cutoff. Couplings update accordingly: $K\mapsto K' = R_b(K)$.

Iterating gives a discrete dynamical system on coupling space. Fixed points $K^* = R_b(K^*)$ are scale-invariant theories — exactly the critical theories. Linearizing near $K^*$,

$$K' - K^* \;=\; M(K - K^*) + O((K-K^*)^2), \qquad M_{ij} = \frac{\partial K'_i}{\partial K_j}\bigg|_{K^*},$$

and the eigenvalues $\lambda_a = b^{y_a}$ classify directions of flow:

$y_a$directionflow behaviourphysical meaning
$y_a > 0$relevantgrows under iterationtunes you off criticality (e.g. $T - T_c$, magnetic field)
$y_a < 0$irrelevantflows into $K^*$microscopic detail that washes out at long distances
$y_a = 0$marginallogarithmic flowrequires nonlinear analysis (e.g. Kosterlitz-Thouless)

Universality is automatic: any two theories that flow to the same fixed point have identical long-distance behaviour, because the irrelevant differences die out under iteration. The set of all theories flowing to a given fixed point is its universality class; its critical exponents are determined by the relevant eigenvalues alone.

Critical exponents from eigenvalues. For Ising-type theories with two relevant directions (temperature and field), one shows $$\nu = \frac{1}{y_t}, \qquad \beta = \frac{d - y_h}{y_t}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{2 y_h - d}{y_t}, \qquad \eta = d + 2 - 2 y_h$$ in $d$ dimensions. The exponents satisfy two scaling relations $\alpha + 2\beta + \gamma = 2$ (Rushbrooke) and $\gamma = \nu(2-\eta)$ (Fisher), reducing six exponents to two independent ones — exactly $y_t$ and $y_h$. This explains why measurements of any two exponents determine the rest.

Wilson-Fisher fixed point. For the $\phi^4$ scalar field theory, $\phi^4$ is marginal at $d = 4$ (its eigenvalue is exactly zero in mean-field). Wilson and Fisher (1972) noticed that perturbing about $d = 4$ — formally treating $\epsilon = 4 - d$ as a small parameter — gives a new fixed point at coupling $g^* = O(\epsilon)$, away from the Gaussian fixed point. Critical exponents have an $\epsilon$-expansion:

$$\nu \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{12}\epsilon + O(\epsilon^2), \qquad \eta \;=\; \tfrac{1}{54}\epsilon^2 + O(\epsilon^3).$$

Setting $\epsilon = 1$ (so $d = 3$) gives $\nu \approx 0.5833$, surprisingly close to the Monte Carlo value $\nu \approx 0.6299$ for the 3D Ising universality class. Higher orders bring the perturbative answer into agreement with Monte Carlo to several decimal places, even though the series is asymptotic and divergent. This is the technical bridge from statistical mechanics to quantum field theory: the same machinery, applied to coupling constants of QFT, becomes the renormalization of QED or QCD.

7. Large deviations and equilibrium fluctuations

The thermodynamic potentials of §2-§4 — entropy $S(E)$, free energy $F(\beta)$, grand potential $\Omega(\beta,\mu)$ — are linked by Legendre transforms. From a probabilistic standpoint, this Legendre structure is exactly the structure of large-deviation rate functions. Modern mathematical statistical mechanics begins from this observation.

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots$ be i.i.d. real-valued random variables with cumulant generating function $\Lambda(\theta) = \log\mathbb{E}[e^{\theta X_1}]$, finite on a neighborhood of $0$. The Cramér-Chernoff theorem says: the empirical mean $\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ satisfies a large-deviation principle,

$$\frac{1}{n}\log P(\bar X_n \approx x) \;\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\; -I(x),$$

where the rate function $I$ is the Legendre transform of $\Lambda$:

$$I(x) \;=\; \Lambda^*(x) \;=\; \sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\theta x - \Lambda(\theta)\bigr).$$

The rate is convex, vanishes at $\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_1]$, and recovers Gaussian behaviour $I(x)\approx (x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ near the mean (the central limit theorem). Far from the mean it is exponentially decaying with a non-Gaussian shape — large deviations are dominated by the most likely improbable path rather than by the typical fluctuation.

Gärtner-Ellis theorem. The Cramér theorem extends from i.i.d. sums to general dependent sequences whenever the limit $$\Lambda(\theta) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{n\theta\,Y_n}\bigr]$$ exists and is differentiable. Then $Y_n$ satisfies a large-deviation principle with rate $I = \Lambda^*$. This is the workhorse of equilibrium statistical mechanics: in the canonical ensemble, the empirical mean energy $\bar E_n$ has CGF $\Lambda(\theta) = -\beta f(\beta - \theta) + \beta f(\beta)$ where $f$ is the free energy density, and the rate function is the entropy density as a function of energy. Free energy and entropy are Legendre dual rate functions.

The bridge to thermodynamics is now sharp:

statistical mechanicslarge deviations
free energy $\beta f(\beta)$cumulant generating function $\Lambda(\theta)$
entropy density $s(\epsilon)$rate function $I(x)$
Legendre relation $\beta f = \beta\epsilon - s$Legendre relation $\Lambda = (\Lambda^*)^* = I^*$
$\partial s/\partial\epsilon = \beta$ defines temperature$I'(x) = \theta^*(x)$ defines the conjugate variable

Phase transitions are non-analyticities of $\beta f(\beta)$ — equivalently, breakdowns of Legendre duality between $\Lambda$ and $I$. When $s(\epsilon)$ has a flat segment (latent heat), the dual $\Lambda$ has a corner; ensemble inequivalence appears in exactly this regime.

Fluctuation-dissipation. A second triumph of the formalism: equilibrium fluctuations encode the response of the system to small external perturbations. Consider a system with Hamiltonian $H$ perturbed to $H - hB$ for small $h$. To linear order the change in the equilibrium average of $A$ is

$$\delta\langle A\rangle \;=\; h\,\beta\bigl(\langle AB\rangle_0 - \langle A\rangle_0\langle B\rangle_0\bigr) \;+\; O(h^2),$$

where averages with subscript $0$ are computed in the unperturbed Gibbs measure. The response coefficient $\chi_{AB} = \beta\,\mathrm{Cov}(A, B)$ is exactly an equilibrium fluctuation. Specializing: heat capacity = energy-energy variance, magnetic susceptibility = magnetization-magnetization variance, conductivity = current-current correlation (Kubo). The slogan: noise equals response.

8. Connections

Statistical mechanics is unusual among graduate subjects: it is at once a settled body of techniques (canonical ensemble, partition functions, mean-field), a still-active research area (ensemble inequivalence in long-range systems, glasses, non-equilibrium steady states, fluctuation theorems), and a continuous source of mathematical structures absorbed by adjacent fields. The renormalization group propagated from criticality into quantum field theory; large deviations migrated from rare-event analysis into Donsker-Varadhan theory of Markov chains and KPZ universality; the Ising-CFT bridge motivates entire programs in conformal bootstrap and integrable systems.

  • Hamiltonians & classical mechanics — Liouville's theorem — the volume-preservation property that makes the microcanonical ensemble dynamically consistent.
  • Information theory — Shannon entropy — the same $-\sum p_i\log p_i$ as Gibbs entropy; statistical mechanics is the maximum-entropy formalism applied to fixed mean energy.
  • Large deviations — Cramér's theorem — the rigorous probabilistic backbone of equilibrium thermodynamic potentials as Legendre transforms of cumulant generating functions.
  • Gauge theory — lattice gauge theory — Wilson's lattice formulation borrows the partition-function language wholesale; confinement is a phase transition of the lattice theory.
  • Stochastic calculus — Langevin and Fokker-Planck — non-equilibrium statistical mechanics adds white noise to Hamiltonian flow; equilibrium recovers Boltzmann from the fluctuation-dissipation balance.
  • Dynamical systems — ergodic theory — the rigorous ergodic hypothesis that justifies the microcanonical assumption is a question of mixing on the energy shell.
