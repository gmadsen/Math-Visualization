Dirichlet's unit theorem

A number field $K$ has only $\pm 1$ for rational units, but the integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ can carry infinitely many units of infinite order. Dirichlet (1846) pinned down the entire structure: $\mathcal{O}_K^\times \cong \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^{r_1+r_2-1}$. The covolume of the free part is the regulator, a fundamental arithmetic invariant that lives next to the class number inside the residue of $\zeta_K$.

1. Logarithmic embedding

Let $K$ be a number field of degree $n=[K:\mathbb{Q}]$. The embeddings $K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}$ split into $r_1$ real ones $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_{r_1}$ and $r_2$ pairs of complex-conjugate ones; pick one representative $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_{r_2}$ from each pair. So $r_1+2r_2=n$, and the pair $(r_1,r_2)$ is the signature of $K$.

Define the logarithmic embedding $$\ell\colon \mathcal{O}_K^\times \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{R}^{r_1+r_2}, \qquad \ell(u) \;=\; \big(\log|\sigma_1(u)|,\,\ldots,\,\log|\sigma_{r_1}(u)|,\;2\log|\tau_1(u)|,\,\ldots,\,2\log|\tau_{r_2}(u)|\big).$$ The factors of $2$ on complex coordinates are not cosmetic: each complex place contributes $|\tau_j(u)|^2$ to the absolute norm $|N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(u)|$, so weighted logs add up to $\log|N(u)|$.

Two key properties.
  • Image lies in a hyperplane. A unit $u$ has $|N(u)|=1$, so $\sum_i \log|\sigma_i(u)| + \sum_j 2\log|\tau_j(u)| = 0$. Hence $\ell(u)$ lies in the trace-zero hyperplane $H = \{x\in\mathbb{R}^{r_1+r_2}:\sum x_i=0\}$, of dimension $r_1+r_2-1$.
  • Kernel is finite. If $\ell(u)=0$ then $|\sigma(u)|=1$ for every archimedean $\sigma$. By a classical theorem of Kronecker, an algebraic integer all of whose conjugates lie on the unit circle is a root of unity. So $\ker\ell = \mu_K$, the (finite cyclic) group of roots of unity in $K$.

2. Statement and proof sketch

Dirichlet's unit theorem (1846). Let $K$ be a number field of signature $(r_1,r_2)$. Then $$\mathcal{O}_K^\times \;\cong\; \mu_K \;\times\; \mathbb{Z}^{r_1+r_2-1}.$$ The free rank $r:=r_1+r_2-1$ is one less than the number of archimedean places. A choice of $\mathbb{Z}$-basis $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_r$ of the free part is a system of fundamental units.

$K$signature $(r_1,r_2)$unit rank$\mathcal{O}_K^\times$
$\mathbb{Q}$$(1,0)$$0$$\{\pm 1\}$
$\mathbb{Q}(i)$$(0,1)$$0$$\{\pm 1, \pm i\}$
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$, $d>0$$(0,1)$$0$$\mu_K$ (size $2,4,$ or $6$)
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$, $d>0$$(2,0)$$1$$\{\pm 1\}\times\langle\varepsilon\rangle$
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$$(1,1)$$1$$\{\pm 1\}\times\langle\sqrt[3]{2}-1\rangle$
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$$(0,\varphi(n)/2)$$\varphi(n)/2-1$cyclotomic units

The proof in two moves

Step 1: discreteness. The image $\ell(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)\subset H$ is discrete. Concretely: in any bounded box of $H$ there are only finitely many lattice points because each component $\log|\sigma(u)|$ being bounded forces $|\sigma(u)|$ bounded for all $\sigma$, hence finitely many algebraic integers (their minimal polynomials have bounded coefficients via symmetric functions). Discreteness gives that $\ell(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)$ is a sublattice of $H$, of rank at most $r=r_1+r_2-1$.

Step 2: full rank, via Minkowski. The hard direction is to produce $r$ multiplicatively independent units. The idea: in $K_\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{r_1}\times\mathbb{C}^{r_2}$, take a centrally-symmetric box $B_c$ of volume just exceeding $2^{r_1}\pi^{r_2}\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}$ (twice the covolume of $\mathcal{O}_K$) and apply Minkowski's convex-body theorem to get a nonzero $\alpha\in\mathcal{O}_K\cap B_c$ — equivalently, a nonzero $\alpha$ of bounded norm. By rescaling $B_c$ along different archimedean coordinates while keeping the volume fixed, you produce different $\alpha$'s with prescribed log-coordinates; quotienting by ideal class equivalence (only finitely many ideal classes!) eventually forces two of these $\alpha$'s to differ by a unit, and you read off log-vectors that span an $r$-dimensional subspace of $H$.

Why one less than the number of places? The product formula $|N(u)|=1$ is one nontrivial linear constraint on the $r_1+r_2$ log-coordinates, and that constraint is exhausted by the trace-zero hyperplane. There are no further constraints: the geometric construction shows you can freely shift mass between any two places. The minus-one is intrinsic.

3. The regulator $R_K$

Once you have fundamental units $\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_r$ ($r=r_1+r_2-1$), the unit lattice $\Lambda_K = \ell(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)\subset H$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $r$ inside an $r$-dimensional hyperplane. It has a covolume — and that covolume, computed in a specific normalization, is the regulator.

Form the $r\times(r+1)$ matrix $$M \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} \log|\sigma_1(\varepsilon_1)| & \log|\sigma_2(\varepsilon_1)| & \cdots & 2\log|\tau_{r_2}(\varepsilon_1)| \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ \log|\sigma_1(\varepsilon_r)| & \log|\sigma_2(\varepsilon_r)| & \cdots & 2\log|\tau_{r_2}(\varepsilon_r)| \end{pmatrix}.$$ Drop any one column (the missing column is determined by the others via $\sum=0$); the absolute determinant of the resulting $r\times r$ minor is independent of the dropped column. That number is the regulator $R_K$.

Independence of choices.
  • Choice of fundamental units. Any two $\mathbb{Z}$-bases of $\mathcal{O}_K^\times/\mu_K$ differ by a $\mathrm{GL}_r(\mathbb{Z})$ matrix, with determinant $\pm 1$. So $|\det|$ — i.e. $R_K$ — is the same for every system of fundamental units.
  • Choice of dropped column. Adding all columns gives the zero column (trace-zero), so the determinants of the $r+1$ possible $r\times r$ minors all coincide up to sign.

Worked examples

$K=\mathbb{Q}$. Rank $0$; the empty determinant is $1$ by convention, $R_\mathbb{Q}=1$.

$K=\mathbb{Q}(i)$. Rank $0$, $R_K=1$. (The four units $\pm 1, \pm i$ are all torsion.)

$K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. Rank $1$, fundamental unit $\varepsilon=1+\sqrt{2}\approx 2.414$. The two real embeddings send $\varepsilon$ to $1+\sqrt{2}$ and $1-\sqrt{2}\approx -0.414$. Log-coords: $(\log(1+\sqrt 2),\,-\log(1+\sqrt 2))$. Drop the second column: $R_K=\log(1+\sqrt 2)\approx 0.8814$.

$K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$. Rank $1$, fundamental unit $\varepsilon=\sqrt[3]{2}-1\approx 0.2599$ (negative log!). The other places give a single complex pair with $|\tau(\varepsilon)|=|\sqrt[3]{2}\zeta_3-1|\approx 1.962$. Two log-coordinates: $(\log 0.2599,\,2\log 1.962)\approx(-1.347,\,1.347)$. Drop one: $R_K\approx 1.347$.

4. The analytic class number formula

The Dedekind zeta function of $K$ is $$\zeta_K(s) \;=\; \sum_{\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_K} \frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{a})^s} \;=\; \prod_{\mathfrak{p}}\Big(1-\tfrac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})^s}\Big)^{-1},\qquad \mathrm{Re}(s)>1,$$ summed over nonzero ideals (or as a product over prime ideals). Like $\zeta(s)$, it extends meromorphically to $\mathbb{C}$ with a single simple pole at $s=1$. The residue is the headline:

Analytic class number formula. $$\boxed{\;\mathrm{Res}_{s=1}\zeta_K(s) \;=\; \frac{2^{r_1}\,(2\pi)^{r_2}\,h_K\,R_K}{w_K\,\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}}\;}$$ with $h_K$ the class number, $R_K$ the regulator, $w_K=|\mu_K|$ the number of roots of unity, $\Delta_K$ the discriminant, and $(r_1,r_2)$ the signature.

Two extreme sanity checks:

  • $K=\mathbb{Q}$. Signature $(1,0)$, $h=R=1$, $w=2$, $\Delta=1$. RHS $=\frac{2\cdot 1\cdot 1\cdot 1}{2\cdot 1}=1$. And $\zeta(s)$ has residue $1$ at $s=1$. ✓
  • $K=\mathbb{Q}(i)$. Signature $(0,1)$, $h=R=1$, $w=4$, $\Delta=-4$. RHS $=\frac{2\pi\cdot 1\cdot 1}{4\cdot 2}=\pi/4$. This is exactly Dirichlet's classical $\pi/4 = 1-1/3+1/5-\cdots$, the value of $L(1,\chi)$ for the non-trivial character mod $4$. ✓

Reading the formula

Each factor on the right is a geometric quantity. Under the identification of $\zeta_K$ with a counting function for ideals,

  • $\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}$ is the additive lattice covolume — the volume of $K_\mathbb{R}/\mathcal{O}_K$;
  • $R_K$ is the multiplicative lattice covolume — the volume of $H/\Lambda_K$;
  • $h_K$ counts the missing principal ideals (size of the class group);
  • $w_K$ counts the kernel of $\ell$ (torsion in the unit group);
  • $2^{r_1}(2\pi)^{r_2}$ is the natural archimedean local factor.

The class number formula is the prototype of a much bigger story: every arithmetic $L$-function attached to an "arithmetic motive" is conjectured to have its leading term at the central point governed by a discriminant times a regulator times a Tate–Shafarevich-style finite group. BSD is this conjecture for elliptic curves; Bloch–Kato is the general framework; the Iwasawa main conjecture proves a $p$-adic version up the cyclotomic tower.

5. Real quadratic units and Pell's equation

The simplest non-trivial Dirichlet rank is the real quadratic case $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$, $d>0$ squarefree. Signature $(2,0)$, unit rank $1$, so $\mathcal{O}_K^\times = \{\pm 1\}\times\langle\varepsilon\rangle$ for a unique fundamental unit $\varepsilon>1$.

Write $\varepsilon = x + y\sqrt{d}$ (with $x,y\in\mathbb{Z}$ if $d\not\equiv 1\bmod 4$; otherwise $x,y\in\tfrac12\mathbb{Z}$). The norm is $N(\varepsilon) = (x+y\sqrt d)(x-y\sqrt d) = x^2 - dy^2 \in\{\pm 1\}$. So $(x,y)$ is a solution of Pell's equation $$x^2 \;-\; d y^2 \;=\; \pm 1.$$ Conversely every Pell solution gives a unit. Dirichlet's theorem is, in this case, the statement that Pell's equation has infinitely many integer solutions for every non-square $d>0$ — and they are exactly the powers of one fundamental solution.

Continued fractions compute the fundamental unit

Lagrange (1770) showed: for $d>0$ non-square, the continued-fraction expansion of $\sqrt{d}$ is purely periodic past $a_0$, $$\sqrt{d} \;=\; [a_0;\,\overline{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_{k-1},2a_0}].$$ Let $p_{k-1}/q_{k-1}$ be the convergent at the end of the first period. Then $$p_{k-1}^2 \;-\; d\,q_{k-1}^2 \;=\; (-1)^{k},$$ and $(p_{k-1},q_{k-1})$ is the smallest positive Pell solution — i.e. the fundamental unit (up to a square if the $-1$ Pell is unsolvable).

The size of $\varepsilon$ can be exponential in $\log d$: $d=61$ already has a 9-digit fundamental solution, and Archimedes' "cattle problem" reduces to Pell with $d=4729494$, whose smallest solution has $\sim 10^{206544}$ digits.

Negative Pell: when does $x^2-dy^2=-1$ have a solution?

The negative Pell equation $x^2-dy^2=-1$ is solvable iff $N(\varepsilon)=-1$, equivalently iff the period $k$ of $\sqrt d$ is odd.

  • $d=2,5,10,13,17,26,29,37,41,50,53,\ldots$ — solvable.
  • $d=3,6,7,11,12,14,15,18,19,21,22,23,\ldots$ — not solvable.

The proportion of squarefree $d$ for which negative Pell is solvable was conjectured by Stevenhagen to be $1-\prod_{j\ge 1}(1-2^{1-2j})\approx 0.5805$ — recently proved (asymptotically) by Koymans–Pagano (2022).

6. $S$-units and the higher analogue

Dirichlet's theorem upgrades to a more general statement once you allow a finite set of "denominators". Fix a finite set $S$ of places of $K$ containing all archimedean places. The ring of $S$-integers is $$\mathcal{O}_{K,S} \;=\; \{\,x\in K : v(x)\ge 0\text{ for all places }v\notin S\,\}.$$ Concretely, $\mathcal{O}_{K,S}$ is $\mathcal{O}_K$ with the primes in $S$ inverted — for $K=\mathbb{Q}$ and $S=\{\infty,2,3\}$, it is $\mathbb{Z}[1/6]$.

The units $\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^\times$ are called $S$-units.

$S$-unit theorem. $\mathcal{O}_{K,S}^\times \;\cong\; \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^{|S|-1}.$

The proof is the same Minkowski-style argument applied to the extended log-embedding $$\ell_S\colon \mathcal{O}_{K,S}^\times \;\to\; \mathbb{R}^{|S|},\qquad u\;\mapsto\;(\log|u|_v)_{v\in S},$$ with $|u|_v = -v(u)\log Nv$ for finite places $v\in S$. The product formula $\prod_v|u|_v=1$ across all places now reads, after restriction to $S$ (since $|u|_v=1$ for $v\notin S$ when $u$ is an $S$-unit), as one linear constraint $\sum_{v\in S}\log|u|_v=0$. The image is again a discrete full-rank lattice in this hyperplane — kernel still $\mu_K$, free rank $|S|-1$.

Consequence: the $S$-unit equation

$S$-unit equation theorem (Mahler 1933, Evertse). The equation $$u + v = 1, \qquad u, v \in \mathcal{O}_{K,S}^\times,$$ has only finitely many solutions.

This is one of the most leveraged finiteness theorems in Diophantine geometry. Two consequences:

  • Siegel's theorem on integral points. An affine curve of geometric genus $\ge 1$ over $K$ has only finitely many $S$-integral points. Reduce via the embedding into the Jacobian; the unit equation handles the resulting torus.
  • Faltings's theorem (Mordell) for higher genus, in the original Vojta–Bombieri proof, also runs through (much more refined) $S$-unit-type bounds.

The $S$-unit equation is effective in $K=\mathbb{Q}$ via Baker-style linear forms in logarithms (Baker, Győry); but Evertse-style proofs over general number fields are typically ineffective — they bound the number of solutions but not their size.

7. Connections

The unit theorem and its regulator sit at a crossroads: they are the multiplicative half of Minkowski's geometry of numbers, the regulator that goes alongside the class number in $\zeta_K$'s residue, and the prototype for every "leading term = regulator times class group" formula in modern arithmetic. Open frontiers include effective bounds for the $S$-unit equation in number fields beyond $\mathbb{Q}$, asymptotic densities for negative Pell (Stevenhagen, recently settled by Koymans–Pagano), and the conjectural Bloch–Kato / equivariant Tamagawa-number formulas that generalise the class number formula to motives.

  • Algebraic number theory — Class group and units — the parent topic; this page expands the rightmost column.
  • Analytic number theory — Dirichlet's theorem on primes in AP — uses the same nonvanishing-of-$L(1,\chi)$ machinery that the class number formula refines for general $K$.
  • Continued fractions — Convergents — the algorithmic engine that solves Pell's equation in real quadratic fields.
  • Computational number theory — Class & unit group computation — Buchmann's subexponential algorithm for $h_K$ and $R_K$ under GRH.
  • Iwasawa theory — main conjecture — climbs the cyclotomic tower; the Iwasawa main conjecture is the $p$-adic incarnation of the class number formula along $\mathbb{Z}_p$-extensions.
  • BSD — Néron–Tate regulator — for elliptic curves, the rank/regulator/$\Sha$ package on the Mordell–Weil lattice mirrors the rank/regulator/class-group package on $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$.
