Ramsey theory

Complete disorder is impossible: every structure large enough — a colored graph, a long interval of integers, a high-dimensional cube — is forced to contain an orderly monochromatic piece.

1. Pigeonhole & $R(3,3)=6$

The whole subject grows from one humble seed, the pigeonhole principle: if $n+1$ objects go into $n$ boxes, some box holds at least two. Push it a little — $kn+1$ objects into $n$ boxes forces a box with $\ge k+1$ — and you get the engine that manufactures order out of any large enough mess.

Here is the founding example. Color every edge of the complete graph $K_6$ either red or blue, however you like. Claim: you cannot avoid a monochromatic triangle. Fix any vertex $v$. It has $5$ incident edges in two colors, so by pigeonhole at least $\lceil 5/2\rceil = 3$ of them share a color — say $v$ has red edges to $a,b,c$. Now look at the triangle $a,b,c$:

  • if any one of $ab, bc, ca$ is red, it forms a red triangle with $v$;
  • otherwise all three of $ab, bc, ca$ are blue — a blue triangle.

Either way a single color closes a triangle. So $R(3,3)\le 6$, where $R(3,3)$ is the least $n$ forcing a monochromatic triangle in every $2$-coloring of $K_n$.

Why $5$ is not enough

The bound is tight: $K_5$ has a $2$-coloring with no monochromatic triangle. Place the $5$ vertices on a pentagon and color the $5$ rim edges red — that red graph is the $5$-cycle $C_5$, which has no triangle. The remaining $5$ edges are the pentagon's diagonals, forming the complementary $5$-cycle (a pentagram); color them blue, again triangle-free. Neither color contains a triangle, so $R(3,3)> 5$. Combined with the upper bound, $R(3,3)=6$ exactly.

Worked mini-example. Try to extend the pentagon coloring to $K_6$: add a sixth vertex $v$ and color its $5$ edges. Whatever you do, $3$ of them are one color — say red to $a,b,c$. The original pentagon coloring already places some edge among any three rim/diagonal vertices; you will find either a red edge inside $\{a,b,c\}$ (red triangle with $v$) or all-blue $\{a,b,c\}$ (blue triangle). The clean $K_5$ witness simply has no room to grow.

2. Ramsey's theorem (finite)

The $K_6$ argument was special, but the phenomenon is universal. Frank Ramsey (1930) proved that for every target there is a threshold past which monochromatic structure is unavoidable.

Ramsey's theorem (graph form). For all $s,t\ge 2$ there is a least integer $R(s,t)$ such that every $2$-edge-coloring of $K_{R(s,t)}$ contains a red $K_s$ or a blue $K_t$.

The previous section computed one entry, $R(3,3)=6$. The diagonal numbers $R(s):=R(s,s)$ are the headline cases. The boundary values are easy: a red edge or a blue $K_t$ is forced in $K_t$ once one color must appear, giving $R(2,t)=t$ and symmetrically $R(s,2)=s$.

The Erdős–Szekeres recurrence

Finiteness — and a usable bound — comes from a single inductive step. Take $N=R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1)$ vertices and pick one, $v$. Its $N-1$ edges split into red neighbors $r$ and blue neighbors $b$ with $r+b=N-1=R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1)-1$. By pigeonhole either $r\ge R(s-1,t)$ or $b\ge R(s,t-1)$.

  • If $r\ge R(s-1,t)$: among the red neighbors lives a red $K_{s-1}$ or a blue $K_t$. A red $K_{s-1}$ plus $v$ (all-red edges) is a red $K_s$. Done.
  • If $b\ge R(s,t-1)$: symmetrically, a blue $K_{t-1}$ plus $v$ gives a blue $K_t$.

So the recurrence holds:

$R(s,t)\le R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1).$

This is exactly Pascal's rule, and the base cases $R(2,t)=t=\binom{t}{1}$ match $\binom{(s)+(t)-2}{(s)-1}$ at the boundary. Induction then delivers the binomial bound:

$R(s,t)\le\binom{s+t-2}{s-1}.$

In particular $R(s,s)\le\binom{2s-2}{s-1}\le 4^{\,s-1}$, so the diagonal Ramsey numbers are at most exponential.

Worked mini-example. $R(3,4)\le R(2,4)+R(3,3)=4+6=10$. The binomial bound gives $\binom{3+4-2}{3-1}=\binom{5}{2}=10$ too. A parity refinement (when both summands in the recurrence are even, the sum can be cut by $1$) sharpens this to $R(3,4)=9$, the known exact value. Likewise $R(3,5)=14$ and $R(4,4)=18$ — every one of these still sits under its binomial ceiling.

3. Bounds & the exponential gap

The recurrence pins the diagonal number from above at $R(s,s)\le 4^{\,s-1}$. The matching lower bound is one of the most celebrated arguments in combinatorics, and it is not constructive: Erdős (1947) showed a good coloring exists without exhibiting one.

The probabilistic lower bound

Color each edge of $K_n$ red or blue independently with probability $\tfrac12$. A fixed set of $s$ vertices spans $\binom{s}{2}$ edges; it is monochromatic with probability $2\cdot 2^{-\binom{s}{2}}=2^{1-\binom{s}{2}}$. By linearity of expectation, the expected number of monochromatic $K_s$ is

$\mathbb{E}[X]=\binom{n}{s}\,2^{\,1-\binom{s}{2}}.$

If $\mathbb{E}[X]< 1$ then, since $X$ is a nonnegative integer, some coloring has $X=0$ — no monochromatic $K_s$ at all — and therefore $R(s,s)> n$. A short estimate shows $\mathbb{E}[X]< 1$ whenever $n\le 2^{s/2}$ (for $s\ge 3$), giving

$R(s,s)> 2^{\,s/2}.$

This is the dedicated probabilistic-method derivation's home; we cross-link it rather than re-deriving the constants here.

The gap

Stacking the two bounds:

$2^{\,s/2}\;<\; R(s,s)\;<\; 4^{\,s}.$

The base of the exponential is somewhere between $\sqrt2\approx 1.414$ and $4$, and pinning it down is a famous open problem. For seventy-five years only lower-order factors moved; in $2023$ Campos, Griffiths, Morris & Sahasrabudhe proved $R(s,s)\le(4-\varepsilon)^s$ for an explicit $\varepsilon> 0$ — the first improvement to the base of the upper bound, and still far from closing the gap.

Worked mini-example (scarcity). Exact diagonal values stop almost immediately: $R(3,3)=6$, $R(4,4)=18$, and then $R(5,5)$ is unknown — bounded only in $[43,48]$. Erdős's quip: if aliens demanded $R(5,5)$ or they destroy Earth, we should marshal every mathematician and computer; but if they demanded $R(6,6)$, we should attack the aliens. 4. Infinite Ramsey

Drop the finiteness and the statement gets cleaner, not harder. Write $[\mathbb{N}]^2$ for the set of unordered pairs of natural numbers.

Infinite Ramsey theorem. Every $2$-coloring of $[\mathbb{N}]^2$ has an infinite homogeneous set: an infinite $H\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ such that all pairs $\{i,j\}\subseteq H$ get the same color.

The construction

Build a sequence $a_0,a_1,a_2,\dots$ and a shrinking pool. Start with $a_0=0$ and the pool $S_0=\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$. The pairs from $a_0$ to $S_0$ are $2$-colored, so by pigeonhole one color $c_0$ is used to infinitely many of them; let $S_1\subseteq S_0$ be that infinite monochromatic neighborhood and set $a_1=\min S_1$. Repeat: from $a_1$, infinitely many edges into $S_1$ share a color $c_1$; keep that infinite pool $S_2$, set $a_2=\min S_2$; and so on. Each $a_k$ comes tagged with a color $c_k$ such that every later $a_j$ ($j> k$) joins $a_k$ in color $c_k$.

Now the colors $c_0,c_1,c_2,\dots$ are an infinite $2$-coloring of indices, so one color — say red — recurs infinitely often, at indices $k_0< k_1<\cdots$. The set $H=\{a_{k_0},a_{k_1},\dots\}$ is infinite, and any pair $\{a_{k_p},a_{k_q}\}$ with $p< q$ is colored $c_{k_p}=$ red. So $H$ is an infinite red-homogeneous set.

Infinite ⟹ finite (compactness)

The infinite theorem implies the finite one. Suppose, for contradiction, that for some $s$ no finite $N$ forced an $s$-clique-or-anticlique: then for every $N$ there is a bad $2$-coloring of $K_N$. These bad colorings form an infinite, finitely-branching tree (a coloring of $K_{N+1}$ restricts to one of $K_N$), so by König's lemma there is an infinite branch — a $2$-coloring of all of $[\mathbb{N}]^2$ with no monochromatic $K_s$, hence no infinite homogeneous set. That contradicts infinite Ramsey. So a finite threshold must exist after all — a slick non-constructive proof of finiteness that gives no bound at all.

Worked mini-example. Color $\{i,j\}$ red if $\lfloor\log_2 i\rfloor=\lfloor\log_2 j\rfloor$ (same binary length) and blue otherwise. Each "length class" $\{2^k,\dots,2^{k+1}-1\}$ is finite, so no infinite red set exists — but picking one integer from each length class gives an infinite set whose pairs are all blue. Infinite Ramsey guarantees such an infinite monochromatic set must exist, here visibly blue.

5. Van der Waerden

Ramsey's theorem lives on graphs; the same "order is unavoidable" principle has an arithmetic incarnation. Color the integers and you are forced to monochromatic arithmetic progressions.

Van der Waerden's theorem (1927). For all $r,k$ there is a least $W(r,k)$ such that every $r$-coloring of $\{1,2,\dots,N\}$ with $N\ge W(r,k)$ contains a monochromatic $k$-term arithmetic progression $a,\,a+d,\,\dots,\,a+(k-1)d$ (with $d\ge 1$).

An arithmetic progression (AP) is the additive analogue of a clique: a perfectly regular pattern that no finite coloring of a long enough interval can dodge. Note this is a statement about partition regularity — splitting $\{1,\dots,N\}$ into $r$ color classes can never make all classes AP-free.

What is and isn't known

Existence is elementary (a clever double induction on $k$ and $r$), but it gives monstrous bounds. The classical proof yields a function growing faster than any primitive-recursive function — Ackermann-type. Gowers later proved a merely tower-free upper bound,

$W(r,k)\le 2^{\,2^{\,r^{\,2^{\,2^{\,k+9}}}}},$

still astronomically large. Exact values are known only for tiny parameters: $W(2,3)=9$, $W(2,4)=35$, $W(2,5)=178$, $W(3,3)=27$. The density refinement — that any subset of $\{1,\dots,N\}$ of positive density (not just a color class) contains long APs — is Szemerédi's theorem, far deeper than the coloring version.

Worked mini-example. $W(2,3)=9$: any red/blue coloring of $\{1,\dots,9\}$ has a monochromatic $3$-AP, but $\{1,\dots,8\}$ can be dodged — the coloring $\texttt{RRBBRRBB}$ (i.e. $1,2$ red, $3,4$ blue, $5,6$ red, $7,8$ blue) has no monochromatic $3$-term AP. 6. Hales–Jewett

Van der Waerden is itself the shadow of a more structural theorem about high-dimensional cubes. Work in $[t]^n$, the strings of length $n$ over the alphabet $\{1,\dots,t\}$ — think of an $n$-dimensional tic-tac-toe board with side $t$.

A combinatorial line is the cube's analogue of a winning row. Choose a nonempty set $W$ of wildcard coordinates and fix the others; as the wildcards move together through $1,2,\dots,t$, they trace out $t$ strings. For instance in $[3]^2$ the line with both coordinates wildcard is $\{11,22,33\}$; the line with first coordinate wildcard and second fixed at $2$ is $\{12,22,32\}$.

Hales–Jewett theorem (1963). For all $t,r$ there is a dimension $HJ(t,r)$ such that every $r$-coloring of $[t]^n$ with $n\ge HJ(t,r)$ contains a monochromatic combinatorial line.

This is a purely combinatorial statement — no arithmetic, no geometry, just strings — which is exactly why it is so strong: it has no "edge effects" to exploit.

Hales–Jewett ⟹ Van der Waerden

Map a string $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\in[t]^n$ to the integer $\phi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-1)\,t^{\,i-1}$, its base-$t$ value. An $r$-coloring of $\{0,\dots,t^n-1\}$ pulls back to an $r$-coloring of $[t]^n$. Under $\phi$, a combinatorial line with wildcard set $W$ becomes

$\phi=a,\;a+d,\;\dots,\;a+(t-1)d,\qquad d=\sum_{i\in W}t^{\,i-1}\ge 1,$

a $t$-term arithmetic progression. So a monochromatic combinatorial line maps to a monochromatic $t$-term AP, and $HJ(t,r)$ furnishes a valid $W(r,t)$. Hales–Jewett is the master theorem; Van der Waerden is the projection to one dimension.

The density version

The density Hales–Jewett theorem (DHJ) replaces "every coloring has a monochromatic line" with "every subset of $[t]^n$ of positive density contains a combinatorial line," once $n$ is large. Proved by the first Polymath collaboration (2009), DHJ implies Szemerédi's theorem the same way HJ implies Van der Waerden — it is the structural summit of the whole edifice.

Worked mini-example. In $[3]^2$ (a $3\times 3$ grid of strings $11,12,\dots,33$) the combinatorial lines are the three rows (first coordinate wildcard), the three columns (second coordinate wildcard), and the main diagonal $\{11,22,33\}$ (both wildcard) — but not the anti-diagonal $\{13,22,31\}$, since the two coordinates move in opposite directions there. With $r=2$ colors and $n=2$ you can still $2$-color $[3]^2$ avoiding a monochromatic line, so $HJ(3,2)> 2$; you must climb to higher $n$.

Connections

Ramsey theory's slogan — complete disorder is impossible — is settled in spirit but wide open in the numbers. The exact diagonal $R(s,s)$ is unknown past $s=4$, the base of its exponential is uncertain to within a factor of $\sqrt2$ versus $4$, and the Van der Waerden numbers $W(r,k)$ are uncomputed beyond a handful of cases. The frontier is quantitative: how large must a structure be before order is forced, and what is the true growth rate? The density versions (Szemerédi, DHJ) trade coloring for measure and reach far deeper, connecting Ramsey theory to ergodic theory and analysis.

  • Probabilistic method — the Ramsey lower bound — the full derivation of $R(s,s)> 2^{s/2}$ by random coloring, the founding example of the probabilistic method.
  • Extremal combinatorics — Ramsey numbers — Ramsey's theorem alongside Schur's theorem, sum-free sets, and the Turán-type counterpoint of forbidding a fixed subgraph globally.
  • Extremal combinatorics — Turán's theorem — the dual question: not "any partition forces order" but "how many edges force a clique."
  • Graph theory fundamentals — graphs & the handshake lemma — the complete graph $K_n$ and edge-counting underpinning every clique argument here.
  • Graph theory fundamentals — graph coloring — vertex coloring is the constructive cousin of the edge-coloring games Ramsey theory plays.
