Gauge theory

Connections on principal bundles, curvature as the obstruction to flatness, and the Yang-Mills action whose critical points are the gauge fields of fundamental physics. From holonomy and Wilson loops to instantons, Chern-Simons, lattice discretization, and the Donaldson / Seiberg-Witten invariants that detect exotic smooth structures in dimension four.

1. Principal bundles and connections

A principal $G$-bundle is a smooth fiber bundle $\pi\colon P\to M$ together with a smooth right action of a Lie group $G$ on $P$ that is free, fiber-preserving, and transitive on each fiber. Local triviality says every $x\in M$ has a neighborhood $U$ with a $G$-equivariant diffeomorphism $\pi^{-1}(U)\cong U\times G$; transition functions on overlaps are smooth maps $g_{\alpha\beta}\colon U_\alpha\cap U_\beta\to G$ acting by left multiplication. The bundle is determined up to isomorphism by its cocycle $\{g_{\alpha\beta}\}$.

The fundamental fields make the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ visible inside $TP$. For each $\xi\in\mathfrak{g}$ define the fundamental vector field $\xi^*$ on $P$ by $\xi^*_p = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\,p\cdot\exp(t\xi)$. Its image at every $p$ spans the vertical subspace $V_p P = \ker d\pi_p \subset T_pP$ — the directions tangent to the fiber.

What we still need. The vertical bundle $V\subset TP$ is canonical (it sees only the fiber action). To talk about parallel transport we need a choice of horizontal complement: a $G$-equivariant subbundle $H\subset TP$ with $V\oplus H = TP$. This choice is a connection.

The cleanest packaging is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on $P$:

A connection 1-form on $P$ is $A\in\Omega^1(P;\mathfrak{g})$ such that:
  • Verticality: $A(\xi^*) = \xi$ for every $\xi\in\mathfrak{g}$ — the form recovers the Lie-algebra direction of fundamental fields.
  • Equivariance: $R_g^* A = \mathrm{Ad}(g^{-1})A$ for every $g\in G$ — translating along the fiber adjoint-twists the form.
The horizontal subspace at $p$ is then $H_p = \ker A_p$.

Picking a local section $\sigma\colon U\to P$ produces a local gauge potential $A^\sigma = \sigma^*A\in\Omega^1(U;\mathfrak{g})$ — the physicist's $A_\mu$. On a triviality patch with coordinates $x^\mu$, $A^\sigma = A_\mu(x)\,dx^\mu$ where each $A_\mu$ is matrix-valued ($A_\mu\in\mathfrak{g}$). Two sections $\sigma$ and $\sigma\cdot g$ give potentials related by the gauge transformation law (§3); the bundle picture is the geometry that makes that law forced.

Given a smooth path $\gamma\colon [0,1]\to M$ and an initial point $\tilde p_0\in\pi^{-1}(\gamma(0))$, the horizontal lift is the unique path $\tilde\gamma\colon [0,1]\to P$ with $\pi\circ\tilde\gamma = \gamma$, $\tilde\gamma(0) = \tilde p_0$, and $\dot{\tilde\gamma}\in H_{\tilde\gamma}$ at every time. This is an ODE of the form $\dot{\tilde\gamma} = -A(\dot\gamma)\cdot\tilde\gamma$ in local coordinates — a first-order linear equation in $G$ — so existence and uniqueness are automatic. Parallel transport $P_\gamma\colon\pi^{-1}(\gamma(0))\to\pi^{-1}(\gamma(1))$ is the resulting fiber map.

2. Curvature and the Yang-Mills action

Given a connection $A$ on a principal $G$-bundle $P\to M$, its curvature is the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 2-form

$$F_A \;=\; dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A\wedge A] \;=\; dA + A\wedge A,$$

where the second equality holds in matrix-Lie-algebra notation (the wedge $A\wedge A$ uses both the antisymmetric form product and matrix multiplication). Equivalently $F_A$ is the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued horizontal 2-form on $P$ measuring the failure of horizontal subspaces to be Frobenius-integrable: $F_A(X, Y) = -A([X^h, Y^h])$ on horizontal lifts. The connection is flat when $F_A = 0$.

The curvature obeys two universal identities:

  • Bianchi identity: $d_A F_A = dF_A + [A, F_A] = 0$, automatic from $d^2 = 0$ and the structure equation. No equation of motion is invoked.
  • Tensoriality under gauge change: $A\to g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ implies $F_A\to g^{-1}F_A g$. The inhomogeneous shift in $A$ is absorbed by the wedge term in $F$; this is the design of the curvature.

Pick an $\mathrm{Ad}$-invariant inner product on $\mathfrak{g}$ (e.g. $\langle X, Y\rangle = -\tr(XY)$ for $\mathfrak{su}(N)$) and a Riemannian metric on $M$. The Yang-Mills action is

$$S_{YM}(A) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\int_M \langle F_A\wedge *F_A\rangle.$$

Varying with respect to $A$ gives the Yang-Mills equations

$$d_A^* F_A \;=\; 0, \qquad d_A F_A \;=\; 0\;(\text{Bianchi}),$$

where $d_A^* = -*d_A*$ is the formal adjoint. The pair is the non-abelian generalization of Maxwell: for $G = U(1)$, $\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{R}$, all brackets vanish, $F = dA$ becomes a closed 2-form, and the equations reduce to $dF = 0$ (no magnetic monopoles) and $d^*F = 0$ (no electric sources) in vacuum. For non-abelian $G$, the wedge term $A\wedge A$ makes Yang-Mills nonlinear — and the source of the rich non-perturbative phenomenology of QCD.

3. Gauge transformations and holonomy

The gauge group is $\mathcal{G} = \Gamma(P\times_{\mathrm{Ad}} G)$ — sections of the associated bundle whose fiber is $G$ acting on itself by conjugation. Concretely, after a local trivialization, $\mathcal{G}$ consists of smooth $G$-valued functions $g\colon U\to G$ identified across overlaps via $g_\beta = h_{\alpha\beta}^{-1} g_\alpha h_{\alpha\beta}$ where $\{h_{\alpha\beta}\}$ are the bundle's transition functions.

An element $g\in\mathcal{G}$ acts on a connection $A$ (locally) by

$$A \;\longmapsto\; A^g \;=\; g^{-1}\,A\,g \;+\; g^{-1}\,dg.$$

The conjugation $g^{-1}Ag$ rotates $A$ in the Lie-algebra fiber; the inhomogeneous Maurer-Cartan tail $g^{-1}dg$ is what makes the transformation respect changes of section rather than tensor changes of frame. Curvature transforms tensorially, $F_A\to g^{-1}F_A g$ — the inhomogeneous tail evaporates because $F$ is built to compensate. So $\tr(F_A^k)$ and $\langle F_A\wedge *F_A\rangle$ are gauge-invariant; $A$ alone is not.

Holonomy. Parallel transport (§1) along a closed loop $\gamma$ based at $x\in M$ produces a fiber automorphism $P_\gamma\colon\pi^{-1}(x)\to\pi^{-1}(x)$, which after a choice of base-point is encoded as an element $\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma; p_0)\in G$. Changing the base-point by $h\in G$ conjugates: $\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma; p_0\cdot h) = h^{-1}\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma; p_0)h$. So $\mathrm{Hol}$ is well-defined as a conjugacy class. Under a gauge transformation $g$, $\mathrm{Hol}_{A^g}(\gamma) = g(x)^{-1}\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma)g(x)$ — also conjugation.

The conjugation-equivariance kills the gauge dependence inside an invariant function of the holonomy. The Wilson loop in representation $R\colon G\to GL(V)$ is

$$W_R(\gamma) \;=\; \tr_V\bigl(R(\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma))\bigr),$$

and is gauge-invariant (cyclicity of trace cancels the conjugation). Wilson loops generate the algebra of gauge-invariant local-and-non-local observables and are the object of choice for confinement diagnostics (§6) and Chern-Simons knot invariants (§5).

For the abelian case $G = U(1)$, the noncommuting matrix structure disappears: $\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma) = \exp\bigl(i\oint_\gamma A\bigr)\in U(1)$. Stokes' theorem on a bounding disk $D$ gives $\oint_\gamma A = \int_D F$, so the holonomy is just the exponential of enclosed flux — the Aharonov-Bohm phase.

4. Self-dual connections and instantons

On an oriented Riemannian 4-manifold $(M, g)$, the Hodge star $*\colon\Omega^k\to\Omega^{4-k}$ takes a special form on 2-forms: $*\colon\Omega^2(M)\to\Omega^2(M)$ with $*^2 = +1$. This involution is the structural seed of all of 4D gauge theory — it splits

$$\Omega^2(M) \;=\; \Omega^2_+(M) \oplus \Omega^2_-(M),$$

the self-dual ($+1$ eigenspace) and anti-self-dual ($-1$ eigenspace) summands. Curvature also splits: $F_A = F_A^+ + F_A^-$.

Bogomolny bound. For any connection on a closed Riemannian 4-manifold, $$S_{YM}(A) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\int |F_A|^2 \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\int(|F_A^+|^2 + |F_A^-|^2) \;\ge\; \bigl|\tfrac{1}{2}\int(|F_A^+|^2 - |F_A^-|^2)\bigr| \;=\; 8\pi^2|k|,$$ where $k = \tfrac{1}{8\pi^2}\int\tr(F_A\wedge F_A)\in\mathbb{Z}$ is the second Chern number (instanton charge). Equality holds iff $F_A^+ = 0$ (anti-self-dual) or $F_A^- = 0$ (self-dual).

So (anti-)self-dual connections ($*F_A = \pm F_A$) are the absolute minima of Yang-Mills in their topological class. They automatically solve the Yang-Mills equations: if $*F_A = -F_A$ then $d_A^* F_A = -*\,d_A * F_A = -*\,d_A(-F_A) = *\,d_A F_A = 0$ by the Bianchi identity. The full ASD condition is a first-order nonlinear PDE — far more tractable than the second-order YM equations — and the seed of Donaldson's program (§7).

The BPST instanton (Belavin-Polyakov-Schwartz-Tyupkin, 1975) is the explicit charge-1 ASD $SU(2)$ connection on $\mathbb{R}^4 = \mathbb{H}$ given (in 't Hooft form) by

$$A \;=\; \mathrm{Im}\!\left(\frac{\bar q\,dq}{|q|^2 + \rho^2}\right) \;\in\; \Omega^1(\mathbb{R}^4;\mathfrak{su}(2)),$$

where $q\in\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{R}^4$ and $\rho > 0$ is the size parameter. The curvature density is

$$|F|^2(x) \;=\; \frac{96\,\rho^4}{(|x|^2 + \rho^2)^4},$$

concentrated within a region of radius $\sim\rho$ around the origin. Translating gives the 4-parameter family $A_{x_0,\rho}$. After conformal compactification $\mathbb{R}^4\cup\{\infty\}\cong S^4$, the gauge field extends smoothly: the BPST instanton lives on $S^4$.

The space of charge-1 BPST instantons on $S^4$ modulo gauge is parametrized by $(x_0, \rho)\in\mathbb{R}^4\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ — a 5-dimensional moduli space $\mathcal{M}_1$. For higher charge $k$, $\dim\mathcal{M}_k = 8k - 3$. Donaldson's invariants (§7) come from intersection theory on $\mathcal{M}_k(X)$ when the base is replaced by a general 4-manifold $X$.

5. Chern-Simons theory

On a closed oriented 3-manifold $M$, define the Chern-Simons 3-form of a connection $A$ on a $G$-bundle (locally / for trivial bundles)

$$\omega_{CS}(A) \;=\; \tr\!\left(A\wedge dA \;+\; \tfrac{2}{3}\,A\wedge A\wedge A\right).$$

A direct computation using the structure equation $F = dA + A\wedge A$ gives the transgression

$$d\omega_{CS}(A) \;=\; \tr(F\wedge F),$$

so $\omega_{CS}$ is a primitive on $M^3$ for the second Chern character living naturally on a 4-dimensional bulk. The Chern-Simons action at level $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ is

$$S_{CS}(A) \;=\; \tfrac{k}{4\pi}\int_M \omega_{CS}(A).$$

Two miracles:

Metric independence. $\omega_{CS}$ uses only $\wedge$ and $d$ — there is no Hodge $*$, no metric. So $S_{CS}$ is a smooth-structure invariant of $(M, A)$. Diffeomorphisms act trivially on the action (modulo the gauge-orbit subtlety below).
Level quantization. Under a 'large' gauge transformation $g\colon M\to G$ (not homotopic to the identity), one finds $S_{CS}(A^g) - S_{CS}(A) = 2\pi k\cdot\deg(g)$, where $\deg(g)\in\pi_3(G) = \mathbb{Z}$ for simply-connected simple $G$. So $e^{iS_{CS}}$ is well-defined as a function on the quotient by gauge transformations only when $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ — the level is quantized.

Witten (1989) showed that the path integral $Z(M) = \int\mathcal{D}A\,e^{iS_{CS}(A)}$ over the gauge orbits computes a topological invariant of the 3-manifold $M$. Inserting Wilson loops $W_R(\gamma) = \tr_R\,\mathrm{Hol}_A(\gamma)$ for a knot $\gamma\subset M$ in representation $R$ produces the colored Jones polynomial of the knot $(\gamma, R)$ — at level $k$ for $G = SU(2)$ and the fundamental rep, the result is the Jones polynomial evaluated at $q = e^{2\pi i/(k+2)}$.

This was the first interpretation of a knot invariant as a quantum field theory expectation value, and the founding example of a topological quantum field theory (Atiyah-Segal axiomatization) producing 3-manifold invariants by cutting along surfaces.

6. Lattice gauge theory

To make the Yang-Mills path integral $Z = \int\mathcal{D}A\,e^{-S_{YM}(A)}$ rigorously well-defined and amenable to Monte Carlo simulation, Wilson (1974) discretized to a hypercubic lattice $\Lambda = a\mathbb{Z}^4$ with spacing $a$. The trick: replace the connection $A_\mu(x)$ by link variables $U_{xy}\in G$ on each oriented edge $\langle x, y\rangle$, interpreted as the parallel transport from $x$ to $y$. In the continuum limit,

$$U_{x, x+a\hat\mu} \;\approx\; \exp\!\bigl(a\, A_\mu(x)\bigr).$$

Around each elementary square (plaquette) bounded by four links, the holonomy is the ordered product

$$U_p \;=\; U_{x, x+a\hat\mu}\, U_{x+a\hat\mu, x+a\hat\mu+a\hat\nu}\, U_{x+a\hat\mu+a\hat\nu, x+a\hat\nu}\, U_{x+a\hat\nu, x}.$$

The Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff expansion gives $U_p \approx \exp(a^2 F_{\mu\nu}(x) + O(a^4))$, identifying the plaquette as the discretized field strength.

The Wilson plaquette action is

$$S_W \;=\; \beta\sum_p\,\mathrm{Re}\,\tr(\mathbb{1} - U_p)\;\approx\;\frac{\beta a^4}{2}\sum_p\,\tr(F_{\mu\nu}^2(x)) + O(a^6),$$

recovering the continuum action with $\beta = 2N/g^2$ for $G = SU(N)$. The win:

  • Exact gauge invariance at finite $a$: under $U_{xy}\mapsto g_x^{-1}U_{xy}g_y$, every closed-loop product is conjugated, hence its real-part trace is unchanged. No gauge fixing during simulation.
  • Compact group $\Rightarrow$ canonical Haar measure: $\int dU\,f(U)$ is well-defined for finite link-variable count, no Faddeev-Popov ghosts, no functional measure issues.
  • Monte Carlo accessible: the partition function $Z = \int\prod dU_{xy}\,e^{-S_W}$ at finite lattice volume is a finite-dimensional integral simulable by Metropolis-Hastings, heat-bath, or hybrid Monte Carlo updates of one link at a time.

The decisive observable is the Wilson loop: for a closed lattice path $\gamma$, $W(\gamma) = \tr\bigl(\prod_{\langle x,y\rangle\in\gamma}U_{xy}\bigr)$. The expectation value $\langle W(\gamma)\rangle$ on a large rectangular $T\times R$ loop encodes the static quark-antiquark potential. Two regimes:

7. Donaldson and Seiberg-Witten invariants

Dimension 4 is uniquely strange. Freedman (1982) classified topological simply-connected 4-manifolds: each is determined by its intersection form on $H^2$ together with the Kirby-Siebenmann obstruction. Donaldson (1983) showed that this topological classification radically overcounts smooth 4-manifolds: many of Freedman's topological types admit no smooth structure, and many that do admit several inequivalent ones. The discriminator is gauge theory.

Donaldson's program. Fix a closed oriented simply-connected 4-manifold $X$ with $b_2^+(X)\ge 1$. Equip it with a Riemannian metric $g$ and consider the moduli space

$$\mathcal{M}_k(X, g) \;=\; \{A \in \mathcal{A}_k\;:\;F_A^+ = 0\}\,/\,\mathcal{G},$$

of charge-$k$ ASD $SU(2)$ connections modulo gauge. For generic $g$, $\mathcal{M}_k$ is a smooth manifold of expected dimension $8k - 3(1 + b_2^+(X))$. A natural map $\mu\colon H_2(X;\mathbb{Z})\to H^2(\mathcal{M}_k;\mathbb{Z})$ produces cohomology on the moduli space from cycles on $X$. Donaldson's polynomial invariants are intersection numbers

$$D_X(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_d) \;=\; \int_{\bar{\mathcal{M}}_k}\mu(\alpha_1)\smile\cdots\smile\mu(\alpha_d),$$

where $\bar{\mathcal{M}}_k$ is the Uhlenbeck compactification (allowing instantons to "bubble off" at points). These intersection numbers depend only on the smooth structure on $X$ — and they distinguish exotic 4-manifolds.

Seiberg-Witten (1994). A decade later, Seiberg and Witten extracted from string-theory dualities a far simpler abelian moduli problem. Pick a Spin$^c$ structure $\mathfrak{s}$ on $X$ (always exists when $X$ is simply-connected and almost-complex; classified by $H^2(X;\mathbb{Z})$). Couple a $U(1)$ connection $A$ on the determinant line $L_\mathfrak{s}$ to a section $\phi$ of the positive spinor bundle $W_\mathfrak{s}^+$. The SW equations are

$$F_A^+ \;=\; \sigma(\phi),\qquad D_A\phi \;=\; 0,$$

where $D_A$ is the Spin$^c$ Dirac operator and $\sigma(\phi)$ is the trace-free part of $\phi\otimes\phi^*$. The crucial features:

  • Compact moduli space: the spinor mass term forbids bubbling, so $\mathcal{M}_{SW}(\mathfrak{s})$ is compact without Uhlenbeck compactification. Generically it is a finite collection of points.
  • Signed point counts: the SW invariant $\mathrm{SW}(\mathfrak{s})\in\mathbb{Z}$ is the algebraic count of points in $\mathcal{M}_{SW}(\mathfrak{s})$. Only finitely many $\mathfrak{s}$ have $\mathrm{SW}(\mathfrak{s})\ne 0$ — the basic classes.
  • Witten's relation: there is a universal formula expressing Donaldson polynomials in terms of SW invariants and basic classes — the Donaldson moduli's hard non-abelian analysis is (conjecturally, proved in many cases) equivalent to the abelian SW computation.

They are distinguished by gauge-theoretic invariants of compact pieces — there is no analogue of this phenomenon in any other dimension.

8. Connections

Gauge theory sits at a crossroads. The same Yang-Mills functional whose moduli detect smooth 4-manifold structures is the action of the Standard Model gauge fields; the Chern-Simons functional whose level quantization gives knot polynomials is also the boundary action of a 4D topological field theory; lattice gauge theory feeds Monte Carlo back into hadron mass calculations. Active research includes the geometric Langlands correspondence (gauge theory on Riemann surfaces $\Leftrightarrow$ representation theory of $\pi_1$), Kapustin-Witten 4D twists, Floer homologies for low-dimensional topology, and conjectural extensions of SW theory to non-abelian groups.

  • Differential geometry — geodesics and parallel transport — the metric-compatible Levi-Civita connection is the special case where $G = O(n)$ and $A$ comes from the metric; gauge theory abstracts the principle.
  • Lie groups and the exponential map — every value $\exp(A)$ takes is a parallel-transport element along an infinitesimal step.
  • Chern-Weil theory — characteristic classes are differential-form polynomials in the curvature; Chern-Simons is the secondary class that lives on a bounding 3-manifold.
  • Atiyah-Singer index theorem — the Dirac operator coupled to a gauge field has analytic index computed by characteristic numbers; this powers Witten's TQFT computations.
  • Knot polynomials — Wilson loops in Chern-Simons reproduce Jones, HOMFLY, and Kauffman polynomials, putting knot theory inside QFT.
