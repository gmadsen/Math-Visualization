Several complex variables & Stein manifolds

In $\mathbb{C}^n$ for $n\ge 2$, isolated singularities are forbidden, the Riemann mapping theorem fails, and the geometry of a domain dictates whether holomorphic functions live there at all. This page walks the SCV canon: the Hartogs phenomenon, plurisubharmonic functions and the Levi form, Hörmander's $L^2$-solution to $\bar\partial$, pseudoconvexity and the Levi problem, Stein manifolds with Cartan's Theorems A and B, and the Cousin problems.

1. Hartogs phenomenon — extension is forced

One-variable complex analysis is built around local singularities. An isolated singularity at $z_0\in\mathbb{C}$ is removable, polar, or essential — the trichotomy is real, and $e^{1/z}$ at $0$ shows the third case is genuinely populated. Pass to $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $n\ge 2$ and the third case empties out. There are no isolated singularities at all.

Hartogs's extension theorem (1906). Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ ($n\ge 2$) be a domain and $K\subset\Omega$ a compact set with $\Omega\setminus K$ connected. Every holomorphic $f\colon \Omega\setminus K\to\mathbb{C}$ extends uniquely to a holomorphic function on all of $\Omega$.

The cleanest test case is the Hartogs figure. Fix radii $0 $$H_r \;=\; \bigl\{|z_1|<1,\, r<|z_2|<1\bigr\}\,\cup\,\bigl\{r<|z_1|<1,\, |z_2|<1\bigr\}.$$

This is the bidisk $\mathbb{D}^2$ with a smaller closed bidisk removed — concretely, the polydisk shell. Hartogs proves that any holomorphic function on $H_r$ extends to all of $\mathbb{D}^2$. The argument is concrete: for fixed $z_1$ with $|z_1|

Iterating this lemma across nested compact holes gives the general statement: in dimension $n\ge 2$, any compact set in a connected domain is a removable obstacle for holomorphic functions. The phenomenon has no one-dimensional shadow because in $\mathbb{C}$ the boundary of a compact hole disconnects $\Omega\setminus K$ — the connectivity hypothesis fails immediately.

Reinhardt domains and the logarithmic image

A domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ is Reinhardt if $(z_1,\ldots,z_n)\in\Omega$ implies $(\zeta_1 z_1,\ldots,\zeta_n z_n)\in\Omega$ for all $|\zeta_j|=1$. Every Reinhardt domain is determined by its image $\log|\Omega| = \{(\log|z_1|,\ldots,\log|z_n|) : z\in\Omega\}\subset\mathbb{R}^n$. The logarithmically convex hull of this image — the smallest convex set containing it — turns out to be exactly the holomorphically convex hull. Hartogs phenomena correspond to non-convex points: a Reinhardt domain whose log-image is non-convex admits an extension to its log-convex hull.

Slogan. Convexity in $\log$-coordinates of a Reinhardt domain $\Leftrightarrow$ no Hartogs extension. The non-Hartogs domains are exactly the log-convex Reinhardt domains.

2. Plurisubharmonic functions and the Levi form

Subharmonic functions in one variable — upper-semicontinuous functions with $\Delta u\ge 0$ in the distributional sense — are the convex-flavored objects that govern logarithms of moduli of holomorphic functions: $\log|f|$ is subharmonic. In several variables the right replacement is plurisubharmonic (PSH).

Definition. An upper-semicontinuous function $u\colon\Omega\to[-\infty,\infty)$ on $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ is plurisubharmonic (PSH) if for every complex line $\ell\colon\zeta\mapsto a+\zeta b$, the restriction $\zeta\mapsto u(a+\zeta b)$ is subharmonic on the open set where it is defined.

Equivalently, when $u$ is $C^2$, plurisubharmonicity is the positivity of the complex Hessian:

$$\bigl(\partial\bar\partial u\bigr)_{j\bar k} \;=\; \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z_j\,\partial\bar z_k}, \qquad u\text{ PSH}\;\Longleftrightarrow\; \bigl[\tfrac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k}\bigr]_{j,k}\ge 0\;\text{(Hermitian positive semidefinite)}.$$

The current $i\,\partial\bar\partial u$ is a real $(1,1)$-form, and PSH says exactly that this current is non-negative as a $(1,1)$-form. Note that PSH is more restrictive than subharmonic: $\Delta u = 4\sum_j u_{j\bar j}\ge 0$ only requires the trace of the complex Hessian to be non-negative, while PSH requires every diagonal entry — and in fact the entire matrix — to be non-negative.

Examples

  • $\log|f|$ for holomorphic $f$. Indeed $\bar\partial\log|f|^2 = \bar\partial\log|f|^2$ is well-defined, and $\partial\bar\partial\log|f|^2 = 0$ wherever $f\ne 0$, while at zeros it picks up a positive current (Lelong number). So $\log|f|$ is PSH on the whole domain.
  • $|z|^2 = \sum |z_j|^2$. The Hessian is the identity $I$ — strictly PSH. More generally, any convex function of $|z_1|^2,\ldots,|z_n|^2$ that is non-decreasing in each argument is PSH.
  • $\log\bigl(|f_1|^2+\cdots+|f_m|^2\bigr)$ for holomorphic $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ — the regularized $\log|f|$ for vector-valued data.

The Levi form

For a real-valued $C^2$ defining function $\rho$ of a domain $\Omega = \{\rho<0\}$ with $d\rho\ne 0$ on $\partial\Omega$, the boundary $\partial\Omega$ inherits a complex tangent bundle $T^{1,0}\partial\Omega$ of complex rank $n-1$ — the kernel of $\partial\rho$ in $T^{1,0}\mathbb{C}^n$. The Levi form at a boundary point $p$ is the restriction of the Hermitian form $\partial\bar\partial\rho|_p$ to $T^{1,0}_p\partial\Omega$. The boundary is Levi pseudoconvex at $p$ if this restricted form is $\ge 0$, and strictly Levi pseudoconvex if $> 0$. The condition is independent of the choice of defining function (changing $\rho\to e^h\rho$ rescales the Levi form by $e^h>0$ on the tangent directions).

3. The $\bar\partial$-equation and Hörmander's $L^2$ estimates

The Cauchy–Riemann operator splits as $d = \partial + \bar\partial$ on a complex manifold, where $\partial$ raises holomorphic degree and $\bar\partial$ raises antiholomorphic degree. Holomorphy is precisely the kernel of $\bar\partial$, and the question driving SCV at the technical level is:

The $\bar\partial$-problem. Given a $\bar\partial$-closed $(0,q)$-form $\alpha$ on $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ — meaning $\bar\partial\alpha=0$ — does there exist a $(0,q-1)$-form $u$ with $\bar\partial u = \alpha$, and how big can we keep $u$?

For $q=1$ on a planar domain ($n=1$), this is the inhomogeneous Cauchy–Riemann equation $\partial_{\bar z} u = f$, always solvable on planar simply connected domains by the Cauchy–Pompeiu integral. In $n\ge 2$ the analytical situation is much more delicate, and the right tool is Hörmander's $L^2$ estimate.

Hörmander (1965). Let $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ be a pseudoconvex domain and $\varphi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ a smooth strictly plurisubharmonic weight. For every $\bar\partial$-closed $(0,1)$-form $\alpha = \sum \alpha_j\, d\bar z_j$ with $$\int_\Omega |\alpha|^2_{i\partial\bar\partial\varphi}\,e^{-\varphi}\,dV \;<\; \infty,$$ there exists a function $u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}$ with $\bar\partial u = \alpha$ and $$\int_\Omega |u|^2 e^{-\varphi}\,dV \;\le\; \int_\Omega |\alpha|^2_{i\partial\bar\partial\varphi}\,e^{-\varphi}\,dV.$$

The norm on $\alpha$ is taken with respect to the metric $i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ on $(0,1)$-forms — the metric is non-degenerate exactly because the weight is strictly PSH. The estimate replaces "is the system overdetermined?" with "find the minimal-norm solution" and quantifies the cost via an explicit weight.

What Hörmander buys you

Pulling on the weight $\varphi$ is the entire game. Three flagship choices:

  • Existence of $\bar\partial$-solutions on pseudoconvex domains. Pick $\varphi(z)=|z|^2$ on a bounded $\Omega$. Then $i\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ is the standard Hermitian metric; the estimate gives a global solution $u$ with explicit $L^2$ control.
  • Ohsawa–Takegoshi extension theorem. A holomorphic function on a complex submanifold $V\subset\Omega$ extends to a holomorphic function on all of $\Omega$ with $L^2$ bounds. The proof solves a $\bar\partial$-equation for the trivial extension's defect, with a weight tailored to vanish to second order along $V$.
  • Approximation theorems. Polynomials are dense in the holomorphic functions on a pseudoconvex domain in suitable weighted $L^2$ spaces — proved by writing a target $f$ as $f = (\text{partial sum}) + \bar\partial(\text{thing})^{-1}\bar\partial(\text{remainder})$, with weights tuned to the polynomial's degree.

The proof of Hörmander's estimate is functional-analytic at heart: it constructs the minimal-norm solution by orthogonal projection in a weighted $L^2$ space, and the strict plurisubharmonicity of the weight bounds the projection's norm via an integration-by-parts identity (the Bochner–Kodaira–Nakano identity in disguise). The same scheme generalizes to bundles, complex manifolds, and the boundary $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem (Kohn–Hörmander).

4. Domains of holomorphy and Levi pseudoconvexity

In one variable, every open set $\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ admits a holomorphic function that does not extend across any boundary point — most spectacularly, the Riemann mapping theorem says simply connected proper open subsets of $\mathbb{C}$ are conformal copies of the disk, on which one can pole or cut. The phenomenon is so universal that it has no name. In $\mathbb{C}^n$ for $n\ge 2$, by contrast, Hartogs's theorem already shows that most domains have hidden extensions. The domains that don't are special.

Definition. $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ is a domain of holomorphy if there exists a holomorphic $f$ on $\Omega$ with no holomorphic extension to any larger connected open set. Equivalently — by the work of Cartan–Thullen — $\Omega$ is holomorphically convex: for every compact $K\subset\Omega$ the holomorphic hull $$\widehat K_\Omega \;=\; \bigl\{\,z\in\Omega : |f(z)|\le\sup_K|f|\;\text{for all $f\in\mathcal{O}(\Omega)$}\,\bigr\}$$ is itself compact in $\Omega$.

The intrinsic geometric characterization came later: a domain of holomorphy is exactly a pseudoconvex domain. There are several equivalent formulations of pseudoconvexity; for a domain with smooth boundary the cleanest is via the Levi form.

Levi pseudoconvex (smooth case). A bounded $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ with $C^2$ boundary defined by $\rho<0$ is Levi pseudoconvex if at every $p\in\partial\Omega$ the Levi form (i.e. $\partial\bar\partial\rho|_p$ restricted to $T^{1,0}_p\partial\Omega$) is positive semidefinite. Strictly Levi pseudoconvex means strictly positive at every boundary point. The condition is intrinsic: it does not depend on the choice of defining function.

Without smoothness, pseudoconvexity is the existence of a continuous PSH exhaustion: a continuous PSH function $\rho\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ with $\rho\to+\infty$ at $\partial\Omega$. The boundary version (Levi) and interior version (PSH exhaustion) agree on smooth domains, and both assertions are equivalent to holomorphic convexity.

Levi's problem (Oka 1942, Bremermann–Norguet 1954, Grauert). $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ is a domain of holomorphy if and only if it is pseudoconvex.

This is the SCV analog of "every simply connected proper open subset of $\mathbb{C}$ is biholomorphic to the disk." It says the geometric shape of the boundary — the sign of the Levi form — determines whether $\Omega$ can host a maximally extended holomorphic function. Levi gave the necessary condition (a domain of holomorphy is pseudoconvex) in 1910; Oka, Bremermann, and Norguet proved sufficiency three decades later, and Grauert later generalized to complex manifolds.

Strict pseudoconvexity and the Diederich–Fornæss exponent

Stronger than pseudoconvex is strictly pseudoconvex: the Levi form is strictly positive at every boundary point. Strictly pseudoconvex domains have the most regular $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem, and the Bergman / Szegő kernels admit explicit asymptotic expansions (Fefferman, Boutet de Monvel–Sjöstrand). Between strict and ordinary pseudoconvex sits a useful quantitative refinement.

Diederich–Fornæss (1977). A bounded smooth pseudoconvex domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ admits a defining function $r$ with $-(-r)^\eta$ plurisubharmonic for some $\eta\in(0,1]$. The supremum of allowable exponents is the Diederich–Fornæss exponent $\eta(\Omega)\in(0,1]$.

The exponent measures how robustly pseudoconvex $\Omega$ is. Strictly pseudoconvex domains have $\eta(\Omega)=1$. Worm domains (Diederich–Fornæss's original counterexample) have $\eta$ strictly less than $1$ — the obstruction reflects a winding annular boundary geometry that defeats the simplest weighted $L^2$ estimates and breaks the global regularity of the $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem.

5. Stein manifolds

Pseudoconvex open subsets of $\mathbb{C}^n$ are the standard environment for SCV. To globalize — to do SCV on a complex manifold $X$ rather than a chart of $\mathbb{C}^n$ — one isolates the manifolds whose function theory is as rich as it is on a domain of holomorphy. These are the Stein manifolds.

Definition (Stein 1951). A complex manifold $X$ is Stein if it satisfies all three:
  1. Holomorphically convex. For every compact $K\subset X$ the holomorphic hull $\widehat K_X$ is compact.
  2. Holomorphically separable. For every $p\ne q$ there exists $f\in\mathcal{O}(X)$ with $f(p)\ne f(q)$.
  3. Holomorphic local coordinates. Around every point there are $f_1,\ldots,f_n\in\mathcal{O}(X)$ giving a chart (i.e. a local biholomorphism onto its image).
Equivalently: $X$ admits a smooth strictly plurisubharmonic exhaustion $\rho\colon X\to\mathbb{R}$ (Grauert). Equivalently: $X$ is biholomorphic to a closed complex submanifold of some $\mathbb{C}^N$ (Remmert–Bishop–Narasimhan embedding).

Stein is the SCV analog of "affine"

The slogan is that Stein manifolds are to complex manifolds what affine varieties are to schemes. Concretely:

  • Affine variety $\mathrm{Spec}(R) \rightsquigarrow $ closed in some $\mathbb{A}^N$. Stein manifold $X \rightsquigarrow$ closed in some $\mathbb{C}^N$.
  • Affine: regular functions separate points and give local coordinates. Stein: holomorphic functions do the same.
  • Affine: cohomology of quasi-coherent sheaves vanishes in positive degree (Serre). Stein: cohomology of coherent analytic sheaves vanishes in positive degree (Cartan's Theorem B).

Examples of Stein manifolds you already know: $\mathbb{C}^n$ itself, every domain of holomorphy in $\mathbb{C}^n$ (so every pseudoconvex domain), every open Riemann surface (Behnke–Stein 1949), the complement of a hypersurface in any Stein manifold. Non-examples: $\mathbb{CP}^n$ (compact, hence has only constant holomorphic functions — fails separability), any compact complex manifold of positive dimension, Hopf surfaces.

Cartan's Theorems A and B (1953). Let $X$ be a Stein manifold and $\mathcal{F}$ a coherent analytic sheaf on $X$.
Theorem A: The global sections $H^0(X,\mathcal{F})$ generate every stalk $\mathcal{F}_x$ as an $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$-module.
Theorem B: $H^q(X,\mathcal{F}) = 0$ for all $q\ge 1$.

Theorem B is the cohomological miracle that makes Stein manifolds tractable: every short exact sequence of coherent sheaves becomes a short exact sequence of global sections, every Čech cohomology computation collapses, and every analytic problem expressible as a sheaf-cohomology obstruction is automatically solvable on a Stein manifold. Theorem A is the local-to-global "you can find global sections through a given stalk vector" statement; together with Theorem B it is the analytic counterpart to Serre's vanishing theorem on affine schemes.

6. Cousin problems and applications

The historical entry point to sheaf cohomology in SCV is via the two Cousin problems, posed by Pierre Cousin in 1895. They are the SCV analogs of Mittag-Leffler and Weierstrass: prescribe local meromorphic / divisor data on overlaps and ask if it can be globalized.

The first Cousin problem (additive, Mittag-Leffler-style)

Given an open cover $\{U_i\}$ of $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^n$ and meromorphic functions $f_i$ on $U_i$ such that the differences $f_i - f_j$ are holomorphic on $U_i\cap U_j$, find a meromorphic $f$ on $\Omega$ with $f - f_i$ holomorphic on each $U_i$. The differences $f_i - f_j\in\mathcal{O}(U_i\cap U_j)$ form a Čech 1-cocycle, and the existence of $f$ is equivalent to writing this cocycle as a coboundary $f_i - f_j = g_i - g_j$ — that is, equivalent to vanishing in $H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O})$.

First Cousin theorem. The first Cousin problem on $\Omega$ is solvable for every choice of cover and local data $\Leftrightarrow H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O}) = 0$. By Cartan's Theorem B, this holds whenever $\Omega$ is Stein.

In one variable, $H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O}) = 0$ for every open $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}$ (use Mittag-Leffler), so the first Cousin problem is always solvable on planar domains — that is exactly the classical Mittag-Leffler theorem prescribing principal parts. In several variables, the obstruction is real on non-Stein domains: there exist domains $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^2$ with $H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O})\ne 0$ where the first Cousin problem can fail.

The second Cousin problem (multiplicative, Weierstrass-style)

Given a cover $\{U_i\}$ and meromorphic $f_i\not\equiv 0$ on $U_i$ such that $f_i / f_j$ is nowhere-zero holomorphic on $U_i\cap U_j$, find a meromorphic $f$ on $\Omega$ with $f / f_i$ nowhere-zero holomorphic on each $U_i$. This is divisor data: the $f_i$ prescribe local zero / pole sets that match on overlaps, and $f$ realizes this divisor globally.

The transition functions $f_i/f_j\in\mathcal{O}^\times(U_i\cap U_j)$ form a Čech 1-cocycle for $\mathcal{O}^\times$, the sheaf of nowhere-zero holomorphic functions. Solvability is equivalent to vanishing in $H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O}^\times) = \mathrm{Pic}(\Omega)$ — the Picard group of holomorphic line bundles. The exponential exact sequence

$$0\to \mathbb{Z}\to\mathcal{O}\xrightarrow{\exp 2\pi i\,\cdot}\mathcal{O}^\times \to 0$$

is the bridge: its long exact cohomology sequence gives

$$H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O})\to H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O}^\times)\to H^2(\Omega,\mathbb{Z})\to H^2(\Omega,\mathcal{O}).$$

If $\Omega$ is Stein, $H^k(\Omega,\mathcal{O}) = 0$ for $k\ge 1$ by Cartan B, so

$$\mathrm{Pic}(\Omega) \;\cong\; H^2(\Omega,\mathbb{Z}).$$
Second Cousin theorem (Stein). On a Stein manifold $\Omega$, the second Cousin problem is solvable iff the divisor's Chern class in $H^2(\Omega,\mathbb{Z})$ vanishes. In particular, on a Stein manifold with $H^2(\Omega,\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ — for instance on every $\mathbb{C}^n$, polydisk, or contractible Stein manifold — every second Cousin problem is solvable.

Why the second Cousin can fail

The classic obstruction lives on a non-Stein domain. Take $\Omega = \mathbb{C}^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ — not Stein, since one can show $H^1(\Omega,\mathcal{O})\ne 0$ in dimension $2$ (actually the punctured $\mathbb{C}^2$ is Stein for $n\ge 2$ by Hartogs, but the Hopf bundle on $\mathbb{C}^2\setminus\{0\}$ as a model survives transfer). A more honest example: the Hopf surface $X = (\mathbb{C}^2\setminus\{0\})/\langle (z,w)\sim(2z,2w)\rangle$ is a non-Stein compact complex surface; its second Cousin problem can fail because $H^2(X,\mathbb{Z}) = 0$ is forced but the exponential exact sequence yields a non-zero $H^1(X,\mathcal{O}^\times) = \mathrm{Pic}(X)\ne 0$ via the non-vanishing $H^1(X,\mathcal{O})$ piece — divisors exist that are not Chern-class-zero realisable.

In one variable, the second Cousin theorem reduces to Weierstrass: $H^2$ of any planar domain vanishes, so every second Cousin problem on $\mathbb{C}$ is solvable. The genuine multivariable obstruction is what makes Stein-and-not-just-pseudoconvex worth naming.

The bigger picture

The two Cousin problems are the historical seeds of sheaf cohomology in SCV. Cartan's Theorem B is the comprehensive answer: on a Stein manifold, every additive Čech obstruction vanishes ($H^q(\Omega,\mathcal{O})=0$), so every Cousin-flavored problem is solvable up to the multiplicative obstruction in $H^2(\Omega,\mathbb{Z})$, which is purely topological. The leap from "solve $\bar\partial$" to "compute coherent sheaf cohomology" — Hörmander estimates implicitly — is the tactical reason SCV connects to algebraic geometry (GAGA, Stein factorizations) and complex geometric topology (the Oka principle).

Connections

Several complex variables sits at the intersection of analysis, geometry, and algebraic geometry. The $\bar\partial$-Neumann theory connects to elliptic PDE and microlocal analysis (Kohn–Hörmander, Catlin's subelliptic estimates); the Stein theory feeds directly into algebraic geometry via Serre's GAGA and the Stein factorization of proper morphisms; the Oka principle (homotopy classes of holomorphic and continuous maps from a Stein manifold agree) is being upgraded by Forstnerič and collaborators into "Oka theory," a parallel to model categories for holomorphic geometry. Open frontiers include the regularity of the $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem on weakly pseudoconvex domains (Christ's worm-domain irregularity), the analytic side of mirror symmetry (SYZ fibrations), and the Cauchy–Riemann embedding problem for abstract CR manifolds.

  • Advanced complex analysis — several complex variables — the prerequisite overview that sets up Hartogs and the $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem at one-page depth.
  • Kähler geometry — Hermitian metric and the Kähler form — Stein manifolds carry plurisubharmonic exhaustions; Kähler manifolds carry closed positive $(1,1)$-forms. The shared tool $i\partial\bar\partial$ is the same in both.
  • Sheaf cohomology — Čech cohomology — the Cousin problems are Čech cocycles in $\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathcal{O}^\times$; Cartan B is the SCV analog of Serre's affine vanishing theorem.
  • Harmonic functions — subharmonic functions and Perron's method — plurisubharmonic functions are the multivariable continuation of the subharmonic theory, with the Levi form replacing the Laplacian.
  • Microlocal analysis — wavefront set — the boundary regularity of the $\bar\partial$-Neumann problem is governed by microlocal estimates on the cotangent bundle of $\partial\Omega$.
  • Moduli spaces — the analytic theory of Teichmüller space, complex manifold deformations, and Hodge structures all live downstream of SCV via Stein-fibred constructions.
