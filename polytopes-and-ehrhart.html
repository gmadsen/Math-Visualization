Polytopes and Ehrhart theory

Count the lattice points inside an integer polytope as you inflate it, and a single polynomial falls out — its degree the dimension, its top coefficient the volume — connecting convex geometry, the Euler relation, and generating functions in one stroke.

1. Convex polytopes: V- and H-representations

A convex polytope in $\mathbb{R}^d$ can be described in two dual ways. The V-representation presents it as the convex hull of a finite set of points, $P = \mathrm{conv}(v_1, \ldots, v_n) = \{\sum_i \lambda_i v_i : \lambda_i \ge 0,\ \sum_i \lambda_i = 1\}$. The H-representation presents the same set as a bounded intersection of half-spaces, $P = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^d : Ax \le b\}$, a finite list of linear inequalities.

Minkowski–Weyl theorem. A subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$ is the convex hull of finitely many points if and only if it is a bounded intersection of finitely many half-spaces.

The boundedness is the whole content of the word polytope: drop it and an intersection of half-spaces is a polyhedron, possibly unbounded (a cone, a slab, a half-space). Minkowski–Weyl is what lets us flip freely between “here are my corners” and “here are my constraints.”

Faces and the face lattice

A face of $P$ is the intersection $P \cap H$ with a supporting hyperplane $H$ — a hyperplane that touches $P$ with all of $P$ on one side — together with the two improper faces $\varnothing$ and $P$ itself. Faces of dimension $0$, $1$, and $d-1$ are the vertices, edges, and facets. Ordered by inclusion, the faces form the face lattice: a graded lattice with bottom $\varnothing$ and top $P$, where meet is intersection and join is the smallest face containing both.

Worked mini-example. The unit square $[0,1]^2$ has V-representation $\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(0,1)\}$ and H-representation $\{0 \le x \le 1,\ 0 \le y \le 1\}$ — four vertices, four facets (here edges). Its face lattice has $1$ empty face, $4$ vertices, $4$ edges, and the square: $1+4+4+1 = 10$ faces, the lattice $\hat 0 < \text{vertices} < \text{edges} < \hat 1$.

2. Faces & the Euler relation

Collect the face counts of a polytope by dimension into the f-vector $(f_0, f_1, \ldots, f_{d-1})$, where $f_i$ is the number of $i$-dimensional faces. For a $3$-polytope this is $(f_0, f_1, f_2) = (V, E, F)$ — vertices, edges, $2$-faces. These numbers are not free: they satisfy one universal linear relation.

Euler–Poincaré relation. For a convex $d$-polytope, $\displaystyle \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} (-1)^i f_i = 1 - (-1)^d.$

For $d = 3$ the right side is $1 - (-1)^3 = 2$, so $f_0 - f_1 + f_2 = 2$, i.e. the familiar $V - E + F = 2$. For $d = 2$ it reads $f_0 - f_1 = 0$, i.e. a polygon has as many edges as vertices. The relation is the polytopal shadow of the fact that the boundary sphere $\partial P \cong S^{d-1}$ has Euler characteristic $1 - (-1)^d$.

Dehn–Sommerville

For simplicial polytopes — every facet a simplex — the single Euler relation expands into a whole family of linear relations among the $f_i$, the Dehn–Sommerville equations, of which Euler is the first. They are what make the $g$-theorem's characterization of simplicial f-vectors possible; we mention them here and pursue counting in the dilation direction instead.

Worked mini-example. The five Platonic solids all satisfy $V - E + F = 2$: tetrahedron $(4,6,4)$ gives $4-6+4=2$; cube $(8,12,6)$ gives $8-12+6=2$; octahedron $(6,12,8)$ gives $6-12+8=2$; dodecahedron $(20,30,12)$ gives $20-30+12=2$; icosahedron $(12,30,20)$ gives $12-30+20=2$.

3. Pick's theorem

Restrict attention to lattice polygons — polygons whose vertices lie at integer points $\mathbb{Z}^2$. For these, the area is determined entirely by counting lattice points, with no integration at all.

Pick's theorem (1899). A simple lattice polygon with $I$ interior lattice points and $B$ boundary lattice points has area $\displaystyle \mathrm{Area} = I + \frac{B}{2} - 1.$

The intuition: each interior point “owns” a full unit of area, each boundary point owns roughly half, and the $-1$ corrects for the polygon being a single closed region (an Euler-characteristic term). The clean proof triangulates the polygon into primitive lattice triangles — triangles with no lattice points except their three vertices — each of which has area exactly $\tfrac12$, and then counts.

Pick's theorem is exactly the two-dimensional case of Ehrhart theory: as we will see in §4, the lattice-point count of a dilated polygon is a quadratic whose coefficients are the area, half the boundary, and $1$.

Worked mini-example. Take the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(4,0)$, $(0,3)$. The number of boundary lattice points an edge with integer direction vector $(p,q)$ contributes is $\gcd(|p|,|q|)$ (counting one endpoint per edge), so $B = \gcd(4,0) + \gcd(0,3) + \gcd(4,3) = 4 + 3 + 1 = 8$. A direct sweep finds $I = 3$ interior points. Pick then gives $\mathrm{Area} = I + B/2 - 1 = 3 + 4 - 1 = 6$, exactly the shoelace area $\tfrac12 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 = 6.$ ✓

4. The Ehrhart polynomial

Pick's theorem counts one polygon. Ehrhart's insight is to count the whole family of dilates at once. For a lattice polytope $P \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ (vertices in $\mathbb{Z}^d$) and an integer $t \ge 1$, write $tP = \{t\,x : x \in P\}$ for the $t$-fold dilate, and define the Ehrhart function

$L_P(t) \;=\; \#\bigl(tP \cap \mathbb{Z}^d\bigr),$

the number of lattice points in the inflated copy.

Ehrhart's theorem (1962). $L_P(t)$ agrees, for all integers $t \ge 0$, with a polynomial in $t$ of degree $d = \dim P$. Moreover

  • the leading coefficient is $\mathrm{vol}(P)$ (lattice points fill out the volume as $t \to \infty$);
  • the constant term is $L_P(0) = 1$ (the dilate $0\cdot P$ is the single point $\{0\}$ counted once);
  • for $d = 2$, the coefficient of $t^{d-1} = t$ is $\tfrac12$ of the boundary lattice-length — exactly Pick's $B/2$.
Worked mini-examples. The unit square $[0,1]^2$ dilates to $[0,t]^2$, whose lattice points form a $(t+1)\times(t+1)$ grid, so $L(t) = (t+1)^2 = t^2 + 2t + 1$ — leading coefficient $1 = \mathrm{vol}$, constant $1$, middle coefficient $2 = \tfrac12\cdot 4$ (half the boundary). The standard triangle $\mathrm{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}$ has $L(t) = \binom{t+2}{2} = \tfrac{(t+1)(t+2)}{2} = \tfrac12 t^2 + \tfrac32 t + 1$ — leading coefficient $\tfrac12 = \mathrm{vol}$, constant $1$.

5. Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity

The Ehrhart polynomial $L_P(t)$ is defined by a counting problem for $t \ge 1$, but as a polynomial it can be evaluated anywhere — including at negative integers. Macdonald discovered that those values are not nonsense: they count the lattice points strictly inside the dilate.

Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity. For a lattice $d$-polytope $P$ with interior $P^\circ$, $\displaystyle (-1)^d\, L_P(-t) \;=\; L_{P^\circ}(t) \;=\; \#\bigl(\mathrm{int}(tP) \cap \mathbb{Z}^d\bigr) \quad (t \ge 1).$

One polynomial thus carries two enumerations: plug in positive $t$ to count all lattice points of $tP$, and plug in negative $t$ (up to the sign $(-1)^d$) to count only the interior ones. The boundary contribution flips between the two.

Worked mini-example. For the unit square, $L(t) = (t+1)^2$ and $d = 2$. Reciprocity gives the interior count as $(-1)^2 L(-t) = (-t+1)^2 = (t-1)^2$. Check directly: the interior lattice points of $[0,t]^2$ are $\{1, \ldots, t-1\}^2$, which is $(t-1)^2$ points. At $t = 3$ that is $4$ interior points; at $t = 1$ it is $0$, correctly saying the unit square itself has no interior lattice points. For the standard triangle, $L(t) = \tfrac{(t+1)(t+2)}{2}$, so the interior count is $L(-t) = \tfrac{(1-t)(2-t)}{2} = \tfrac{(t-1)(t-2)}{2}$, vanishing at $t = 1, 2$ as it must.

6. The Ehrhart series & h*-vector

The cleanest packaging of the entire sequence $L_P(0), L_P(1), L_P(2), \ldots$ is a generating function, the Ehrhart series

$\displaystyle \mathrm{Ehr}_P(z) \;=\; \sum_{t \ge 0} L_P(t)\, z^t.$

Because $L_P$ is a polynomial of degree $d$, this series is a rational function with denominator $(1-z)^{d+1}$. Writing the numerator out gives the central object of the subject.

Stanley's theorem. $\displaystyle \mathrm{Ehr}_P(z) = \frac{h^*(z)}{(1-z)^{d+1}}, \qquad h^*(z) = h^*_0 + h^*_1 z + \cdots + h^*_d z^d,$ where the $h^*$-vector $(h^*_0, \ldots, h^*_d)$ consists of nonnegative integers.

The nonnegativity is deep — it is not visible from the polynomial $L_P$ and was Stanley's theorem via commutative algebra (the $h^*$-vector is the Hilbert function of a Cohen–Macaulay graded ring). Two normalizations are forced: $h^*_0 = 1$ always (from $L_P(0) = 1$), and

$\displaystyle \sum_{i=0}^{d} h^*_i \;=\; h^*(1) \;=\; d!\,\mathrm{vol}(P),$

the normalized volume — always a positive integer for a lattice polytope.

Worked mini-examples. Unit square: $L(t) = (t+1)^2$, and $\sum_t (t+1)^2 z^t = \tfrac{1+z}{(1-z)^3}$, so $d = 2$, $h^* = (1,1,0)$, and $\sum h^*_i = 2 = 2!\cdot 1$. Standard triangle: $L(t) = \tfrac{(t+1)(t+2)}{2}$, and $\mathrm{Ehr}(z) = \tfrac{1}{(1-z)^3}$, so $h^* = (1,0,0)$ and $\sum h^*_i = 1 = 2!\cdot\tfrac12$. Both numerators have nonnegative coefficients, as Stanley guarantees.

Connections

Ehrhart theory sits where convex geometry, commutative algebra, and combinatorics meet. The frontier is alive: classifying the $h^*$-vectors that actually arise (which integer sequences are Ehrhart $h^*$-vectors?) is open, the question of when a lattice polytope is Gorenstein or has a unimodal $h^*$-vector drives current research, and reflexive polytopes are the combinatorial backbone of mirror symmetry for Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces. A beginner is often surprised that counting integer points — the most elementary thing imaginable — should encode volume, the Euler characteristic, and the Hilbert function of a ring all at once.

  • Combinatorial optimization — polyhedral combinatorics — the V/H duality and integer hulls of §1 are the language of linear and integer programming; total unimodularity is exactly when the LP relaxation has integral vertices.
  • Graph theory — planarity & Euler's formula — the $V - E + F = 2$ of §2 is the same relation that governs planar graphs, viewed through the boundary $2$-sphere of a $3$-polytope.
  • Enumerative combinatorics — generating functions — the Ehrhart series of §6 is a generating function whose rational form $(1-z)^{-(d+1)}$ encodes that $L_P$ is a polynomial of degree $d$.
  • Order theory & lattices — meet & join — the face lattice of a polytope is a graded lattice, ordered by inclusion, with the empty face at bottom and $P$ at top.
