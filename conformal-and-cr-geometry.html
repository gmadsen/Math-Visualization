Conformal & CR geometry

Riemannian geometry, but you only get to keep angles. The story splits in two: in dimension $n\ge 3$ the conformal class $[g] = \{e^{2u}g\}$ produces the Yamabe equation $L_g u = \lambda u^{(n+2)/(n-2)}$, a critical-exponent PDE with the wrong embedding for compactness — solved by Trudinger, Aubin, and Schoen across 24 years. On odd-dimensional cousins — real hypersurfaces in $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ — the CR analog runs in parallel: contact 1-forms in place of metrics, the sublaplacian in place of the Laplacian, the Heisenberg group in place of Euclidean space, and the Levi form in place of the second fundamental form.

1. Conformal class of a Riemannian metric

Fix a smooth manifold $M^n$ with a Riemannian metric $g$. The conformal class of $g$ is

$$[g] \;=\; \bigl\{\,\tilde g \;:\; \tilde g = e^{2u}\,g \;\;\text{for some } u\in C^\infty(M)\,\bigr\}.$$

Geometrically, two metrics in the same conformal class agree on angles but disagree on lengths. At a point $p$, the rescaling $g_p \to e^{2u(p)} g_p$ is just a positive scalar multiple of an inner product — so cosines $g(X,Y)/\sqrt{g(X,X)g(Y,Y)}$ survive unchanged while individual norms $\sqrt{g(X,X)}$ multiply by $e^{u(p)}$.

Why exponentiate? Writing $\tilde g = e^{2u}g$ rather than $\tilde g = \omega^2 g$ (with $\omega>0$) is the convention that makes the curvature transformation laws look cleanest. The factor of $2$ aligns with the convention that conformal factors on a Hermitian inner product correspond to logarithms with the right sign for the heat-equation / sub-elliptic estimates downstream.

How curvature transforms

The Levi-Civita connection is metric-compatible and torsion-free, but it is not conformally invariant. Under $\tilde g = e^{2u}g$, the connection picks up explicit correction terms in $du$:

$$\tilde\nabla_X Y \;=\; \nabla_X Y + (Xu)Y + (Yu)X - g(X,Y)\,\mathrm{grad}_g\, u.$$

Iterating this through the Riemann curvature tensor (a tedious but mechanical computation) produces the conformal transformation law for the scalar curvature in dimension $n\ge 3$:

$$R_{\tilde g} \;=\; e^{-2u}\Bigl(R_g \;-\; 2(n-1)\,\Delta_g u \;-\; (n-1)(n-2)\,|\nabla_g u|^2\Bigr).$$

Two observations. First, the $|\nabla u|^2$ term is genuinely nonlinear in $u$ — this is the entire reason conformal geometry is hard. Second, in dimension $n=2$ this $|\nabla u|^2$ coefficient vanishes ($n-2 = 0$), so the formula linearizes: $R_{\tilde g} = e^{-2u}(R_g - 2\Delta_g u)$. The 2-dimensional case is qualitatively easier — every closed surface admits a constant-curvature metric in its conformal class (uniformization).

Why dimension 2 is special

In two dimensions, the conformal class on a Riemann surface is the complex structure. The Riemann mapping theorem and uniformization both come from the rigidity of $[g]$ in dimension $2$ — a conformal class is the same as a holomorphic atlas. Above dimension $2$ the conformal class no longer determines a complex structure (there are too few conformal vector fields for the dimension count to work), and conformal geometry becomes its own discipline of nonlinear PDE for $u\in C^\infty(M)$.

2. The conformal Laplacian

The transformation law for scalar curvature has a $\Delta_g u$ term in it. The natural question is whether there is a single differential operator $L_g$ — depending on $g$ — that linearizes this transformation in a clean way. There is.

Definition. On $(M^n,g)$ with $n\ge 3$, the conformal Laplacian is $$L_g \;=\; -\Delta_g + \frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}\,R_g.$$ (Some conventions take $+\Delta_g$; the present sign aligns $L_g$ with the convention that $-\Delta$ is a positive operator — its principal symbol is $|\xi|^2$, not $-|\xi|^2$.)

The miracle is that $L_g$ is conformally covariant: under $\tilde g = e^{2u} g$ one has

$$L_{\tilde g}\bigl(\phi\bigr) \;=\; e^{-(n+2)u/2}\,L_g\!\bigl(e^{(n-2)u/2}\,\phi\bigr).$$

The pair of weights $(n-2)/2$ on the input and $(n+2)/2$ on the output is calibrated exactly. Their sum is $n$ (the dimension), their difference is $2$ (the order of $L_g$). Both facts are reflections of a deeper structural pattern: conformally covariant operators of order $2k$ always have input/output weights $(n-2k)/2$ and $(n+2k)/2$. The Paneitz operator (order $4$, §4) uses weights $(n-4)/2$ and $(n+4)/2$; the GJMS operators of order $2k$ use $(n-2k)/2$ and $(n+2k)/2$.

Conformal change of metric to constant scalar curvature

The conformal-covariance identity has a striking practical use. Write $\tilde g = u^{4/(n-2)}g$ for some $u>0$, which is the same as $e^{2v}g$ with $v = \tfrac{2}{n-2}\log u$. Apply $L_{\tilde g}$ to the constant function $1$:

$$L_{\tilde g}(1) \;=\; \frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}\,R_{\tilde g}.$$

By conformal covariance, $L_{\tilde g}(1) = u^{-(n+2)/(n-2)}\,L_g(u)$. So requiring $R_{\tilde g}\equiv\mathrm{const}=\tilde R$ in the new conformal metric becomes the equation

$$L_g\, u \;=\; \frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}\,\tilde R\cdot u^{(n+2)/(n-2)}.$$

This is the Yamabe equation — a single semilinear elliptic PDE for the conformal factor $u$. The right-hand side has the critical exponent $(n+2)/(n-2) = 2^* - 1$, where $2^* = 2n/(n-2)$ is the critical Sobolev exponent. That criticality is what makes Yamabe a celebrated test case for nonlinear elliptic PDE.

3. The Yamabe problem and the Yamabe equation

Yamabe (1960) asked the question that names the field:

The Yamabe problem. Given a closed Riemannian manifold $(M^n,g)$ with $n\ge 3$, does there exist a metric $\tilde g\in[g]$ in the same conformal class with constant scalar curvature $R_{\tilde g}\equiv\mathrm{const}$?

The previous section reduced this to the existence of a positive smooth solution $u\in C^\infty(M)$ to the Yamabe equation

$$L_g\, u \;=\; -\Delta_g u + \frac{n-2}{4(n-1)}\,R_g\, u \;=\; \lambda\, u^{(n+2)/(n-2)},$$

where $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ is the (a priori unknown) constant. The equation has a variational formulation: solutions are critical points of the Yamabe functional

$$Y_g(u) \;=\; \frac{\int_M u\,L_g u\,dV_g}{\Bigl(\int_M u^{2^*}\,dV_g\Bigr)^{2/2^*}}\,, \qquad 2^* = \frac{2n}{n-2}.$$

This functional is scale-invariant under $u\mapsto cu$, and its infimum over positive smooth functions (the Yamabe invariant $Y(M,[g])$) is a conformal invariant of $(M,[g])$.

Why this is hard

The Yamabe equation is a critical-exponent elliptic PDE. The Sobolev embedding $H^1(M)\hookrightarrow L^{2^*}(M)$ is continuous but not compact at the borderline exponent $2^*$. A minimizing sequence for $Y_g$ in $H^1$ can converge weakly to $u_\infty$ but the $L^{2^*}$-norm can concentrate — bubbles of mass can escape to a point. Whether the limiting $u_\infty$ is itself a solution depends on whether bubble formation is ruled out.

Yamabe — the four-step program

1

The four-step program

The Trudinger–Aubin–Schoen resolution proceeds in four logical steps:

  1. Subcritical compactness. Replace the exponent $2^*$ with $p<2^*$. The Sobolev embedding $H^1\hookrightarrow L^p$ is compact, so a minimizer $u_p$ of the perturbed functional exists by the direct method.
  2. The Yamabe inequality. Define the Yamabe constant $Y(M,[g]) = \inf Y_g(u)$ over positive smooth $u$. Aubin's inequality says $Y(M,[g]) \le Y(S^n) = \tfrac{n(n-2)}{4}\omega_n^{2/n}$, with equality iff $(M,[g]) = (S^n,[g_{\mathrm{round}}])$. If strict, taking $p\to 2^*$ in step 1 produces an honest Yamabe solution.
  3. Strict inequality via Aubin's test functions ($n\ge 6$, non-LCF). When $n\ge 6$ and the Weyl tensor $W_g$ is not identically zero, Aubin (1976) constructed explicit test functions (localized bubbles centered at a point where $|W|\ne 0$) showing $Y(M,[g]) < Y(S^n)$.
  4. Strict inequality via the positive mass theorem ($n\in\{3,4,5\}$ or LCF). In the remaining cases — small dimension or locally conformally flat — Schoen (1984) replaced Aubin's test function with the conformal Green's function and identified the strict inequality with the positive mass theorem of Schoen–Yau (which had just been proved in the GR context).
Slogan. Yamabe = subcritical compactness + Aubin's inequality + a strict-inequality argument that depends on the dimension and the locally-conformally-flat property. The deepest case (positive mass theorem) is the one most beautifully tied to general relativity.

4. Q-curvature and the Paneitz operator

The conformal Laplacian $L_g$ is the order-$2$ conformally covariant operator on $(M^n,g)$. Paneitz (1983) discovered its 4th-order sibling.

The Paneitz operator. On $(M^n,g)$ with $n\ge 5$, $$P_g \;=\; \Delta_g^2 \;+\; \delta\!\bigl((n-2)J_g\,g - 4\,\mathrm{Sch}_g\bigr)\,d \;+\; \frac{n-4}{2}\,Q_g,$$ where $\mathrm{Sch}_g = \tfrac{1}{n-2}\bigl(\mathrm{Ric}_g - J_g\,g\bigr)$ is the Schouten tensor (acting on 1-forms), $J_g = R_g/(2(n-1))$ is its trace (the normalized scalar curvature), and $Q_g$ is the Q-curvature of $g$.

$P_g$ is conformally covariant in exactly the same sense as $L_g$, but with weights $(n-4)/2$ and $(n+4)/2$:

$$P_{\tilde g}(\phi) \;=\; e^{-(n+4)u/2}\,P_g\!\bigl(e^{(n-4)u/2}\phi\bigr), \qquad \tilde g = e^{2u}g, \;\;n\ge 5.$$

The pattern repeats. The GJMS construction (Graham–Jenne–Mason–Sparling 1992) shows there is a conformally covariant operator $P_{2k}$ of order $2k$ for every $k\ge 1$ when $n\ge 2k+1$, with weights $(n-2k)/2$ and $(n+2k)/2$.

Dimension 4 is the critical dimension for Q-curvature

Just as Yamabe is hard in the critical dimension $n=2$ where the formula degenerates, Q-curvature has a critical dimension at $n=4$. When $n=4$, the prefactor $\tfrac{n-4}{2} = 0$ vanishes — so $Q$ no longer appears multiplicatively in $P_g$, but only additively in the transformation law:

$$Q_{\tilde g} \;=\; e^{-4u}\bigl(Q_g + P_g u\bigr), \qquad \tilde g = e^{2u}g, \;\;n = 4.$$

So $\int_M Q_g\,dV_g$ is a conformal invariant: integrating both sides against $dV_{\tilde g} = e^{4u}\,dV_g$ collapses the $e^{-4u}$ and the term $\int_M P_g u\,dV_g$ vanishes when $M$ is closed (integration by parts, $P_g$ is self-adjoint).

The Chern–Gauss–Bonnet identity in dimension 4

The integral $\int Q\,dV$ is more than a conformal invariant — it has a topological interpretation. On a closed 4-manifold:

$$\int_M Q_g\,dV_g \;+\; \frac{1}{4}\int_M |W_g|^2\,dV_g \;=\; 8\pi^2\,\chi(M),$$

where $W_g$ is the Weyl tensor (the conformally invariant piece of the Riemann curvature) and $\chi(M)$ is the Euler characteristic. The Weyl-tensor integral is itself a conformal invariant, so this is a relation between two conformal invariants and a topological invariant.

The Chern–Gauss–Bonnet formula in dimension 4 says $\chi(M) = \tfrac{1}{32\pi^2}\int_M (|W|^2 + 4Q) \,dV$ (in appropriate normalisations) — the same identity rearranged. Q-curvature is, in this sense, the conformally invariant part of the dimension-4 Gauss–Bonnet integrand.

5. CR structures and the Heisenberg model

Real hypersurfaces in $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ — boundaries of pseudoconvex domains, for instance — inherit a peculiar mixed structure. They have a real tangent bundle (so they admit real differentiation), but their tangent spaces also remember the complex structure of the ambient $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$. CR geometry abstracts that mixed structure.

Definition. A CR structure on a real $(2n+1)$-manifold $M$ is a pair $(H, J)$ where
  • $H\subset TM$ is a smooth real rank-$2n$ subbundle (the horizontal distribution);
  • $J\colon H\to H$ is a smooth bundle endomorphism with $J^2 = -\mathrm{id}_H$ (an almost-complex structure on $H$);
satisfying the integrability condition: $[H^{1,0}, H^{1,0}]\subset H^{1,0}\otimes\mathbb{C}$, where $H^{1,0}\subset H\otimes\mathbb{C}$ is the $+i$-eigenspace of $J$.

Equivalently, the integrability condition demands $[X-iJX, Y-iJY]$ is again in $H\otimes\mathbb{C}$ as a section of the $+i$-eigenspace, for all sections $X, Y$ of $H$. This is the CR analog of the integrability of an almost-complex structure (Newlander–Nirenberg), but with one important difference: it only applies to $H$ (not all of $TM$), so the conclusion is not that $M$ is a complex manifold. Indeed $M$ has odd real dimension and cannot be one.

Real hypersurfaces are the model examples

Let $M = \{\rho = 0\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ be a smooth real hypersurface with $d\rho\ne 0$ on $M$. The horizontal distribution is

$$H_p \;=\; T_pM \cap J_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}(T_pM),$$

where $J_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}$ is the standard complex structure on $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$. This $H_p$ has real dimension $2n$ — it is the largest $J_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}$-invariant subspace of $T_pM$. The complex structure $J$ on $H_p$ is just the restriction of $J_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}$. The integrability condition holds automatically.

The transverse direction is supplied by the characteristic vector field $T$ — the unique real vector field on $M$ that points in the imaginary normal direction. In coordinates, if $\rho$ is the defining function then $T = J_{\mathbb{C}^{n+1}}(\mathrm{grad}\,\rho)|_M$.

The Heisenberg group: the flat CR model

The Heisenberg group $\mathbb{H}^n = \mathbb{C}^n\times\mathbb{R}$ with multiplication

$$(z,t)\cdot(z',t') \;=\; (z+z',\; t+t'+2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\langle z,z'\rangle), \qquad \langle z,z'\rangle = \sum z_j\bar z'_j,$$

carries a canonical CR structure: the horizontal distribution $H$ is spanned by the left-invariant vector fields

$$X_j \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j} + 2y_j\frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \qquad Y_j \;=\; \frac{\partial}{\partial y_j} - 2x_j\frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \qquad j = 1,\ldots,n,$$

where $z_j = x_j + iy_j$, and $J$ sends $X_j\to Y_j$, $Y_j\to -X_j$. The characteristic direction is $T = \partial/\partial t$.

The Heisenberg group plays the role of Euclidean space in CR geometry: it is the model of zero Webster curvature, the tangent cone at any point of a CR manifold, and the homogeneous space for the natural dilation group $\delta_\lambda(z,t) = (\lambda z, \lambda^2 t)$.

6. The Levi form and strict pseudoconvexity

The intrinsic data of a CR structure $(H, J)$ does not — by itself — pick out a notion of positivity or convexity. To get that you choose a contact 1-form.

Definition. A contact 1-form on a CR manifold $(M^{2n+1}, H, J)$ is a real 1-form $\theta$ with $\ker\theta = H$ and $\theta\wedge(d\theta)^n\ne 0$ everywhere (the contact condition). The associated Levi form at $p\in M$ is the Hermitian form $$\mathcal{L}_\theta(X, \bar Y) \;=\; -i\,d\theta(X,\bar Y) \quad\text{for}\;\; X, Y \in H^{1,0}_p.$$

Two contact 1-forms on the same CR structure differ by a positive real function: $\tilde\theta = e^{2u}\theta$. The Levi form rescales accordingly: $\mathcal{L}_{\tilde\theta} = e^{2u}\mathcal{L}_\theta$. So the sign of $\mathcal{L}_\theta$ — its definiteness, signature, signature distribution — is independent of the choice of contact form. This is the CR analog of the conformal class on a Riemannian manifold: pseudoconvexity is a structural property of $(M, H, J)$, not of any particular $\theta$.

Levi form on a real hypersurface

When $M = \{\rho = 0\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$ has $\rho$ as a defining function, take $\theta = -\mathrm{Im}\,\partial\rho|_M$ as a contact 1-form (up to sign). Then the Levi form on $T^{1,0}M$ is exactly the restriction of the complex Hessian:

$$\mathcal{L}_\theta(X,\bar Y) \;=\; \sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial^2 \rho}{\partial z_j\partial\bar z_k}\,X_j\bar Y_k, \qquad X, Y\in T^{1,0}_pM.$$

So the Levi form on a real hypersurface is exactly what we used in §6 of Several complex variables: the complex Hessian of the defining function, restricted to the complex-tangential directions.

Signature changes the analysis

The most studied case is strictly pseudoconvex: $\mathcal{L}_\theta$ positive definite (signature $(n, 0)$ on $T^{1,0}$). The CR Yamabe problem and the embeddability of abstract CR manifolds into $\mathbb{C}^N$ are both best understood in this category. Levi-flat ($\mathcal{L}_\theta\equiv 0$) hypersurfaces foliate by complex hypersurfaces — they are the CR analog of integrable foliations. Indefinite Levi form (signature $(p,q)$ with $p,q\ge 1$) appears on boundaries of unbounded Reinhardt-style domains and connects to representation theory of indefinite unitary groups $U(p,q)$.

7. The Webster connection and the CR Yamabe problem

The Riemannian Yamabe story has four pieces: a conformally covariant operator (the conformal Laplacian $L_g$), an associated curvature (scalar curvature $R_g$), a model space (the round sphere $S^n$), and a critical-exponent PDE. The CR Yamabe story copies all four into pseudo-Hermitian geometry.

The Webster connection

A pseudo-Hermitian structure on a CR manifold is a choice of contact 1-form $\theta$ — equivalently, a choice within the CR conformal class. Webster (1978) proved the CR analog of the Levi-Civita uniqueness theorem:

Theorem (Webster). On a strictly pseudoconvex pseudo-Hermitian manifold $(M^{2n+1}, \theta)$, there is a unique linear connection $\nabla^W$ on $TM$ such that
  1. $\nabla^W$ is compatible with $H$ ($\nabla^W$ preserves the splitting $TM = H\oplus\mathbb{R}T$);
  2. $\nabla^W J = 0$ on $H$;
  3. $\nabla^W$ has prescribed torsion (the Tanaka-Webster torsion $\tau$, which captures the non-integrability of the contact distribution as a tensor).

The Webster connection is the CR analog of the Levi-Civita connection: it specializes the choice of $\theta$ (instead of the choice of metric) into a canonical differential calculus. The Webster scalar curvature $R^W$ is the analog of $R_g$.

The sublaplacian

The natural Laplacian in CR geometry is not the Riemannian one but the sublaplacian $\Delta_b$, built from the Webster connection's horizontal derivatives only:

$$\Delta_b u \;=\; \mathrm{tr}_{H}\bigl(\nabla^W d^bu\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^n (X_j^2 + Y_j^2)\,u \quad\text{(in Heisenberg coords)}.$$

Unlike $\Delta_g$, the sublaplacian is sub-elliptic: its principal symbol vanishes on the characteristic direction $T$. By Hörmander's bracket-generating theorem, however, $\Delta_b$ is hypoelliptic — solutions of $\Delta_b u = f$ are smooth wherever $f$ is.

The CR conformal Laplacian and the CR Yamabe equation

Jerison and Lee (1987) defined the CR conformal Laplacian

$$L_b \;=\; -\!\left(2 + \frac{2}{n}\right)\Delta_b \;+\; R^W,$$

and proved it is conformally covariant: under $\tilde\theta = e^{2u/n}\theta$,

$$L_{\tilde b}(\phi) \;=\; e^{-(n+1)u/n}\,L_b\!\bigl(e^{(n-1)u/n}\phi\bigr).$$

(Different sources use different normalizations; the structural fact is what matters: weight $(n-1)/n$ in, weight $(n+1)/n$ out, difference $2/n$ matching the order $2$ of $L_b$ up to the homogeneous-dimension rescaling.)

The CR Yamabe problem. On a closed strictly pseudoconvex CR manifold $(M^{2n+1}, H, J)$, does there exist a pseudo-Hermitian structure $\theta$ with constant Webster scalar curvature $R^W\equiv\mathrm{const}$?

By exactly the same computation as in the Riemannian case (write $\tilde\theta = u^{2/n}\theta$ and apply conformal covariance), this reduces to the CR Yamabe equation

$$L_b\, u \;=\; \lambda\, u^{(n+2)/n} \;=\; \lambda\, u^{(Q+2)/(Q-2)},$$

where $Q = 2n + 2$ is the homogeneous dimension of $\mathbb{H}^n$. The exponent $(Q+2)/(Q-2)$ takes the place of the Riemannian $(n+2)/(n-2)$. The reason: the Heisenberg dilation $\delta_\lambda(z,t) = (\lambda z, \lambda^2 t)$ weights $z$ with $1$ and $t$ with $2$, so the metric ball of radius $\lambda$ has volume $\sim\lambda^{2n+2} = \lambda^Q$.

Jerison–Lee's resolution

Jerison and Lee (1987-89) proved the CR Yamabe problem for all closed strictly pseudoconvex CR manifolds — the precise analog of Trudinger–Aubin–Schoen. The architecture is the same: subcritical compactness, a CR Yamabe inequality $Y_{\mathrm{CR}}(M) \le Y_{\mathrm{CR}}(S^{2n+1})$ with equality only on the CR sphere, and a strict-inequality argument using the Heisenberg group's invariant Green's function and a CR analog of the positive mass theorem.

The bigger picture. The conformal–CR dictionary is one of the cleanest analogies in modern geometry. Every theorem in Riemannian conformal geometry has a CR cousin — sometimes harder (the CR positive mass theorem is harder than the Riemannian one because the sublaplacian is sub-elliptic), sometimes nearly identical. The unifying perspective comes from parabolic geometry — both conformal and CR structures are examples of Cartan geometries modeled on homogeneous spaces $G/P$ where $P\subset G$ is a parabolic subgroup. The Fefferman-Graham ambient construction makes this connection precise: a CR manifold is the boundary at infinity of an ambient asymptotically hyperbolic Kähler manifold, and conformal geometry is the boundary at infinity of an asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein manifold.

Connections

Conformal and CR geometry sit at a busy intersection of analysis, differential geometry, and several complex variables. The Yamabe problem and its CR analog opened the door to a parallel theory of nonlinear elliptic PDE for higher-order conformally covariant operators (the GJMS family) — Q-curvature prescription on closed $4$-manifolds is one open frontier, and the analogous Q-prescription on CR manifolds in arbitrary dimension is still being worked out. The Fefferman–Graham ambient metric construction connects conformal geometry to asymptotically hyperbolic Einstein manifolds (the AdS/CFT correspondence in physics), and the parallel ambient-Kähler construction for CR manifolds underlies recent advances in the holomorphic geometry of strictly pseudoconvex domains. Three contemporary open problems: the full Yamabe problem on noncompact manifolds with prescribed asymptotics; the analytic embeddability of abstract three-dimensional CR manifolds (the only dimension where embeddability can fail); and the development of Q-curvature on metric measure spaces beyond the smooth-manifold setting.

  • Riemannian geometry — Ricci curvature and Einstein manifolds — Einstein metrics are critical points of the Hilbert action; in a fixed conformal class, the Yamabe problem produces representatives that are Einstein up to a constant scalar curvature constraint.
  • Several complex variables — domains of holomorphy and Levi pseudoconvexity — strict pseudoconvexity is the boundary condition that makes the CR Yamabe analog well-posed; the Levi form is the same object in both settings.
  • PDE — Lax–Milgram, elliptic regularity, embedding — the Yamabe equation is a critical-exponent semilinear elliptic PDE; the regularity bootstrap that produces smooth solutions from $H^1$ weak solutions runs through Sobolev embedding and elliptic regularity.
  • Sobolev spaces $W^{k,p}$ — the critical exponent $2^* = 2n/(n-2)$ for the Sobolev embedding is exactly the borderline that makes Yamabe hard; its lack of compactness drives the entire technical story.
  • Lie groups — the Heisenberg group is the CR-flat model and the simplest example of a non-abelian nilpotent Lie group; the Webster connection's invariance group is $SU(n+1,1)$ on the CR sphere.
  • Ricci flow — Yamabe flow $\partial_t g = -(R_g - \bar R)g$ is a conformal-class-preserving heat flow whose stationary points are the Yamabe metrics; convergence theory parallels Ricci flow's.
