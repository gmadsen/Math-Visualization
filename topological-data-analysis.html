Topological data analysis

Hand a computer a finite point cloud and ask what shape is this? TDA's answer: thicken every point into a ball of radius $\epsilon$, build the resulting simplicial complex, read off its homology, and watch how features are born and die as $\epsilon$ grows. The persistence diagram is stable, computable in polynomial time, and the basis of an entire applied-topology toolkit.

1. Point clouds & filtrations

The starting datum is a finite set $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$. We want to assign a shape to $X$ — a topological invariant that does not depend on a particular triangulation or smoothing. The move is to thicken: replace each point by a closed ball $B(x_i, \epsilon)$, study the union $X_\epsilon = \bigcup_i B(x_i,\epsilon)$, and let the radius $\epsilon$ vary.

By the nerve theorem, the homotopy type of $X_\epsilon$ is captured by an abstract simplicial complex. The Čech complex $\check C_\epsilon(X)$ has a $k$-simplex $\sigma = \{x_{i_0},\dots,x_{i_k}\}$ whenever $\bigcap_j B(x_{i_j},\epsilon) \neq \emptyset$. The Vietoris–Rips complex is the easier-to-compute cousin:

$\mathrm{VR}_\epsilon(X) \;=\; \{\sigma \subset X : \mathrm{diam}(\sigma) \le 2\epsilon\}.$

Rips uses pairwise distances only — no intersections of multiple balls — so it can be built from a distance matrix alone. The interleaving $\check C_\epsilon \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_\epsilon \subseteq \check C_{\sqrt{2}\,\epsilon}$ means the two have indistinguishable persistent homology up to a scale factor, which is why VR is the default in software.

As $\epsilon$ grows from $0$ to $\infty$, complexes nest:

$\mathrm{VR}_{\epsilon_0}(X) \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_{\epsilon_1}(X) \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathrm{VR}_{\epsilon_n}(X) = \Delta^{n-1}.$

This nested family is a filtration. At $\epsilon=0$ it is $n$ isolated points; at $\epsilon=\infty$ it is the full simplex on $X$, which is contractible. Everything interesting happens between.

Vietoris–Rips filtration of a noisy circle
slide $\epsilon$ to grow the balls; watch edges and triangles appear

Points are drawn at radius $\epsilon$. A pair of points is joined by an edge iff their distance is $\le 2\epsilon$; a triple becomes a filled triangle iff all three pairwise distances are $\le 2\epsilon$. The readout reports the number of vertices, edges, and triangles, plus the (rank-0, rank-1) Betti numbers $\beta_0, \beta_1$ computed by reduction. The "annulus" preset reveals a single loop ($\beta_1 = 1$) over a clear window of $\epsilon$.

The thickening scale $\epsilon$ is the new axis of analysis. Persistent homology, the centerpiece of TDA, asks: as we sweep $\epsilon$ from $0$ to $\infty$, what topological features (components, loops, voids) persist over wide ranges of $\epsilon$, and which flicker briefly before dying?

2. Simplicial homology — the bookkeeping

Persistence runs simplicial homology in parallel at every scale, so we need the basics. Fix a field $k$ (typically $k=\mathbb{F}_2$ in software). For each abstract simplicial complex $K$, let $C_n(K)$ be the $k$-vector space with basis the $n$-simplices. The boundary $\partial_n : C_n \to C_{n-1}$ sends an oriented simplex to the alternating sum of its codimension-$1$ faces:

$\partial_n[v_0,\dots,v_n] \;=\; \sum_{i=0}^{n} (-1)^i \, [v_0,\dots,\widehat{v_i},\dots,v_n].$

The composite $\partial_{n-1} \circ \partial_n = 0$, so the kernel and image nest: $\mathrm{im}\,\partial_{n+1} \subseteq \ker\partial_n \subseteq C_n$. The $n$-th homology is the quotient

$H_n(K; k) \;=\; \ker \partial_n / \mathrm{im}\, \partial_{n+1}.$

Cycles modulo boundaries. The dimension $\beta_n = \dim_k H_n$ is the $n$-th Betti number: $\beta_0$ counts connected components, $\beta_1$ counts independent $1$-dim holes, $\beta_2$ counts enclosed voids. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ the orientation signs vanish and the computation is row-reduction over $\mathbb{F}_2$ — fast, parallelisable, exactly what software wants.

Cycles vs. boundaries on the boundary of a triangle
click an edge to toggle it; see whether the chain is a cycle or a boundary

Three vertices, three edges. The current 1-chain $c$ is the formal $\mathbb{F}_2$-sum of selected edges. The readout shows $\partial c$ (the formal $\mathbb{F}_2$-sum of endpoints, each counted mod 2) and answers two questions: is $c$ a cycle ($\partial c = 0$)? Is $c$ a boundary (does the 2-simplex's boundary equal $c$)? Toggle the central 2-cell on/off to fill in or empty the triangle and see how homology changes.

Persistent homology will compute $H_n$ not for one complex but for the entire filtration $\{K_i\}$, tracking which cycles are born at which $\epsilon$ and which boundaries kill them later.

3. Persistent homology — birth, death, barcode

Fix a filtration $\emptyset = K_0 \subseteq K_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq K_n = K$. Applying $H_k(-; k)$ to every inclusion gives a sequence of vector spaces and linear maps:

$H_k(K_0) \;\xrightarrow{f_0}\; H_k(K_1) \;\xrightarrow{f_1}\; \cdots \;\xrightarrow{f_{n-1}}\; H_k(K_n).$

This is a persistence module: a sequence of vector spaces tied together by maps. The data we want from it: when does a homology class first appear, and when does it become trivial?

For each pair $i \le j$, define the persistent homology group

$H_k^{i,j} \;=\; \mathrm{im}\bigl( H_k(K_i) \to H_k(K_j) \bigr),$

the classes in $K_j$ already present back at scale $i$. A class is born at $i$ if it lies in $H_k(K_i)$ but not in the image from $H_k(K_{i-1})$; it dies entering $K_j$ if it maps to zero in $H_k(K_j)$ but is non-zero in $H_k(K_{j-1})$.

Pack the bookkeeping into the persistence diagram (Cohen-Steiner–Edelsbrunner–Harer 2007): a multiset of points $(b,d) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ with $b < d$, one per born-then-killed class, plus the diagonal $\{(t,t)\}$ counted with infinite multiplicity. Equivalently, the barcode: one horizontal bar from $b$ to $d$ per feature. Long bars = robust features (e.g. a genuine loop in the data); short bars near the diagonal = topological noise.

The map $X \mapsto \mathrm{Dgm}_k(X)$ from input filtration to persistence diagram is the central pipeline of TDA. The next two sections explain why it is well-behaved: it is stable under input perturbation (§4) and uniquely encoded as a barcode (§5).

4. Stability — small input perturbations cause small diagram perturbations

For TDA to be useful on real data, the persistence diagram must be robust to noise. The stability theorem of Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, and Harer (2007) supplies exactly that guarantee.

Compare two filtrations by tame functions $f, g : K \to \mathbb{R}$ on the same complex (think: distance to two nearby point clouds). Define the bottleneck distance between their persistence diagrams as

$W_\infty\bigl(\mathrm{Dgm}(f), \mathrm{Dgm}(g)\bigr) \;=\; \inf_{\gamma} \;\sup_{p} \; \|p - \gamma(p)\|_\infty,$

where $\gamma$ ranges over partial matchings between the two diagrams (with unmatched points sent to their nearest diagonal point). Then:

Stability theorem. $W_\infty\bigl(\mathrm{Dgm}(f), \mathrm{Dgm}(g)\bigr) \;\le\; \|f - g\|_\infty$.

Persistence is $1$-Lipschitz with respect to the $L^\infty$ norm on input filtrations. For point-cloud Rips filtrations, the same statement reads: the Hausdorff distance between two clouds bounds the bottleneck distance between their persistence diagrams. Noisy data $\Rightarrow$ noisy diagrams, but only as noisy as the data.

Stability proof — sketch in four slides
scrub to step through the setup / inclusion / interleaving / matching argument

A scrubber through the four key moves: (0) hypotheses and setup; (1) sublevel-set inclusions $\{f \le t\} \subseteq \{g \le t+\varepsilon\}$ where $\varepsilon = \|f-g\|_\infty$; (2) these inclusions induce an $\varepsilon$-interleaving of persistence modules; (3) the interleaving exhibits a matching of diagrams with $L^\infty$-cost $\le \varepsilon$, so $W_\infty(\mathrm{Dgm}(f),\mathrm{Dgm}(g)) \le \|f-g\|_\infty$.

Two practical consequences: (i) Hausdorff-close point clouds yield bottleneck-close diagrams, so persistence-based features are statistical; (ii) noise floor estimation: bars with $d - b$ smaller than the input noise can be discarded.

5. Structure theorem — barcodes are the complete invariant

What does a persistence module look like as an algebraic object? The classification is clean.

For each interval $[b, d) \subset \mathbb{R}$ (allowing $d = +\infty$), the interval module $\mathbb{I}[b,d)$ is the persistence module that assigns $k$ to every $t \in [b,d)$ and $0$ elsewhere, with all transition maps either the identity or zero. Pictorially, $\mathbb{I}[b,d)$ is a single bar from $b$ to $d$.

Structure theorem (Crawley-Boevey, Zomorodian–Carlsson). Every pointwise-finite-dimensional persistence module $M$ over a field decomposes uniquely (up to reordering) as a direct sum of interval modules: $$M \;\cong\; \bigoplus_{\alpha} \mathbb{I}[b_\alpha, d_\alpha).$$ The multiset $\{(b_\alpha, d_\alpha)\}$ is the persistence diagram of $M$.

The persistence diagram is therefore a complete invariant: two pointwise-finite persistence modules are isomorphic iff their diagrams are equal as multisets. No hidden algebraic data beyond the bars.

The proof goes via the category of finitely-presented graded modules over $k[t]$ — the same machinery as the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID. The algebraic punchline is that $k[t]$ is a PID, and the indecomposable summands of a finitely generated graded $k[t]$-module are exactly the interval modules.

The multi-parameter generalization is genuinely harder: a persistence module indexed by $\mathbb{R}^2$ (e.g. density + scale) does not decompose into rectangles — there is no analogous structure theorem in two or more parameters. This is the active multi-parameter persistence frontier.

6. Mapper — a skeleton of the data

Persistent homology summarizes a point cloud as a multiset of bars. Mapper (Singh, Mémoli & Carlsson, 2007) summarizes it as a graph — a 1-dimensional skeleton showing the cloud's branching structure.

The algorithm:

  1. Choose a filter function $f : X \to \mathbb{R}^k$ (e.g. $L^2$-norm, density estimate, first PCA coordinate, a learned embedding coordinate).
  2. Cover $f(X)$ by overlapping intervals (or hypercubes) $U_1,\dots,U_m$ with overlap fraction $p$.
  3. For each $U_i$, run a clustering algorithm (single-linkage, DBSCAN) on $f^{-1}(U_i) \cap X$. Each cluster is a Mapper node.
  4. Two nodes are connected by an edge iff they share a point in $X$ — i.e. their underlying clusters intersect. (Higher simplices for triple intersections, etc., make it a full simplicial complex; in practice the graph suffices.)

The output is the nerve of the cover by clusters. The flexibility to pick $f$ and the cover is both a strength (it can be tuned to the question) and a hazard (it must be tuned responsibly).

Mapper on a Y-shaped point cloud
drag the cover size; watch the topology of the skeleton change

A Y-shaped cloud has three arms meeting at a junction. We use the $x$-coordinate as the filter $f$. With too few bins, the entire Y collapses to a line; with finely tuned bins and modest overlap, the Y's branching point appears as a degree-3 node. Real applications use Mapper to surface unexpected branch points in patient-clustering or single-cell data (Singh–Mémoli–Carlsson 2007; Nicolau–Levine–Carlsson breast-cancer paper, 2011).

Mapper is the workhorse of "exploratory TDA" outside academia: Nicolau–Levine–Carlsson (2011, PNAS) used it to surface a previously unrecognised survival-favourable subgroup of breast cancers, and Li et al. (2015, Sci. Transl. Med.) later applied it to type-II diabetes patient stratification. Carlsson's company Ayasdi (founded 2008, acquired by SymphonyAI 2019) built a commercial platform around it. Caveats: Mapper graphs are not a topological invariant — they depend on $f$ and the cover — so they are best read as hypothesis generators, then validated with persistent homology or domain knowledge.

7. Cohomology, Reeb graphs, and the broader toolkit

Persistence has spawned a family of related constructions; the four worth knowing:

Persistent cohomology

By duality $H^k(K_i) = H_k(K_i)^*$ when $K_i$ is finite, so the cohomological bars equal the homological bars (over a field). What's gained operationally: cohomology yields cocycles, and certain cocycles (1-cocycles with $\mathbb{S}^1$ coefficients, after lifting) can be used as circular coordinates — explicit angle-valued maps $X \to S^1$ encoding loops in the data. Persistent cohomology is also strictly faster in practice than persistent homology (de Silva–Morozov–Vejdemo-Johansson 2011): the "cohomology shortcut" inverts the matrix once instead of computing column reductions.

Zigzag persistence

Standard persistence requires a single filtration direction. Zigzag persistence (Carlsson–de Silva 2010) allows the direction to switch: $K_0 \to K_1 \leftarrow K_2 \to K_3 \leftarrow \cdots$ models filtrations with merges, sliding windows of time-varying point clouds, or two different filtrations to compare. The Gabriel-quiver classification of $A_n$-type representations still gives a barcode.

Multi-parameter persistence

A two-parameter persistence module $\{V_{s,t}\}$ indexed by $(s,t) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ does not decompose into rectangle-shaped indecomposables: the moduli of indecomposables is wild. Surrogate invariants (rank invariant, fibered barcode, persistence landscape, $\Lambda$-Hilbert function, GRIL) are the field's active research direction.

Reeb graphs & mergetrees

The Reeb graph of $(X, f : X \to \mathbb{R})$ identifies points of $X$ whose preimages lie in the same connected component of $f^{-1}(t)$ — a 1-D skeleton encoding the level-set topology. Mapper is a discrete approximation. Merge trees are the $\beta_0$-only sub-summary, used in scalar-field visualization since the 1990s.

Software ecosystem

  • Ripser (Bauer 2021) — fastest Rips persistence; the de facto standard for moderate-scale clouds.
  • GUDHI (INRIA) — broad C++/Python library: Rips, Čech, Alpha complexes, Mapper, multi-parameter, Wasserstein metrics.
  • Dionysus (Morozov) — research-oriented, supports zigzag and cohomology.
  • scikit-tda — Python umbrella (Ripser, Mapper-via-KeplerMapper, persim).
  • giotto-tda — sklearn-style API; emphasises ML pipelines.

8. Connections



TDA sits at the meeting of algebraic topology, combinatorics, statistics, and applied computation. The frontier is plural: multi-parameter persistence (no structure theorem), differentiable persistence (gradient-based learning over diagrams), and "topology-aware deep learning" (persistence loss layers, persistence-stabilised features). The unsettled question is whether persistence buys real predictive lift on noisy real-world data or merely a sophisticated descriptor; the empirical jury is split.



    

  • Algebraic topology — singular homology — the textbook companion to the simplicial homology used pointwise here; the long exact sequence of a pair sits behind every persistence calculation.

  • Simplicial complexes — combinatorial persistence — sister page focused on the combinatorial $f$-vectors and Stanley–Reisner machinery underneath the Rips and Čech constructions.

  • Spectral methods for massive data sets — graph Laplacian — the linear-algebraic complement to TDA on data; the Laplacian eigenvectors recover $\beta_0$ as the multiplicity of 0, but say nothing about higher-dimensional features.

  • High-dimensional geometry — concentration of measure — concentration phenomena explain why Mapper covers and Rips scales work at all: in high dimensions, typical pairwise distances cluster tightly, sharpening the persistence diagram's signal.
    • 

  • Homological algebra — chain complexes & exact sequences — the algebraic backbone: every persistence computation is a chain-complex computation with the filtration imposed as a grading.
