Arithmetic statistics

Number-theoretic objects — number fields, class groups, elliptic curves, $L$-functions — when ordered by some height become statistical populations. Davenport–Heilbronn, Cohen–Lenstra, Bhargava: the slogan is that arithmetic, viewed in bulk, has shape, and that shape is computable.

Reader's note. This page assumes comfort with class groups and discriminants from algebraic number theory, with the height machine of heights in arithmetic geometry (Northcott finiteness gives the very notion of "ordering by $X$"), and with the rank/$L$-function dictionary of BSD and $L$-functions. The bend that distinguishes arithmetic statistics from analytic number theory is that we want distributions, not just averages — Cohen–Lenstra and Katz–Sarnak each predict a specific limiting measure.

1. Counting number fields

Order number fields $K/\mathbb{Q}$ of degree $n$ by absolute discriminant: $|\mathrm{disc}(K)|\le X$. The basic counting function is

$N_n(X) \;=\; \#\{K/\mathbb{Q}\text{ of degree }n\;:\;|\mathrm{disc}\,K|\le X\}.$

For $n=2$ the answer is classical: every quadratic field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ with $d$ squarefree, $\mathrm{disc}=d$ or $4d$, so $N_2(X)\sim \frac{6}{\pi^2}X$. For $n=3$ the answer is the Davenport–Heilbronn theorem (1971):

$N_3(X) \;\sim\; \dfrac{1}{3\,\zeta(3)}\,X.$

The proof reduces to counting $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-orbits of binary cubic forms via the Delone–Faddeev correspondence (cubic rings $\leftrightarrow$ integer binary cubic forms), then applying a geometry-of-numbers count of lattice points in a fundamental domain — with a delicate sieve to discard reducible / non-maximal orbits.

Bhargava's parametrizations

Higher degrees were stuck for thirty years until Manjul Bhargava reframed the problem. Bhargava's PhD work and follow-ups extended the Delone–Faddeev recipe to a tower of "higher composition laws":

  • Quartic rings $\leftrightarrow$ $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\times\mathrm{SL}_3(\mathbb{Z})$-orbits on pairs of integer ternary quadratic forms.
  • Quintic rings $\leftrightarrow$ $\mathrm{GL}_4(\mathbb{Z})\times\mathrm{SL}_5(\mathbb{Z})$-orbits on quadruples of integer $5\times 5$ skew-symmetric matrices.

Each parametrization plus a geometry-of-numbers count gives an asymptotic $N_n(X)\sim c_n X$ for $n=4,5$. For $n\ge 6$ no such parametrization is known and we are guided by the conjecture of Malle: $N_n(X)\sim c_n X^{1/a(G)}(\log X)^{b(G)-1}$ where $G\le S_n$ is the Galois group of the Galois closure and $a(G),b(G)$ are explicit invariants of $G$.

Cubic field counter

Empirical curve uses tabulated counts of cubic fields by $|\mathrm{disc}|$ (LMFDB-style data, smoothed). Predicted line is $X/(3\zeta(3))\approx X/3.606$. Davenport–Heilbronn says the ratio $N_3(X)\cdot 3\zeta(3)/X\to 1$; the convergence is logarithmically slow.

Take-away. The discriminant ordering is a height: Northcott finiteness over $\mathbb{Q}$ guarantees only finitely many fields below any cap. Asymptotic density inside that finite population is what arithmetic statistics measures.

2. Cohen–Lenstra heuristics

The class group $\mathrm{Cl}(K)$ measures the failure of unique factorization in $\mathcal{O}_K$. For imaginary quadratic $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$ ($d>0$ squarefree), what does $\mathrm{Cl}(K)$ look like as $d$ varies? Class numbers $h(K)=|\mathrm{Cl}(K)|$ are notoriously erratic — Gauss conjectured (proved by Heilbronn) only that $h(K)\to\infty$ — but Cohen and Lenstra (1983) found that the structure of $\mathrm{Cl}(K)$ at each odd prime $p$ is governed by a clean random-matrix-style measure.

The CL measure

Fix an odd prime $p$. The Cohen–Lenstra heuristic asserts: as $K$ ranges over imaginary quadratic fields ordered by $|d|$, the $p$-part $\mathrm{Cl}(K)[p^\infty]$ is distributed as a random finite abelian $p$-group $A$ with probability proportional to $1/|\mathrm{Aut}(A)|$. Concretely, the probability that $\mathrm{Cl}(K)[p^\infty]=A$ tends to

$\Pr_{\mathrm{CL}}(A) \;=\; \dfrac{1}{|\mathrm{Aut}(A)|}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-p^{-k}\bigr).$

Several testable consequences:

  • Probability $p\nmid h(K)$: $\prod_{k\ge 1}(1-p^{-k})$. For $p=3$ this is $\approx 0.5601$, matching tabulated data strikingly well.
  • $p$-rank is mostly $0$: the chance the $p$-rank is $\ge 1$ is $1-\prod(1-p^{-k})\approx p^{-1}+p^{-2}+\cdots$ for large $p$.
  • Cyclic-or-trivial $p$-part: the marginal $\Pr(p\text{-rank}\le 1)$ is $\prod_{k\ge 1}(1-p^{-k})\cdot\bigl(1+p^{-1}/(1-p^{-1})^2\bigr)$. For $p=3$ this is $\approx 0.980$ — almost all imaginary-quadratic class groups are cyclic at $p=3$.

Real fields and function fields

For real quadratic $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ ($d>0$), Cohen–Lenstra–Friedman–Washington predict a different measure — twisted by the rank of the unit group — because $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ has rank one and contributes a factor $|\mathrm{Aut}(A)|^{-1}\cdot |A|^{-1}$. The real heuristic predicts $p\nmid h$ with probability $\prod_{k\ge 2}(1-p^{-k})$, which for $p=3$ is $\approx 0.7544$.

For $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$ replacing $\mathbb{Q}$, Ellenberg–Venkatesh–Westerland (2016) proved a Cohen–Lenstra-type theorem unconditionally by computing the cohomology of Hurwitz moduli spaces — the function-field analogue of CL is a theorem, not a heuristic.

3. Bhargava's Selmer averages

Order elliptic curves $E:\,y^2=x^3+Ax+B$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ by naive height $H(E)=\max(|A|^3,|B|^2)$ (this is a "discriminant-flavored" height: see naive heights). For each prime $\ell$, the $\ell$-Selmer group $\mathrm{Sel}_\ell(E)$ sits in the exact sequence

$0\to E(\mathbb{Q})/\ell E(\mathbb{Q})\to\mathrm{Sel}_\ell(E)\to\sha(E)[\ell]\to 0,$

so its size bounds $E(\mathbb{Q})$'s Mordell–Weil rank: $\mathrm{rk}\,E(\mathbb{Q})\le \log_\ell|\mathrm{Sel}_\ell(E)|$. Bhargava and Shankar (2010, 2013, 2014) computed the average size of $\mathrm{Sel}_\ell$ over the height-ordered family, for $\ell=2,3,4,5$.

Each row is a hard theorem. The proofs run through orbit-counting: $2$-Selmer elements correspond to $\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-orbits on integer binary quartic forms with appropriate invariants; $3$-Selmer to $\mathrm{PGL}_3$-orbits on ternary cubic forms; etc. Geometry of numbers in fundamental domains plus a delicate sieve do the bulk.

Density consequences

Here is the dazzling payoff: take $\ell=5$. Then $\mathbb{E}[|\mathrm{Sel}_5|]=6$. If the $5$-Selmer rank were always $\ge 2$, the average would be $\ge 1+5^2=26$. Since it is $6$, a positive proportion of $E$ have $5$-Selmer rank $\le 1$, hence $\mathrm{rk}\,E(\mathbb{Q})\le 1$ for that proportion. Sharpening this: at least $\tfrac{20.62}{100}$ of $E/\mathbb{Q}$ have rank $0$, and at least $\tfrac{83.75}{100}$ have rank $\le 1$ (Bhargava–Shankar–Skinner–Zhang).

Selmer-size histogram

Quadratic-twist families $E_d:y^2=x^3-dx$ have $|\mathrm{Sel}_2(E_d)|$ controlled by the $2$-Selmer group of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ — sizes are powers of $2$. We sample $|\mathrm{Sel}_2|\in\{2^k\}$ from the Bhargava–Shankar limiting distribution (Poisson-like in $\log_2$). Empirical mean should approach $3$.

4. Average rank of elliptic curves

Order $E/\mathbb{Q}$ by naive height as before. The Goldfeld conjecture (1979) predicts: $50\%$ of $E$ have rank $0$, $50\%$ have rank $1$, and a density-zero exceptional set has rank $\ge 2$. In particular the average analytic rank is exactly $\tfrac12$.

Goldfeld's prediction was the natural reading of the parity heuristic — $\mathrm{ord}_{s=1}L(E,s)$ is a non-negative integer, the parity is governed by the global root number $w(E)$ which is $\pm 1$ with density $\tfrac12$ each, and "minimal" means $\mathrm{rk}=0$ when $w=+1$, $\mathrm{rk}=1$ when $w=-1$. Higher rank costs additional "accidental" zeros.

Unconditional averages

Bhargava–Skinner–Zhang (2014) proved: at least $66.48\%$ of $E/\mathbb{Q}$ have analytic rank $\le 1$ and satisfy BSD. The argument combines:

  • Bhargava–Shankar Selmer averages (last section).
  • The Skinner–Urban / Skinner converse to BSD: if $\mathrm{rk}_\mathrm{an}\le 1$ and certain $p$-adic conditions, then $\mathrm{rk}_\mathrm{an}=\mathrm{rk}_\mathrm{alg}$ and $\sha$ is finite.
  • Parity considerations from the global root number.

The analytic average rank is thus provably $\le \tfrac{1}{2} + \epsilon$ for an explicit $\epsilon$. Goldfeld conjectures equality.

5. Function-field analogues

Replace $\mathbb{Q}$ by $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$. Number fields become finite extensions of $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$; class groups of imaginary quadratic fields become Picard groups of hyperelliptic curves over $\mathbb{F}_q$; $L$-functions become rational functions whose zeros are eigenvalues of Frobenius. Two surprising features emerge.

Random-matrix prediction

For a curve $C/\mathbb{F}_q$ of genus $g$, $L(C,T)=\det(1-T\,\mathrm{Frob}\mid H^1)$ is a polynomial of degree $2g$ with roots on the circle $|T|=q^{-1/2}$. Katz–Sarnak (1999) proved: the normalized angles $\theta_i\in[0,\pi]$ (where roots are $q^{-1/2}e^{i\theta_i}$) become equidistributed as $q\to\infty$ with respect to the eigenvalue measure of the unitary symplectic group $\mathrm{USp}(2g)$. So the Frobenius eigenvalue statistics of families of curves match the eigenvalue statistics of random matrices from a classical compact group.

Cohen–Lenstra over $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$

For hyperelliptic $C:y^2=f(t)$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ (large $q$), Ellenberg–Venkatesh–Westerland (2016) proved the function-field Cohen–Lenstra distribution by computing the cohomology of the Hurwitz schemes $\mathcal{H}_n^G$ — moduli of $G$-covers of $\mathbb{A}^1$ branched at $n$ points. The proof discovers the Cohen–Lenstra weighting from a topological computation, where the original heuristic was numerical-experimental.

6. Connections to $L$-functions and zeros

The Katz–Sarnak philosophy posits that every natural family of $L$-functions has a symmetry type — unitary $U$, orthogonal $O$, or symplectic $\mathrm{USp}$ — such that low-lying zeros (zeros near the central point $s=\tfrac12$) follow the eigenvalue statistics of the corresponding random-matrix ensemble. Empirically and conjecturally:

Why this matters arithmetically

Zero statistics encode arithmetic averages. For example, the $1$-level density of zeros of $L(s,\chi_d)$ near $s=\tfrac12$ predicts the proportion of $d$ for which $L(\tfrac12,\chi_d)\ne 0$ — a non-vanishing question equivalent to the BSD-on-average problem for quadratic twists. Soundararajan and Conrey–Soundararajan proved $\ge 87.5\%$ non-vanishing using moments + ratios, matching the Katz–Sarnak prediction.

Average-rank conjectures, the Iwaniec–Luo–Sarnak family-orthogonality results, and the Bhargava–Shankar Selmer averages all converge on the same picture: $L$-function families look like random-matrix ensembles, and which ensemble is dictated by the symmetry of the underlying motive.

Take-away. The bridge from arithmetic statistics to random matrices is not metaphor — it's a precise dictionary. Choose a family, identify its symmetry type, and the eigenvalue statistics of the matching random-matrix ensemble predict every reasonable family-average about the $L$-functions: zero locations, moments, non-vanishing proportions, $L'(\tfrac12)$ statistics, and (Bhargava–Shankar) the underlying Selmer / rank averages.

Connections

Arithmetic statistics is open at every frontier: Malle's conjecture is wide open for $n\ge 6$, the Cohen–Lenstra heuristics for cubic and higher-degree fields are largely conjectural, the elliptic-curve average rank is conjecturally $\tfrac12$ but only proved $\le 1.17$ unconditionally, and the Katz–Sarnak symmetry assignment for high-rank automorphic families is in flux.

  • Algebraic number theory — class groups — the populations whose distribution Cohen–Lenstra describes.
  • Heights — Northcott finiteness — the height ordering is what makes "average" meaningful.
  • BSD — Bhargava–Skinner–Zhang prove BSD on average using Selmer averages.
  • $L$-functions and Sato–Tate — the random-matrix endpoint of the symmetry-type story.
  • Analytic number theory — moments of $\zeta$ and $L$-functions feed every density theorem.
