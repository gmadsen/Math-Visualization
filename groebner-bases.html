Gröbner bases & computational commutative algebra

Monomial orders, the multivariable division algorithm, Buchberger's S-polynomial criterion, and the elimination theorem — the algorithmic core that turns ideals in $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ into a working solver for polynomial systems and implicit equations.

1. Polynomial rings & monomial orders

Fix a field $k$ and the polynomial ring $R = k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$. A monomial is $x^\alpha = x_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$ for some exponent vector $\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^n$. Every $f\in R$ has a unique expansion $f = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha\, x^\alpha$ with $c_\alpha\in k$ and finite support — the entire ring is a free $k$-module on the monomials.

To talk about a "leading term" we need a way to compare monomials. A monomial order on $R$ is a total order $>$ on the set of monomials satisfying:

  • Total: any two distinct monomials are comparable.
  • Multiplicative: if $\alpha > \beta$ then $\alpha + \gamma > \beta + \gamma$ for every $\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^n$.
  • Well-ordering: every nonempty set of monomials has a least element.

Equivalently (assuming totality and multiplicativity), the well-ordering condition is the same as $1 \le x^\alpha$ for all $\alpha$ — every variable has positive "weight". The three orders that matter in practice:

ordercompare $x^\alpha$ vs $x^\beta$geometric flavor
lexcompare $\alpha_1$ vs $\beta_1$; tie ⇒ $\alpha_2$ vs $\beta_2$; etc.variable-elimination order
grlexcompare $|\alpha|$ vs $|\beta|$ (total degree); tie ⇒ lexdegree-prioritizing
grevlexcompare $|\alpha|$ vs $|\beta|$; tie ⇒ rightmost differing coordinate, with the smaller exponent winningfastest in practice for generic ideals

For any $f = \sum c_\alpha x^\alpha$ with $c_\alpha\ne 0$, the chosen order picks out a maximum exponent $\alpha^*$ — the leading monomial $\mathrm{LM}(f) = x^{\alpha^*}$, with leading coefficient $\mathrm{LC}(f) = c_{\alpha^*}$ and leading term $\mathrm{LT}(f) = c_{\alpha^*} x^{\alpha^*}$. All three depend on the order; switch from lex to grevlex and the same polynomial can have a different leading term.

Why lex differs so radically. Under lex with $x>y$, the comparison $x > y^{1000}$ holds — any positive $x$-exponent dominates any $y$-exponent. Under grlex, $y^{1000}$ wins because total degree is checked first. This single fact is why lex is the right order for elimination (§7) and the wrong order for nearly everything else.

2. The multivariable division algorithm

Given a monomial order and an ordered tuple of divisors $(g_1,\dots,g_s)$, the multivariable division algorithm rewrites any $f\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ as

$f = a_1 g_1 + a_2 g_2 + \cdots + a_s g_s + r$

where the quotients $a_i\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ and the remainder $r$ have one definite property: no monomial appearing in $r$ is divisible by any $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$.

The procedure is a direct generalization of polynomial long division: at each step, look at the current $\mathrm{LT}$ of the dividend; scan $(g_1,\dots,g_s)$ in order for the first $g_i$ whose leading term divides it; if found, subtract the appropriate multiple of $g_i$; if not found, peel that monomial off into the remainder $r$ and continue. Repeat until the dividend is zero.

The single-variable case is a deceptive special case. In $k[x]$ the remainder of $f$ on division by $g$ is unique — a fact every undergraduate proves as part of the Euclidean algorithm. In $k[x,y]$ this fails. Try $f = x^2 y + xy^2 + y^2$ divided by $(g_1,g_2) = (xy-1,\, y^2-1)$ versus $(g_2,g_1)$ under lex: the order $(g_1, g_2)$ produces remainder $r = x + y + 1$, while $(g_2, g_1)$ produces $r = 2x + 1$. Both satisfy the no-monomial-divisible-by-any-$\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$ invariant, but they're different polynomials. The remainder depends on the ordering of the divisor list. Gröbner bases (§3) are exactly the divisor sets for which this defect disappears.
Multivariable division — order matters
$f = x^2 y + xy^2 + y^2$ in $k[x,y]$, lex with $x > y$

Watch how the same dividend, with the same divisor set in two different orders, produces two different remainders. The "no monomial of $r$ divisible by any $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$" condition holds in both runs — that's the only invariant.

3. Gröbner bases: definition

The flaw in §2's division algorithm — non-uniqueness of the remainder — is structural, not procedural. We can fix it, but only by demanding that the divisor set carry enough leading terms to detect every leading term that the ideal could conceivably produce.

For an ideal $I\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$, write $\mathrm{LT}(I) = \{\mathrm{LT}(f) : f\in I\setminus\{0\}\}$ (a set of monomials with coefficients) and $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ for the ideal generated by them. Because $\mathrm{LT}(I)$ is closed under multiplication by all of $k\setminus\{0\}$, the ideal $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ is in fact a monomial ideal — generated by the leading monomials, ignoring coefficients.

Definition. Fix a monomial order on $R = k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$. A finite subset $G = \{g_1,\dots,g_s\}\subset I$ is a Gröbner basis for $I$ when $$\langle \mathrm{LT}(g_1), \dots, \mathrm{LT}(g_s)\rangle \;=\; \langle \mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle.$$ Equivalently: for every $f\in I\setminus\{0\}$, some $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$ divides $\mathrm{LT}(f)$.

Three immediate consequences justify the definition:

generates Any Gröbner basis $G$ of $I$ generates $I$ as an ideal. (Proof: divide any $f\in I$ by $G$ — the remainder $r$ lies in $I$ but has no monomial divisible by any $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$, so $\mathrm{LT}(r)\notin\langle\mathrm{LT}(G)\rangle = \langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$, forcing $r=0$.)

unique remainder The division algorithm applied to any $f$ produces the same remainder regardless of the ordering of $G$. (The same argument: two different remainders $r_1, r_2$ would differ by an element of $I$, and that difference's leading term would have to be divisible by some $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$ — contradicting that neither $r_i$ has any monomial divisible by any $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$.)

ideal membership $f\in I$ if and only if the remainder of $f$ on division by $G$ is zero.

Existence is guaranteed by Hilbert's basis theorem applied to the leading-term ideal: $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ is a monomial ideal in a Noetherian ring, hence finitely generated, and any finite subset $G\subset I$ whose leading terms generate $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ is a Gröbner basis. The proof is non-constructive — it tells us $G$ exists but not how to find it. That is the gap §4–§5 fill.

Staircase & standard monomials
a leading-term ideal $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle\subset k[x,y]$ partitions $\mathbb{N}^2$

The shaded region is $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ — every monomial it contains. The unshaded region (below the staircase) is the set of standard monomials, which form a $k$-basis of the quotient $k[x,y]/I$ when $I$ is the original ideal. Vector-space dimension $\dim_k k[x,y]/I$ equals the count of standard monomials — finite iff the staircase reaches both axes (zero-dimensional ideal).

4. S-polynomials & Buchberger's criterion

To turn the abstract definition of §3 into an algorithm, we need a local test for whether a candidate $G = \{g_1,\dots,g_s\}$ already generates the entire leading-term ideal of $\langle G\rangle$. Buchberger's insight: the only obstruction comes from pairs.

For nonzero $f, g\in k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$, let $\gamma = \mathrm{lcm}(\mathrm{LM}(f),\mathrm{LM}(g))$ — the smallest monomial divisible by both leading monomials. The S-polynomial is

$S(f,g) \;=\; \dfrac{\gamma}{\mathrm{LT}(f)} f \;-\; \dfrac{\gamma}{\mathrm{LT}(g)} g.$

By construction, the leading terms of the two summands are both equal to $\gamma$ (with cancellable coefficients), so they cancel; whatever survives is a polynomial in $\langle f,g\rangle$ whose leading monomial is strictly smaller than $\gamma$ — a "syzygy" exposing structure that neither $f$ nor $g$ revealed alone.

Buchberger's criterion. Fix a monomial order. A finite generating set $G = \{g_1,\dots,g_s\}$ of an ideal $I$ is a Gröbner basis if and only if for every pair $(i,j)$ with $i

The "only if" direction is straightforward: if $G$ is a Gröbner basis then $S(g_i,g_j)\in I$ has all leading terms divisible by some $\mathrm{LT}(g_k)$, so division reduces it to $0$. The "if" direction is the content — it says S-polynomial obstructions are the only obstructions. The proof reorganizes any element of $I$ as a linear combination of $g_i$ in which cancellations between leading terms factor through S-polynomials.

S-polynomial computer
cancellation of leading terms, exposed

In the third example, $S(f,g)$ does not reduce to zero modulo $\{f,g\}$ — Buchberger's criterion fails, so $\{f,g\}$ is not yet a Gröbner basis. We have to adjoin the remainder and continue. That is exactly what §5 does.

5. Buchberger's algorithm

Buchberger's criterion is a test, not yet an algorithm. The algorithm turns the criterion into a closure procedure: when an S-polynomial fails to reduce to zero, append its remainder to the basis and repeat.

Algorithm (Buchberger). Input: a finite generating set $F = \{f_1,\dots,f_t\}\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ for an ideal $I$, plus a monomial order. Output: a Gröbner basis $G$ for $I$.
  1. Set $G \leftarrow F$.
  2. Repeat: for each pair $(g_i, g_j)\in G$ with $i
  3. If every pair gives $r_{ij} = 0$, return $G$.

Termination. Each time we adjoin a nonzero $r_{ij}$, we are adding a polynomial whose leading term lies in $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ but not in $\langle\mathrm{LT}(G_{\text{old}})\rangle$ (otherwise the division would have reduced it). So the chain of monomial ideals $\langle\mathrm{LT}(G_0)\rangle \subsetneq \langle\mathrm{LT}(G_1)\rangle \subsetneq \cdots$ strictly ascends. By Hilbert's basis theorem, $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ is Noetherian, so this chain stabilizes after finitely many steps and the algorithm halts.

Correctness. When the loop terminates, every $S(g_i,g_j)$ reduces to zero modulo $G$, so by Buchberger's criterion $G$ is a Gröbner basis of the ideal it generates — which is $\langle F\rangle = I$, since we only added polynomials already in $I$.

Complexity. The algorithm is correct and terminating, but it can be slow. Mayr and Meyer constructed ideals in $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ generated by polynomials of bounded degree whose Gröbner bases contain elements of degree doubly exponential in $n$. Improvements — Buchberger's two pair-elimination criteria, F4 (Faugère 1999), F5 (Faugère 2002), signature-based algorithms — chip away at the constants and prune dead pairs, but the worst-case complexity class is irreducibly $\mathrm{EXPSPACE}$-complete.

Buchberger run on $\{x^2 + y^2 - 1,\; xy - 1\}$
unit circle ∩ hyperbola — lex with $x > y$

The algorithm appends the S-polynomial remainder $x + y^3 - y$ as a third generator, then the further S-polynomial yields the univariate $y^4 - y^2 + 1$. After minimization the reduced lex Gröbner basis is $\{x + y^3 - y,\; y^4 - y^2 + 1\}$ — a triangular shape that solves the system in §8.

6. Reduced Gröbner bases

Buchberger's algorithm produces a Gröbner basis, but the output depends on bookkeeping choices: which pairs are processed first, in what order divisors are scanned. Different runs on the same ideal can return different bases. To get a canonical representative, we trim.

Definition. A Gröbner basis $G$ is reduced when:
  • every $g\in G$ is monic ($\mathrm{LC}(g) = 1$);
  • for each $g\in G$, no monomial of $g$ is divisible by $\mathrm{LT}(g')$ for any other $g'\in G\setminus\{g\}$.

The first condition is a normalization; the second says no generator carries any tail term that another generator could "explain". Together they squeeze all redundancy out of the basis.

Theorem. For a fixed monomial order, every nonzero ideal $I\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ has a unique reduced Gröbner basis.

This is the canonical representative theorem. Two ideals $I, J\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ are equal if and only if their reduced Gröbner bases under the same order are identical sets of polynomials. Ideal equality — a priori an existential question — becomes a literal string comparison.

The reduction procedure is mechanical. Starting from any Gröbner basis $G$:

  1. Minimize. Drop any $g$ whose leading term is divisible by another generator's leading term (it is redundant for $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$).
  2. Interreduce. Replace each remaining $g$ by its remainder on division by $G\setminus\{g\}$ — this kills any tail term divisible by another's leading term.
  3. Normalize. Divide each $g$ by its leading coefficient to make it monic.

The output is independent of the starting basis: any two Gröbner bases of $I$ pass through the same reduced form.

Reduce two non-canonical Gröbner bases
two messy starting bases of the same ideal — both reduce to the same canonical form

Both starting bases are valid (non-reduced) Gröbner bases of $\langle xy - 1,\; y^2 - 1\rangle$ under lex with $x>y$. Minimization, interreduction, and normalization converge to the unique reduced basis $\{x - y,\; y^2 - 1\}$.

7. Elimination ideals

Now the payoff. Let $I\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$. The $\ell$-th elimination ideal is

$I_\ell = I \cap k[x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_n].$

Geometrically, if $V(I)\subset\mathbb{A}^n$ is the variety of $I$, then $V(I_\ell)\subset\mathbb{A}^{n-\ell}$ is the Zariski closure of the projection $\pi_\ell\colon V(I)\to\mathbb{A}^{n-\ell}$ onto the last $n-\ell$ coordinates. Eliminating $x_1,\dots,x_\ell$ from the equations of $V(I)$ describes the shadow of $V(I)$ on the lower-dimensional space.

Elimination Theorem. Let $G$ be a Gröbner basis of $I$ under lex order with $x_1 > x_2 > \cdots > x_n$. Then for every $\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}$, $$G_\ell := G\cap k[x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_n]$$ is a Gröbner basis of the elimination ideal $I_\ell$.

The proof is short. Take $f\in I_\ell$. Its leading term $\mathrm{LT}(f)\in k[x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_n]$ — only the small variables appear. By the Gröbner basis property, $\mathrm{LT}(f)$ is divisible by some $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$ for $g_i\in G$. Under lex with $x_1>\cdots>x_n$, if $g_i$ contains any of $x_1,\dots,x_\ell$ then $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)$ contains one of those variables — but $\mathrm{LT}(g_i)\mid\mathrm{LT}(f)\in k[x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_n]$ then forces a contradiction. So $g_i\in k[x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_n]$, i.e. $g_i\in G_\ell$. Thus the leading terms of $G_\ell$ generate $\langle\mathrm{LT}(I_\ell)\rangle$, and $G_\ell$ is a Gröbner basis.

Why lex. The argument depends on the dichotomy "$x_1,\dots,x_\ell$ outranks anything $x_1,\dots,x_\ell$-free". Lex makes this dichotomy literal: a single nonzero exponent in any of $x_1,\dots,x_\ell$ dominates every $x_1,\dots,x_\ell$-free monomial. Grlex and grevlex weigh total degree first, so a high-degree polynomial in $x_{\ell+1},\dots,x_n$ can outrank a low-degree polynomial that involves $x_1$. The cleanest cure when one wants both elimination and grevlex's speed is a block order: lex on the elimination block, grevlex on the rest.

Application — intersection of ideals. Given $I, J\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$, introduce a fresh variable $t$ and form the ideal $K = tI + (1-t)J\subset k[t, x_1,\dots,x_n]$. Then $K\cap k[x_1,\dots,x_n] = I\cap J$. Compute a lex Gröbner basis with $t > x_1 > \cdots > x_n$ and read off $K\cap k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$.
Eliminate $x$ from $I = \langle x^2+y^2-1,\; xy-1\rangle$
lex with $x > y$ — projection to the $y$-axis

The unit circle $x^2+y^2=1$ and the hyperbola $xy=1$ have no real intersection. Eliminating $x$ produces the univariate $y^4 - y^2 + 1$, whose discriminant for $u=y^2$ is $1-4 = -3 < 0$ — confirming all four intersection $y$-coordinates are non-real. The elimination ideal is exactly the ideal of the projection of $V(I)\subset\mathbb{A}^2_{\overline{k}}$ onto the $y$-axis.

8. Solving polynomial systems & implicitization

The elimination theorem gives Gröbner bases their two most consequential applications. Both reduce a multivariate problem to a chain of univariate problems by exploiting lex's variable-by-variable ordering.

Solving a zero-dimensional system

An ideal $I\subset k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ is zero-dimensional when $V(I)\subset\overline{k}^n$ is finite — equivalently, $\dim_k k[x_1,\dots,x_n]/I < \infty$. For such ideals the lex Gröbner basis $G$ has a triangular shape: the elimination ideal $I_{n-1} = I\cap k[x_n]$ is principal, generated by some $h_n(x_n)$. Solve $h_n(x_n) = 0$ — a univariate polynomial — to get finitely many values for $x_n$. For each such value, the next elimination ideal $I_{n-2} = I\cap k[x_{n-1}, x_n]$ becomes a univariate problem in $x_{n-1}$ once $x_n$ is fixed. Continue inductively. This is back-substitution, identical in spirit to solving a triangular linear system but with polynomial unknowns.

Shape lemma. Generic enough zero-dimensional radical ideals admit a "shape" reduced lex Gröbner basis $\{x_n^d + \cdots,\; x_{n-1} - h_{n-1}(x_n),\; \dots,\; x_1 - h_1(x_n)\}$ — a univariate polynomial in the smallest variable plus an explicit formula for each other variable as a polynomial in $x_n$. Computing the variety reduces to univariate root-finding.

Implicitization

Given a parametrization $\phi\colon \mathbb{A}^m \to \mathbb{A}^n$, $(t_1,\dots,t_m)\mapsto (f_1(\mathbf{t}),\dots,f_n(\mathbf{t}))$, the implicit equations of the closure of the image are the elimination ideal:

$\text{ideal of }\overline{\phi(\mathbb{A}^m)} \;=\; \langle x_1 - f_1, \dots, x_n - f_n\rangle \;\cap\; k[x_1,\dots,x_n].$

Compute a Gröbner basis under lex with $t_1, \dots, t_m > x_1, \dots, x_n$ and extract the elements with no $t$. Geometrically: parametric "draw" → Gröbner elimination → implicit "carve".

Two case studies — solve & implicitize
$\{x^2+y^2-1,\; xy-1\}$ and the rational normal cubic $(t,t^2,t^3)$

Both examples are small enough to compute and verify by hand. The first is zero-dimensional (four complex solutions, no real ones); the second is one-dimensional (a curve in $\mathbb{A}^3$ — the twisted cubic — recovered by eliminating the parameter $t$).

Connections

Gröbner bases are the computational frontier of commutative algebra: every theoretical statement about ideals in $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ becomes, in principle, a computation — but the doubly-exponential worst case keeps the area lively. Modern research pushes algorithms (F4, F5, signature-based, FGLM order conversion) and hybrid numeric–symbolic methods (homotopy continuation) into regimes Buchberger's original procedure cannot reach. Theoretical commutative algebra continues to find new structural homes for the leading-term machinery — initial ideals, SAGBI bases for subalgebras, tropical geometry as the "logarithmic shadow" of Gröbner degenerations.

  • Commutative algebra — Noetherian rings & Hilbert basis — the existence half of "every ideal has a finite Gröbner basis" lives here; ascending chain conditions on $\langle\mathrm{LT}(F)\rangle$ are what make Buchberger terminate.
  • Abstract algebra — polynomial rings & irreducibility — the single-variable Euclidean algorithm is the historical seed; multivariable division and Gröbner bases are its honest generalization.
  • Schemes & Bézout's theorem — the elimination theorem computes set-theoretic projections of varieties; reduced Gröbner bases give Hilbert series, dimension, and degree of $V(I)$ algorithmically.
  • Galois theory — solving zero-dimensional polynomial systems is exactly what the resolvent constructions of classical Galois theory wanted; lex Gröbner bases give a uniform algorithmic surface.
  • Computational number theory — Gröbner bases over $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{F}_p$ underpin polynomial-system solvers used in cryptanalysis (HFE, multivariate signature schemes) and ideal arithmetic in number fields.
