POMDPs and belief states

When the agent can't see the state — only noisy observations — it must act on a belief, a distribution over states; this restores a Markov problem over the continuous belief simplex, whose optimal value is piecewise-linear and convex.

1. The POMDP model

A Markov decision process assumes the agent always knows which state it is in. Drop that assumption and you get a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP): the world still evolves as an MDP, but the agent never sees the state $s$ — only a noisy observation $o$. Formally a POMDP is a tuple

$\mathcal M = (\mathcal S,\ \mathcal A,\ T,\ R,\ \Omega,\ Z,\ \gamma)$

extending the MDP $(\mathcal S,\mathcal A,T,R,\gamma)$ — with transition kernel $T(s'\mid s,a)$ and reward $R(s,a)$ — by a set of observations $\Omega$ and an observation function $Z(o\mid s',a)$, the probability of perceiving $o$ after action $a$ lands the world in the (hidden) state $s'$. Each row $Z(\cdot\mid s',a)$ is a distribution over $\Omega$, just as each $T(\cdot\mid s,a)$ is a distribution over $\mathcal S$.

The Tiger problem (running example). Two states: the tiger is behind the left door ($\textsf{TL}$) or the right door ($\textsf{TR}$). Three actions: listen, open-left, open-right. Listening leaves the tiger where it is ($T=\mathrm{Id}$) and returns a noisy growl: $Z(\text{hear-left}\mid\textsf{TL},\text{listen})=0.85$ and $Z(\text{hear-left}\mid\textsf{TR},\text{listen})=0.15$ — the ear is right $85\%$ of the time. Opening a door is the gamble: open the tiger-free door for a reward, open the tiger's door for a big penalty. Crucially the agent only ever hears growls; it never directly sees the tiger.

Pick an action and sample an observation. The hidden true state is fixed (tiger-left) so you can see how listening leaks information through $Z$ while opening a door produces no informative observation at all.

2. Belief states & the Bayes update

Since the state is hidden, the agent acts on what it can compute: a belief $b\in\Delta(\mathcal S)$, a probability distribution over states with $b(s)\ge 0$ and $\sum_s b(s)=1$. For the two-state Tiger problem the belief is a single number $b=b(\textsf{TL})\in[0,1]$ (with $b(\textsf{TR})=1-b$): the entire belief space is the segment $[0,1]$.

After taking action $a$ and seeing observation $o$, the belief updates by Bayes filtering — a predict step followed by a correct step:

$\displaystyle b^{a,o}(s') \;=\; \eta\;\underbrace{Z(o\mid s',a)}_{\text{correct}}\;\underbrace{\sum_{s} T(s'\mid s,a)\,b(s)}_{\text{predict}},\qquad \eta=\Big(\textstyle\sum_{s'} Z(o\mid s',a)\sum_s T(s'\mid s,a)b(s)\Big)^{-1}.$

The inner sum pushes the prior $b$ through the dynamics $T$ (the prediction); multiplying by the observation likelihood $Z(o\mid s',a)$ is the Bayesian correction; $\eta$ renormalizes so $b^{a,o}$ is again a distribution. This is exactly recursive Bayes' rule applied one step at a time. The remarkable consequence: $b$ is a sufficient statistic for the full action-observation history. Two histories that yield the same belief are interchangeable for every future decision, so the agent can throw the history away and keep only $b$.

Worked mini-example. Start undecided, $b=0.5$, and listen. Listening leaves the state fixed, so the predict step keeps $(0.5,0.5)$. Suppose you hear a growl on the left, with accuracy $0.85$: correct by $Z$ to get $b'(\textsf{TL})\propto 0.85\cdot 0.5=0.425$ and $b'(\textsf{TR})\propto 0.15\cdot 0.5=0.075$. Normalize by $\eta=1/(0.425+0.075)=2$: $b'(\textsf{TL})=0.85$, $b'(\textsf{TR})=0.15$. One growl moved you from $0.5$ to $0.85$. Hear another left growl and the belief sharpens further toward certainty; a right growl pulls it back.

3. The belief MDP

Because the belief is a sufficient statistic, a POMDP is equivalent to a fully observable MDP whose states are beliefs. This belief MDP $(\Delta(\mathcal S),\mathcal A,\tau,\rho,\gamma)$ has:

  • state space the (continuous) belief simplex $\Delta(\mathcal S)$ — for two states, the segment $[0,1]$;
  • reward $\displaystyle \rho(b,a)=\sum_s b(s)\,R(s,a)$, the belief-weighted average of the underlying rewards;
  • transition $\tau(b'\mid b,a)=\sum_{o:\,b^{a,o}=b'} P(o\mid b,a)$, where the next belief is the deterministic update $b^{a,o}$ and $P(o\mid b,a)=\sum_{s'}Z(o\mid s',a)\sum_s T(s'\mid s,a)b(s)$ is the probability of seeing $o$.

So given an action $a$, the belief jumps to one of finitely many successor beliefs $b^{a,o}$ — one per observation $o$ — with probabilities $P(o\mid b,a)$. Now everything from the MDP theory applies verbatim: the Bellman optimality equation

$\displaystyle V^*(b)=\max_{a}\Big\{\rho(b,a)+\gamma\sum_{o}P(o\mid b,a)\,V^*(b^{a,o})\Big\}$

characterizes the optimal value, and a greedy policy in belief space is optimal. The only — but serious — catch is that the state space is now a continuum: we cannot tabulate one value per state. The structure of $V^*$ over that continuum, developed next, is what rescues computation.

Worked mini-example. From $b=0.5$ in the Tiger problem, the listen action sends the belief to one of two successors: hearing left lands at $b^{\text{listen,L}}=0.85$ and hearing right at $b^{\text{listen,R}}=0.15$ (by the symmetric Bayes update). At $b=0.5$ each growl is equally likely, $P(\text{hear-left}\mid b)=0.5\cdot 0.85+0.5\cdot 0.15=0.5$, so the belief MDP transitions to $0.85$ or $0.15$ each with probability $0.5$ — a genuine stochastic transition in belief space, even though the underlying tiger never moved.

Pick an action and watch where the belief can land. Listening fans out into two successor beliefs (one per growl); opening a door ends the episode (it resets the belief to $0.5$ for the next episode).

4. Piecewise-linear convex value

The belief MDP has a continuous state space, so its value function is a function $V^*:\Delta(\mathcal S)\to\mathbb R$ over the simplex. The structural miracle of POMDPs is that, for finite horizons, this $V^*$ is piecewise-linear and convex (PWLC):

$\displaystyle V^*(b)=\max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\ \alpha\cdot b=\max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\ \sum_s \alpha(s)\,b(s),$

for a finite set $\Gamma$ of $\alpha$-vectors. Each $\alpha\in\Gamma$ is an $|\mathcal S|$-vector: its entry $\alpha(s)$ is the value, if the true state were $s$, of committing to one particular conditional plan (a fixed action followed by an observation-contingent continuation). The expected value of that plan under belief $b$ is the linear function $\alpha\cdot b$. The optimal value picks the best plan at each belief, so $V^*$ is the upper envelope of finitely many lines — a convex, piecewise-linear function. Convexity has a meaning: the agent values information, so $V^*$ is highest at the certain corners $b\in\{0,1\}$ and dips in the uncertain middle.

Worked mini-example. With the belief coordinate $b=b(\textsf{TL})$ so that $\alpha\cdot b=\alpha(\textsf{TL})\,b+\alpha(\textsf{TR})\,(1-b)$, consider three plans. "Always open-right" is great when the tiger is left and disastrous when right: $\alpha_R=(10,-100)$, the line $110\,b-100$. "Always open-left" is the mirror: $\alpha_L=(-100,10)$, the line $10-110\,b$. They cross at $b=0.5$. A third plan "listen, then decide" has a flatter, higher line near the middle, $\alpha_{\text{listen}}\approx(2,2)$ minus a small cost — so near $b=0.5$ listening dominates both open plans (it is worth paying to gather information), while near the certain corners opening the obviously-safe door wins. $V^*$ is the upper envelope of all three.

Toggle the $\alpha$-vectors on and off. The thick yellow curve is the upper envelope $V(b)=\max_\alpha\alpha\cdot b$ — note it is convex and piecewise-linear, with a kink wherever the maximizing plan changes.

5. Exact value iteration

Value iteration for POMDPs maps a set $\Gamma$ of $\alpha$-vectors to a new set $\Gamma'$ representing the backed-up value $V'=TV$. The exact DP backup has two stages. First, for each action $a$ and each observation $o$, form the per-observation backprojections of every $\alpha\in\Gamma$:

$\displaystyle \alpha^{a,o}(s)=\sum_{s'} Z(o\mid s',a)\,T(s'\mid s,a)\,\alpha(s').$

Then for each action take a cross-sum over observations — pick one backprojected vector per observation and add them, together with the immediate reward — to build a candidate plan:

$\displaystyle \alpha^a = R(\cdot,a) + \gamma\sum_{o}\alpha^{a,o_{(\cdot)}},\qquad \Gamma'=\bigcup_a\{\text{all such }\alpha^a\}.$

This is the Monahan/Sondik enumeration. The trouble is the count: choosing one vector per observation gives $|\Gamma|^{|\Omega|}$ combinations per action, so $|\Gamma'|$ can be as large as $|\mathcal A|\,|\Gamma|^{|\Omega|}$ — a doubly-exponential blowup over the horizon. The remedy is pruning: an $\alpha$-vector that is never the maximizer at any belief on the simplex contributes nothing to the upper envelope and is deleted. "Is there a belief $b$ where $\alpha$ strictly beats all the others?" is a linear program; if it is infeasible, $\alpha$ is dominated and discarded. Even with pruning, finite-horizon POMDP planning is PSPACE-complete.

Worked mini-example. On the Tiger problem one backup over the three base plans (open-left, open-right, listen) with two observations generates a fistful of candidate $\alpha$-vectors — the cross-sum produces one per (action, observation-assignment). Many lie strictly below the envelope formed by the survivors: e.g. a plan that opens-left after hearing a right growl is dominated everywhere by the plan that opens-right then. Pruning's LP detects exactly these and removes them, leaving only the handful of vectors that surface on the upper envelope.

6. Point-based methods

Exact value iteration is defeated by the doubly-exponential $\alpha$-vector blowup. Point-based value iteration (PBVI, and its anytime descendant SARSOP) sidesteps it with one idea: only ever back up the value at a finite, sampled set $B=\{b_1,\dots,b_m\}$ of belief points. For each $b\in B$ the backup keeps the single best $\alpha$-vector at that belief, so the set $\Gamma$ never grows beyond $|B|$ vectors — bounded, regardless of horizon.

Because every kept $\alpha$-vector is the value of a genuinely realizable conditional plan, $\max_{\alpha\in\Gamma}\alpha\cdot b$ can never exceed $V^*(b)$: point-based methods produce a lower bound on the optimal value. Adding more (and better-placed) belief points adds plans and lifts the bound monotonically toward $V^*$. The belief set is usually grown by simulating reachable beliefs from the initial $b_0$, since those are the only beliefs the agent will actually encounter — concentrating effort where it pays. The result is near-optimal in practice on problems far too large for exact VI.

Worked mini-example. Seed PBVI on the Tiger problem with the single belief $B=\{0.5\}$. The best plan there is "listen" (information is most valuable when you are most uncertain), so the bound is low and flat. Add the reachable beliefs $\{0.15,0.85\}$ that one growl produces, and "open the confident door" plans enter $\Gamma$, raising the bound near the corners. Keep sampling toward $\{0.03,0.97,\dots\}$ and the lower-bound envelope climbs to hug the true PWLC $V^*$ — never crossing above it.

Press add belief point to sample a new reachable belief, then back up to refit one $\alpha$-vector per point. The yellow lower-bound envelope climbs toward the faint true $V^*$ as $B$ grows; it never rises above it.

Connections

The belief state is the conceptual fix for partial observability — it restores the Markov property at the price of a continuous, exponentially large state space, and that tension is exactly where the frontier lives. Exact solution is PSPACE-complete, so practical work is all approximation: point-based solvers (SARSOP), online tree search (POMCP, DESPOT) that plan from the current belief without representing $V^*$ everywhere, and, in deep RL, learning a belief implicitly with a recurrent network instead of computing it. Continuous-state and continuous-observation POMDPs, and robustness to a misspecified model $Z$, remain genuinely hard.

  • Markov decision processes — the MDP model — a POMDP is an MDP plus observations; the belief MDP shows it is an MDP, just over the belief simplex.
  • Probability theory — conditioning & Bayes' rule — the belief update is one step of recursive Bayesian filtering: predict through $T$, correct by $Z$, renormalize.
  • Kalman filtering — state estimation — the continuous-state, Gaussian analogue of belief filtering: the same predict-correct loop, with the belief a Gaussian summarized by its mean and covariance.
  • Convex optimization — convex functions — $V^*$ being a max of linear pieces is exactly a convex, piecewise-linear function; pruning dominated $\alpha$-vectors is a linear-feasibility test.
  • Reinforcement learning — the RL problem — when $T$, $R$, and even $Z$ are unknown, model-free RL on POMDPs learns the belief implicitly (e.g. with recurrent policies) rather than computing it.
