Random matrix theory

Eigenvalues of large random matrices have universal limit laws — Wigner's semicircle, Marchenko–Pastur for sample covariance, Tracy–Widom at the edge — that recur from quantum chaos and statistics to the zeros of $\zeta(s)$. Free probability supplies the noncommutative calculus; Katz–Sarnak supplies the dictionary to $L$-functions.

1. Random matrix ensembles and the Dyson index

A random matrix ensemble is a probability measure on a space of matrices. The richest examples — and the ones whose eigenvalue statistics turn out to be universal — fix a base symmetry and then sample i.i.d.\ Gaussian entries respecting it. Three classical Wigner ensembles cover the symmetry zoo:

ensemblematrix spaceentry algebraDyson $\beta$physical model
GOE$N\times N$ real symmetric$\mathbb{R}$$1$time-reversal symmetric, spin $0$
GUE$N\times N$ complex Hermitian$\mathbb{C}$$2$time-reversal broken
GSE$N\times N$ quaternion self-dual$\mathbb{H}$$4$time-reversal symmetric, spin $\tfrac12$

In every case the entries above the diagonal are independent, complex/quaternion entries split into independent Gaussian real components, and the variance is calibrated so that the joint density of eigenvalues $\lambda_1\le\cdots\le\lambda_N$ has the explicit closed form

$p_\beta(\lambda)\;=\;\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta}}\;\prod_{i

The Vandermonde factor $\prod|\lambda_i-\lambda_j|^\beta$ is level repulsion: eigenvalues hate being close, and the strength of the repulsion is the Dyson index $\beta$. Setting $\beta=0$ (the "Poisson" limit) would put i.i.d.\ eigenvalues on the line — and is exactly what real-symmetric-but-non-Wigner ensembles like integrable Hamiltonians give. The pattern is Berry's conjectures of quantum chaos: integrable spectra are Poisson, chaotic spectra are GOE/GUE.

Why three?

Dyson's threefold way (1962) classified random Hamiltonians by the action of time reversal $T$ and spin parity. The associative division algebras over $\mathbb{R}$ are $\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}$ (by the Frobenius theorem); these are exactly the entry algebras of GOE/GUE/GSE, and the Dyson index $\beta\in\{1,2,4\}$ is their real dimension. The list of three is the same list of three that classifies finite-dimensional real division algebras — a fact tying random matrices to the quaternions and octonions story.

2. Wigner's semicircle law

Wigner asked, in nuclear physics in the 1950s, what the typical density of eigenvalues of a "random" large symmetric matrix looks like. The answer — derived in his 1955 paper — is the semicircle.

Let $H$ be a real symmetric $N\times N$ matrix with i.i.d.\ entries above the diagonal, mean $0$, variance $1$, and finite higher moments. Form the rescaled matrix $H/\sqrt N$ and let $\mu_N$ be the empirical spectral measure

$\mu_N \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{\lambda_i(H/\sqrt N)}.$

Then $\mu_N \to \rho_{\mathrm{sc}}$ weakly almost surely, where

$\rho_{\mathrm{sc}}(x)\;=\;\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^2}\,\mathbf{1}_{[-2,2]}(x).$

The moment method

The cleanest proof is combinatorial. The $k$-th moment of $\mu_N$ is

$\int x^k\,d\mu_N(x)\;=\;\frac{1}{N}\tr\bigl((H/\sqrt N)^k\bigr)\;=\;\frac{1}{N^{1+k/2}}\sum_{i_1,\dots,i_k}H_{i_1 i_2}H_{i_2 i_3}\cdots H_{i_k i_1}.$

Odd $k$ contributes nothing in the limit (entries of mean zero). For even $k=2m$ the only walks $i_1\to i_2\to\cdots\to i_{2m}\to i_1$ that survive in the limit are non-crossing pair-matchings of the $2m$ edges — and these are counted by the Catalan number $C_m=\tfrac{1}{m+1}\binom{2m}{m}$. The result:

$\lim_{N\to\infty}\int x^{2m}\,d\mu_N(x)\;=\;C_m.$

The semicircle density $\rho_{\mathrm{sc}}$ has the moment-generating function $\sum C_m x^{2m}$, so by Carleman's moment-determinacy criterion $\mu_N\Rightarrow\rho_{\mathrm{sc}}$.

Beyond Gaussian. The proof via moments needed only that entries have mean zero, variance one, and finite higher moments. The limit law $\rho_{\mathrm{sc}}$ doesn't see any other feature of the entry distribution — the first whisper of universality.

3. Marchenko–Pastur and sample covariance

Wigner matrices model Hamiltonians. Sample covariance matrices are the statistician's bread and butter: given $n$ samples in $p$ dimensions, the empirical covariance $\hat\Sigma=\tfrac{1}{n}XX^\top$ is a $p\times p$ matrix whose eigenvalues drive PCA, regression, and discriminant analysis. The Marchenko–Pastur theorem (1967) is the universality law for $\hat\Sigma$.

Let $X$ be a $p\times n$ matrix with i.i.d.\ entries of mean $0$, variance $1$. The empirical spectral measure of $\tfrac{1}{n}XX^\top$ converges, in the limit $p,n\to\infty$ with $p/n\to c\in(0,\infty)$, to the Marchenko–Pastur distribution

$\rho_c(x)\;=\;\dfrac{1}{2\pi c x}\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}\,\mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}(x) \;+\; \max(0,\,1-1/c)\,\delta_0,\qquad a,b=(1\mp\sqrt c)^2.$

Phase transition at $c=1$

Three regimes by the ratio $c=p/n$:

  • $c<1$ (more samples than dimensions): bulk on $[(1-\sqrt c)^2,(1+\sqrt c)^2]$, soft edges, no zero eigenvalues.
  • $c=1$ (square): lower edge touches zero — the density has a $1/\sqrt x$ singularity at $0$. The classical "barely-invertible" regime.
  • $c>1$ (more dimensions than samples): the rank of $\hat\Sigma$ is $\le n

The atom at $0$ is not a smooth limit: it lives because the matrix is rank-deficient. Above it, the bulk still has the Marchenko–Pastur shape but on the shifted range $[(1-\sqrt c)^2,(1+\sqrt c)^2]$ (with $a=(1-\sqrt c)^2>0$ for $c>1$ as well).

Marchenko–Pastur explorer
drag $c=p/n$ across the phase transition

Yellow curve: theoretical MP density $\rho_c$. Cyan bars: empirical eigenvalue histogram of a single $p\times p$ sample covariance with $p=\max(\lfloor 240c\rfloor,40)$ and $n=240$. At $c>1$ note the dot at $0$ — the rank-deficiency point mass.

Statistical lesson. When $p/n$ is comparable to $1$, your sample covariance eigenvalues are not close to the population covariance eigenvalues. Even if the true covariance is identity, $\hat\Sigma$'s eigenvalues spread over $[(1-\sqrt c)^2,(1+\sqrt c)^2]$. Naïve $\hat\Sigma^{-1}$ in regression explodes; shrinkage (Ledoit–Wolf, ridge) is mandatory.

4. Tracy–Widom and the soft edge

The semicircle and Marchenko–Pastur tell us what bulk eigenvalues do. What about the largest? For a GUE matrix of size $N$, the bulk runs out at $\lambda \approx 2\sqrt N$. The fluctuations of $\lambda_{\max}$ around this edge are the heart of the soft-edge story.

The right scaling is

$\xi_N \;=\; N^{2/3}\bigl(\lambda_{\max}-2\sqrt N\bigr).$

Then $\xi_N\Rightarrow F_\beta$, the Tracy–Widom distribution of index $\beta$. The three classical $\beta$ values give three distributions $F_1,F_2,F_4$, characterised by a Fredholm-determinant identity:

$F_2(s)\;=\;\det\bigl(\mathbf{1}-\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{Ai}}\bigr)\big|_{[s,\infty)}\;=\;\exp\!\left(-\int_s^\infty (x-s)\,q(x)^2\,dx\right),$

where $q$ is the Hastings–McLeod solution of Painlevé II: $q''(x)=xq(x)+2q(x)^3,\quad q(x)\sim\mathrm{Ai}(x)$ as $x\to+\infty$. Mean $\approx -1.7711$, variance $\approx 0.8132$. The distribution is asymmetric: a longer left tail (eigenvalues drift inward), a shorter right tail (the edge is "soft" — eigenvalues do occasionally poke out far).

Universality beyond random matrices

Tracy–Widom shows up wherever a soft edge exists in integrable probability. A handful of famous examples:

  • Longest increasing subsequence (LIS). Baik–Deift–Johansson (1999): the LIS of a random permutation of $\{1,\dots,n\}$, properly scaled, is $F_2$. Last-passage percolation under exponential weights is the same statistic via RSK.
  • KPZ growth. The fluctuations of the height function of the totally asymmetric simple exclusion process (TASEP) — and of the KPZ equation itself — follow $F_2$ (curved initial data) or $F_1$ (flat initial data).
  • Polynuclear growth, Aztec diamonds, six-vertex models. The whole "KPZ universality class".
  • Statistics of largest eigenvalues in Wishart (sample covariance) matrices — the Bai–Yin / Johansson theorems put $F_1, F_2$ at the Marchenko–Pastur edges.
Tracy–Widom vs. Gaussian
three $\beta$ densities versus a standard normal

Densities are smooth pedagogical approximations to $f_\beta(s)=-F_\beta'(s)$ on $[-6, 4]$ — Gaussian bumps with $\exp(-cs^{3/2})$ / $\exp(-c|s|^3)$ tail corrections, matched to the canonical Painlevé-II Hastings–McLeod moments (mean $\approx -1.77$, variance $\approx 0.81$ for $\beta=2$). The largest eigenvalue is more likely to be smaller than expected than larger. This rules out naïve "$\lambda_{\max}$ is normal because it's an extreme statistic" intuitions inherited from Fisher–Tippett.

5. Free probability and free convolution

Random matrices are noncommutative random variables: matrix sums and products don't commute, and the spectral measure of $A+B$ has no closed-form relation to the spectral measures of $A$ and $B$ alone — unless those matrices are free. Voiculescu (1985) introduced freeness as the noncommutative analogue of classical independence.

Definition: freeness in a tracial $\ast$-algebra

Let $(\mathcal{A},\tau)$ be a unital $\ast$-algebra equipped with a state $\tau:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{C}$ (think: matrices with $\tau(M)=\tfrac{1}{N}\tr M$). Subalgebras $\mathcal{B}_1,\mathcal{B}_2\subset\mathcal{A}$ are free if for every alternating word $b_1 b_2\cdots b_k$ with $b_i\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon(i)}$, $\epsilon(i)\ne\epsilon(i+1)$, and $\tau(b_i)=0$ for all $i$:

$\tau(b_1 b_2\cdots b_k)\;=\;0.$

Compare with classical independence: $\mathbb{E}[X_1\cdots X_k]=\prod\mathbb{E}[X_i]$ for independent centred variables. Freeness is structurally different — alternation matters because the operators don't commute.

Asymptotic freeness of random matrices

Voiculescu's miracle (1991): let $A_N, B_N$ be two independent $N\times N$ GUE matrices, each rescaled to have spectral measure converging to a semicircle. Then $A_N$ and $B_N$ are asymptotically free: any normalised trace polynomial in $A_N, B_N$ converges, as $N\to\infty$, to the corresponding free-probabilistic quantity. Random matrices realise free probability concretely.

$R$-transform and free additive convolution

The role of the characteristic function (for classical independence) is played by Voiculescu's $R$-transform:

$R_\mu(z)\;=\;G_\mu^{\langle-1\rangle}(z)-\dfrac{1}{z},\qquad G_\mu(z)=\int\dfrac{d\mu(x)}{z-x}.$

Here $G_\mu$ is the Cauchy transform of $\mu$ and $G_\mu^{\langle-1\rangle}$ is its compositional inverse. The headline property: for $A,B$ free with spectral measures $\mu_A,\mu_B$, the spectral measure of $A+B$ is the free additive convolution $\mu_A\boxplus\mu_B$, and

$R_{\mu_A\boxplus\mu_B}(z)\;=\;R_{\mu_A}(z)+R_{\mu_B}(z).$

This linearises sums of free variables exactly as $\log\hat\varphi$ linearises sums of classical independents. The free CLT (Voiculescu): sums of i.i.d.\ free centred variables, properly normalised, converge to the semicircle — the free-probability analogue of the Gaussian.

Free additive convolution
$\mu_1\boxplus\mu_2$ for two semicircles of variable widths

For two semicircles $\rho_{r_i}(x)=\tfrac{2}{\pi r_i^2}\sqrt{r_i^2-x^2}$ on $[-r_i,r_i]$, the free additive convolution is the semicircle of radius $r=\sqrt{r_1^2+r_2^2}$ — exactly the rule that says $R$-transforms add. Compare to the classical convolution (dashed): two uniform-on-$[-r_i,r_i]$ would give a trapezoid; two centred Gaussians give a Gaussian of variance $\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2$. Semicircles are stable under $\boxplus$.

The dictionary. Free probability is to noncommutative probability what classical probability is to commutative probability. Independence $\to$ freeness; cumulants $\to$ free cumulants; Gaussian $\to$ semicircle; characteristic function $\to$ $R$-transform; Itô integral $\to$ free SDEs (free Brownian motion). Two parallel theories, sharing scaffolding but not theorems.

6. Bulk and edge universality

The semicircle law and Marchenko–Pastur depended on entries having mean $0$, variance $1$, and finite higher moments. Strikingly, they didn't depend on the distribution. Universality elevates this from a side remark to a theorem: not just the global density, but every local eigenvalue statistic of a Wigner ensemble depends on the entry distribution only through its first two moments.

The local Wigner law

Zoom in by a factor of $N$ on a point $E\in(-2,2)$ inside the bulk. The eigenvalues in a window of width $1/N$ form, after rescaling by the mean spacing $1/(N\rho_{\mathrm{sc}}(E))$, a determinantal point process with the sine kernel:

$K_{\sin}(x,y)\;=\;\dfrac{\sin\pi(x-y)}{\pi(x-y)}.$

For GUE this is Dyson's classical result (1962). For general Wigner matrices it was conjectured for decades and proved by Erdős–Schlein–Yau and independently Tao–Vu around 2010, via two complementary routes:

  • Dyson Brownian motion + local relaxation (Erdős–Schlein–Yau): if you smear a Wigner ensemble by a small Gaussian for a tiny time $t\sim N^{-1+\epsilon}$, local statistics relax to the GUE local statistics. Then a separate argument removes the smearing.
  • Four-moment method (Tao–Vu): if two Wigner ensembles match their first four moments, their local eigenvalue statistics are asymptotically identical. Match a Wigner matrix to GUE, transfer the answer.

Edge universality

The same story plays out at the soft edge: $N^{2/3}(\lambda_{\max}-2\sqrt N)$ converges to $F_\beta$ regardless of entry distribution (subject to a sub-Gaussian tail). The Tracy–Widom law is universal.

Universality is the moral CLT. Just as the CLT says the limit of $\bar X_N$ depends only on $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, the Wigner / Marchenko–Pastur / Tracy–Widom universality theorems say the limit of every local eigenvalue statistic depends only on the symmetry class $(\beta)$ and the variance. Distributional fingerprints wash out.

7. Katz–Sarnak: random matrices meet $L$-functions

The most arresting universality phenomenon in random matrix theory is not internal — it's a bridge to analytic number theory. The zeros of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line look exactly like eigenvalues of a random unitary matrix.

Montgomery's pair correlation

In 1972 Hugh Montgomery, computing the pair correlation function of the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ under GRH, found a function:

$R_2(\alpha)\;=\;1-\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\alpha}{\pi\alpha}\right)^2.$

Freeman Dyson, hearing the formula at tea in Princeton, recognised it instantly: it is the pair correlation of GUE eigenvalues. Andrew Odlyzko's massive numerical computations (1987 onward, eventually computing the $10^{20}$-th zero with high precision) confirmed the match to many decimal places.

Katz–Sarnak symmetry types

Nick Katz and Peter Sarnak (1999) elevated this from coincidence to conjecture: every natural family $\mathcal{F}$ of $L$-functions carries a symmetry type from the list

$\{\,U(N),\;O(N)^+,\;O(N)^-,\;\mathrm{USp}(2N)\,\},$

and the low-lying zeros of the family equidistribute against the eigenvalues of a Haar-random matrix in the corresponding compact group. The symmetry type is determined by symmetries of the family:

The symmetry type can usually be predicted from the family's monodromy group on the function-field side: replace $\mathbb{Q}$ by $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$, the $L$-functions become polynomials in $q^{-s}$, and the Frobenius conjugacy class in the geometric monodromy group dictates the matrix-ensemble analogue. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ this is a heuristic; over function fields it's a Deligne–Katz theorem.

What's known, what's conjectured. Over function fields the Katz–Sarnak symmetry assignment is a theorem (Katz–Sarnak; Deligne's equidistribution). Over $\mathbb{Q}$ it's a conjecture, but partial results are striking: the one-level density of the family of quadratic Dirichlet $L$-functions is symplectic for test functions supported in $[-1,1]$ (Özlük–Snyder, Rubinstein); for elliptic-curve $L$-functions the orthogonal-split-by-root-number prediction is supported by Iwaniec–Luo–Sarnak. Soundararajan's $\ge 87.5\%$ non-vanishing of $L(\tfrac12,\chi_d)$ is one of the headline arithmetic consequences. The dictionary in plain English: random matrices are not a metaphor for $L$-functions, they are the conjectured ensemble from which the Frobenius elements are drawn.

Connections

Random matrix theory is a hub: born in nuclear physics (Wigner), it has become the universal limit theory for spectral statistics across operator algebras, integrable probability, statistical mechanics, and analytic number theory. The frontier is the marriage of integrability (exactly solvable models like TASEP, last-passage percolation, the KPZ equation) with universality (Tracy–Widom appearing without a closed-form invariant). Free probability is fully alive too — Voiculescu's $\mathrm{Free}(F_n)$ free entropy, the question of whether free group factors $L(F_n)$ are isomorphic for different $n$, the program connecting free probability to large-deviation rate functions — and the Katz–Sarnak picture is still a conjecture over number fields, with concrete special cases proved one family at a time.

  • Probability theory — central limit theorem — the classical CLT is the bulk-of-the-line analogue of the semicircle for sums.
  • High-dimensional geometry — concentration applications — Marchenko–Pastur is the geometric backbone of compressed sensing and high-dimensional statistics.
  • Operator algebras — von Neumann algebras — free probability lives natively in tracial $\mathrm{II}_1$ factors; Voiculescu's free entropy is a $\mathrm{II}_1$-factor invariant.
  • Sato–Tate — the semicircle measure — Sato–Tate's semicircle density for Frobenius angles is the same semicircle, arising from $\mathrm{SU}(2)$-Haar measure on the unit interval.
  • Arithmetic statistics — symmetry types of $L$-function families — the Katz–Sarnak dictionary's number-theory side; Bhargava-style density theorems plug into the same machinery.
  • Spectral graph theory — Ramanujan graphs — Alon–Boppana shows random regular graphs have spectral measure close to Kesten–McKay, the graph analogue of the semicircle.
  • Large deviations — the rate function for eigenvalue fluctuations away from the semicircle is the logarithmic energy: random matrices are a 2D Coulomb gas.
