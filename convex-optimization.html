Convex optimization

A convex program minimises a convex function over a convex set — the local-to-global miracle is that any local minimum is global, and that every interesting structural question (existence, optimality conditions, sensitivity, algorithms) has a clean geometric answer. Duality reads the problem from both sides at once; conic programming packages LP, QP, SOCP, and SDP into a single template; interior-point methods make them polynomial-time; proximal and stochastic methods scale to the millions of variables that modern statistics demands.

1. Convex sets, functions, and the Legendre–Fenchel transform

A set $C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ is convex when every chord between two of its points stays inside: $tx+(1-t)y\in C$ for all $x,y\in C$ and $t\in[0,1]$. A function $f\colon\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}$ is convex iff its epigraph

$$\mathrm{epi}\,f \;=\; \{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1} : t\ge f(x)\}$$

is convex — equivalently, the chord from $(x,f(x))$ to $(y,f(y))$ lies above the graph: $f(tx+(1-t)y)\le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)$. This Jensen inequality is the entire definition; everything else (closedness of sublevel sets, continuity in the interior of the effective domain, monotonicity along rays from a minimiser) follows.

Supporting hyperplanes and the bipolar picture

A closed convex set $C\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ admits a supporting hyperplane $\{x : \langle a,x\rangle = \alpha\}$ at every boundary point, with the hyperplane on the outside: $\langle a,x\rangle\le\alpha$ for all $x\in C$. The miracle is that $C$ is recovered as the intersection of all such half-spaces. The function-side reflection of this — that a closed proper convex $f$ is the supremum of all affine minorants — is exactly

$$f^{**}(x) \;=\; f(x), \qquad f^*(y) \;:=\; \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^n}\,\bigl\{\langle y,x\rangle - f(x)\bigr\}.$$

The conjugate $f^*$ (the Legendre–Fenchel transform) lives in the dual $y$-space; the biconjugate theorem $f^{**}=f$ for closed proper convex $f$ is the analytic shadow of the supporting-hyperplane geometry. A short table of conjugate pairs is the dictionary of duality:

2. Subgradients and first-order optimality

For convex $f$, a vector $g\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is a subgradient at $x$ when

$$f(y) \;\ge\; f(x) + \langle g,\,y-x\rangle \qquad \text{for all } y\in\mathbb{R}^n.$$

Geometrically, $g$ is the slope of an affine minorant that touches the graph at $(x,f(x))$. The subdifferential $\partial f(x)$ is the set of all such slopes — a non-empty closed convex set in the interior of $\mathrm{dom}\,f$. At smooth points $\partial f(x) = \{\nabla f(x)\}$; at non-smooth points the subdifferential is a non-singleton.

The textbook example is $f(x)=|x|$ on $\mathbb{R}$: $\partial f(x)$ equals $\{+1\}$ for $x>0$, $\{-1\}$ for $x<0$, and the whole interval $[-1,+1]$ at the kink $x=0$. The kink "owns" every slope between the left and right derivatives.

The constrained optimality condition

Over a convex set $C$, the right generalisation of $\nabla f = 0$ is the variational inequality

$$\langle \nabla f(x^*),\,y - x^*\rangle \;\ge\; 0 \qquad \text{for all } y\in C,$$

equivalently $-\nabla f(x^*)\in N_C(x^*)$ where $N_C(x^*)=\{v : \langle v,y-x^*\rangle\le 0\;\forall y\in C\}$ is the outward normal cone. Combining the smooth and non-smooth halves:

$$0 \;\in\; \partial f(x^*) + N_C(x^*).$$

For unconstrained problems $N_C\equiv\{0\}$ and this collapses to $0\in\partial f(x^*)$.

3. Lagrangian duality and the KKT system

The constrained primal problem

$$\min_x f(x) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad g_i(x)\le 0\ (i=1..m), \quad h_j(x)=0\ (j=1..p)$$

has Lagrangian

$$L(x,\lambda,\nu) \;=\; f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j h_j(x), \qquad \lambda\ge 0.$$

The dual function $g(\lambda,\nu)=\inf_x L(x,\lambda,\nu)$ is always concave (an infimum of affine functions), and weak duality is the one-line bound $g(\lambda,\nu)\le p^*$ for every dual-feasible $(\lambda,\nu)$. The dual problem is $\max g(\lambda,\nu)$ s.t. $\lambda\ge 0$.

Slater's condition and strong duality

For convex programs (convex $f, g_i$; affine $h_j$), Slater's condition — existence of an $\tilde x$ with $g_i(\tilde x)<0$ for all non-affine $g_i$ and $h_j(\tilde x)=0$ — implies strong duality: $p^*=d^*$, with the dual optimum attained. This is what makes "solve the dual instead of the primal" a legitimate strategy.

The KKT conditions

Under Slater (and convexity), $x^*$ is primal-optimal iff there exist $\lambda^*\ge 0$, $\nu^*$ such that

  • Stationarity: $0 \in \partial f(x^*) + \sum\lambda_i^*\,\partial g_i(x^*) + \sum\nu_j^*\nabla h_j(x^*)$.
  • Primal feasibility: $g_i(x^*)\le 0$, $h_j(x^*)=0$.
  • Dual feasibility: $\lambda_i^*\ge 0$.
  • Complementary slackness: $\lambda_i^*\,g_i(x^*)=0$ — an inactive constraint ($g_i<0$) has zero multiplier; an active constraint ($g_i=0$) may have $\lambda_i>0$.

4. Conic programming: LP, QP, SOCP, SDP

Every convex program admits a conic standard form:

$$\min_x\, c^\top x \quad \text{s.t.} \quad Ax = b, \quad x\in K,$$

where $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ is a closed convex cone. As $K$ ranges over a sequence of self-dual cones we recover a strict hierarchy of model classes — and dramatic expressive power for a single algorithmic framework:

$\mathbb{R}^n_+$ (non-negative orthant)linearLPdiet, transportation
$\mathcal{L}^n=\{(x,t):\|x\|_2\le t\}$ (Lorentz)second-orderSOCProbust LS, portfolio risk
$\mathbb{S}^n_+$ (PSD matrices)semidefiniteSDPMAX-CUT, Lyapunov
+ exponential cone, power coneconic-exponential(generalised)entropy, geometric programming

The dual cone $K^*=\{y : \langle y,x\rangle\ge 0\;\forall x\in K\}$ controls the dual program — each of $\mathbb{R}^n_+$, $\mathcal{L}^n$, $\mathbb{S}^n_+$ is self-dual, which is why duality theory for LP/SOCP/SDP is so clean.

The Goemans–Williamson SDP relaxation of MAX-CUT

MAX-CUT — partition the vertex set of a weighted graph to maximise the weight of cut edges — is NP-hard. The integer programming formulation $\max \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}(1-y_iy_j)$ with $y_i\in\{\pm 1\}$ (so each cut edge contributes $w_{ij}$ and each uncut edge contributes $0$) is relaxed by lifting each $y_i$ to a unit vector $v_i\in S^{n-1}$:

$$\max \;\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}\,(1-v_i\!\cdot\!v_j), \quad \|v_i\|=1.$$

This is an SDP in the Gram matrix $X_{ij}=v_i\!\cdot\!v_j$ with $X\succeq 0$, $\mathrm{diag}(X)=1$. After solving, round via a uniformly random hyperplane: set $y_i=\mathrm{sign}(\langle v_i, r\rangle)$ for $r\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. The expected cut weight is at least

$$\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \;=\; \min_{\theta\in[0,\pi]} \frac{\theta/\pi}{(1-\cos\theta)/2} \;\approx\; 0.87856\ldots$$

times the SDP optimum — and under the Unique Games Conjecture this constant is the best polynomial-time approximation possible.

5. Gradient and proximal methods

For smooth convex $f$ with $L$-Lipschitz gradient, the basic gradient descent iteration $x_{k+1}=x_k-\alpha\,\nabla f(x_k)$ with $\alpha=1/L$ satisfies

$$f(x_k) - f^* \;\le\; \frac{L\,\|x_0-x^*\|^2}{2k} \;=\; O(1/k).$$

Adding strong convexity ($f-\tfrac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^2$ still convex) tightens this to geometric $O((1-\mu/L)^k)$. The condition number $\kappa=L/\mu$ controls the rate.

Nesterov's accelerated gradient (1983) introduces an extrapolation step

$$y_k = x_k + \tfrac{k-1}{k+2}(x_k - x_{k-1}), \qquad x_{k+1} = y_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(y_k),$$

and achieves $f(x_k)-f^* = O(1/k^2)$, matching the Nemirovski–Yudin lower bound for first-order methods on smooth convex problems. The momentum coefficient $\tfrac{k-1}{k+2}\to 1$ as $k\to\infty$; Nesterov-style methods "remember" past directions and ride them.

Proximal operators for non-smooth pieces

Many problems split as $\min f(x)+g(x)$ with $f$ smooth and $g$ non-smooth (e.g. $\|x\|_1$, an indicator of a constraint set, a nuclear norm). The proximal operator

$$\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}(y) \;=\; \arg\min_x \Bigl\{ g(x) + \tfrac{1}{2\alpha}\,\|x-y\|^2 \Bigr\}$$

steps in $y$ as if $g=0$, then resolves $g$ exactly. The composite step

$$x_{k+1} \;=\; \mathrm{prox}_{\alpha g}\bigl(x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)\bigr)$$

is the proximal gradient method (ISTA); accelerating it with Nesterov momentum gives FISTA, the workhorse of large-scale $\ell_1$-regularised regression. For $g=\lambda\|x\|_1$ the prox is component-wise soft-thresholding: $\mathrm{prox}_{\alpha\lambda\|\cdot\|_1}(y)_i = \mathrm{sign}(y_i)\,\max(|y_i|-\alpha\lambda, 0)$.

6. Interior-point methods and self-concordance

Interior-point methods (IPMs) solve $\min f(x)$ s.t. $x\in K$ by replacing the cone constraint with a barrier $B\colon\mathrm{int}\,K\to\mathbb{R}$ that diverges on $\partial K$, and minimising the perturbed problem

$$\min_x \;f(x) + \mu\,B(x)$$

at a sequence of barrier parameters $\mu\downarrow 0$. The minimisers $x(\mu)$ form a smooth central path in $\mathrm{int}\,K$ that converges to the constrained optimum. Newton's method, started at $x(\mu)$, finds $x(\mu')$ for the next barrier value in a small number of damped Newton steps — and that's where the polynomial-time guarantee comes from.

The classical examples:

  • Log-barrier on $\mathbb{R}^n_+$: $B(x) = -\sum_{i=1}^n \log x_i$. Self-concordance parameter $\nu = n$.
  • Lorentz cone $\mathcal{L}^n$: $B(x,t) = -\log(t^2 - \|x\|^2)$. $\nu = 2$.
  • PSD cone $\mathbb{S}^n_+$: $B(X) = -\log\det X$. $\nu = n$.

The structural condition that makes Newton's method work uniformly is self-concordance (Nesterov–Nemirovski): in one variable,

$$|\phi'''(x)| \;\le\; 2\,\phi''(x)^{3/2},$$

which makes the Newton decrement $\lambda(x)=\sqrt{\nabla\phi(x)^\top \nabla^2\phi(x)^{-1}\nabla\phi(x)}$ an affine-invariant measure of nearness-to-optimum. Damped Newton converges quadratically once $\lambda(x)<1/4$, and an outer loop on $\mu$ achieves $\varepsilon$-accuracy in $O(\sqrt{\nu}\,\log(1/\varepsilon))$ iterations. This is why LP, QP, SOCP, and SDP are all polynomial-time.

7. Stochastic and online convex optimization

When the objective is an expectation $f(x)=\mathbb{E}_\xi[F(x,\xi)]$ — e.g. an average loss over a giant training set — full-batch gradients become prohibitive. Stochastic gradient descent replaces $\nabla f$ with an unbiased estimate $\hat g_k$ (one sample, one mini-batch):

$$x_{k+1} \;=\; x_k - \alpha_k\,\hat g_k, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat g_k\mid x_k] = \nabla f(x_k).$$

For convex Lipschitz objectives, SGD with step $\alpha_k=O(1/\sqrt{k})$ and Polyak averaging $\bar x_T = \tfrac{1}{T}\sum x_k$ achieves

$$\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(\bar x_T)\bigr] - f^* \;=\; O(1/\sqrt{T}),$$

independent of dimension $n$ — the magic that powers modern machine learning.

Online convex optimization and regret

The online version: an adversary picks a convex loss $\ell_t$ at each round; the player commits to $x_t$ before seeing $\ell_t$; the goal is to make

$$R_T \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^T \ell_t(x_t) - \min_{x\in C}\sum_{t=1}^T \ell_t(x)$$

(the regret versus the best fixed action in hindsight) grow sublinearly. Online gradient descent $x_{t+1}=\Pi_C(x_t-\alpha\nabla\ell_t(x_t))$ achieves $R_T = O(\sqrt{T})$ — the analogue of SGD's bound for adversarial losses.

Mirror descent and the entropy regulariser

Mirror descent replaces the Euclidean projection step with a Bregman divergence:

$$x_{t+1} \;=\; \arg\min_{x\in C} \Bigl\{ \langle\nabla\ell_t(x_t),x\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\,D_\psi(x, x_t) \Bigr\}, \qquad D_\psi(x,y)=\psi(x)-\psi(y)-\langle\nabla\psi(y),x-y\rangle$$

for a strongly convex regulariser $\psi$. Two important instances:

  • $\psi(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|_2^2$: $D_\psi(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^2$ — recovers online gradient descent, regret $O(\sqrt{T})$ with dimension factor $\sqrt{n}$.
  • $\psi(x)=\sum x_i\log x_i$ on the simplex $\Delta_n$: $D_\psi$ is the KL divergence; the update becomes the multiplicative-weights / exponentiated-gradient rule $x_{t+1,i}\propto x_{t,i}\,e^{-\alpha\nabla\ell_t(x_t)_i}$ — regret $O(\sqrt{T\log n})$, exponentially better in $n$.

Choosing the regulariser to match the geometry of the feasible set is the central design move of mirror descent — and the reason boosting, multiplicative weights, follow-the-regularised-leader, and a dozen other algorithms all sit under one analytic umbrella.

8. Connections

Convex optimization sits at the geometric centre of modern applied mathematics: any problem with a convex relaxation gets a polynomial-time solver and a duality-based bound on the gap to the original. The active research frontier moves outward — beyond convexity (non-convex landscapes with benign geometry: deep networks, low-rank matrix recovery, phase retrieval), beyond first-order (variance-reduced SGD, second-order stochastic methods, distributed and federated optimisation), and beyond static optimization (online learning, control as optimisation, optimal transport as an LP on probability measures).

  • Combinatorial optimization — LP duality — the discrete analogue: TU constraint matrices make the LP relaxation integer-optimal, and SDP relaxations like Goemans–Williamson lift this to NP-hard problems.
  • Optimal control — Pontryagin's maximum principle — the same Lagrangian / Hamiltonian structure runs in continuous time, with the co-state $\lambda(t)$ playing the role of a multiplier on the dynamical constraint $\dot x = f(x,u)$.
  • Mathematical finance — Merton portfolio — utility maximisation under stochastic dynamics; the HJB equation is an infinite-dimensional analogue of the KKT stationarity condition.
  • Variational methods — Euler–Lagrange — the calculus of variations is convex optimization on function spaces; coercivity and lower semicontinuity replace boundedness and continuity, but the duality theme survives via Fenchel–Rockafellar.
  • Functional analysis — Krein–Milman — the Hahn–Banach separation theorem is the analytic source of every supporting-hyperplane argument; LP / SOCP / SDP duality is its finite-dimensional shadow.
  • Information theory — entropy is the Legendre conjugate of the log-moment-generating function; relative entropy is a Bregman divergence; the Csiszár–Sanov picture and large-deviations rate functions are convex-analytic to their core.
