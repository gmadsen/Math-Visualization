Kähler geometry

Complex manifolds whose Hermitian metric carries a closed $(1,1)$-form. Three categories of geometry — Riemannian, complex, and symplectic — fuse into one, and that fusion forces Hodge theory, hard Lefschetz, and Yau's theorem.

1. Almost complex structure and integrability

Every smooth manifold has a tangent bundle. To put complex geometry on it we ask for a fibrewise multiplication-by-$i$: a smooth bundle endomorphism $J\colon TM \to TM$ with $J^2 = -\mathrm{id}$. Such a $J$ is called an almost complex structure; the pair $(M, J)$ is an almost complex manifold. Real dimension must be even.

An almost complex $J$ does not yet make $M$ a complex manifold — it only does so on each tangent space, point by point. To get honest holomorphic charts we need integrability. Complexify: $TM \otimes \mathbb{C} = T^{1,0} \oplus T^{0,1}$ where $T^{1,0}$ is the $+i$-eigenspace of $J$ and $T^{0,1}$ is its conjugate. This induces a bigrading $$\Omega^k(M;\mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \Omega^{p,q}(M)$$ on complex differential forms. The exterior derivative splits as $d = \partial + \bar\partial$ if and only if $J$ is integrable.

Newlander–Nirenberg (1957). $J$ comes from a system of holomorphic charts iff the Nijenhuis tensor $$N_J(X,Y) = [X,Y] + J[JX,Y] + J[X,JY] - [JX,JY]$$ vanishes identically. Equivalently $\bar\partial^2 = 0$, equivalently $T^{0,1}$ is closed under Lie bracket. The Nijenhuis tensor is the only obstruction.

Why we will assume integrability. Throughout the rest of the page $M$ will be a complex manifold of complex dimension $n$, i.e. an integrable almost complex structure. So $\partial$ and $\bar\partial$ are honest first-order operators with $\partial^2 = \bar\partial^2 = 0$, and Dolbeault cohomology $H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(M)$ is well-defined.

2. Hermitian metric and the Kähler form

Now layer a metric on top. A Hermitian metric on $(M,J)$ is a Riemannian metric $h$ that is compatible with $J$: $$h(JX, JY) = h(X,Y) \quad\text{for all }X,Y \in TM.$$ Equivalently, $h$ extended $\mathbb{C}$-bilinearly is a positive Hermitian form on $T^{1,0}M$.

The combination of $h$ and $J$ packages into a single real $(1,1)$-form, the fundamental form: $$\omega(X,Y) = h(JX, Y).$$ Antisymmetry of $\omega$ follows from compatibility, $\omega \in \Omega^{1,1}(M;\mathbb{R})$, and non-degeneracy is automatic. So far this is just the Hermitian data rewritten.

Kähler condition. The triple $(M,J,h)$ is Kähler when $\omega$ is closed: $$d\omega = 0.$$ This single equation is equivalent, individually, to each of the following:
  • $\nabla J = 0$ for the Levi-Civita connection (parallel transport commutes with $J$);
  • locally $\omega = i\,\partial\bar\partial K$ for some smooth real-valued Kähler potential $K$;
  • the holonomy of $h$ lies in $U(n) \subset O(2n)$.
Three conditions on three different geometric structures collapse into one closed real $(1,1)$-form.

Examples.

  • $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $h$ the standard Euclidean metric: $\omega = \tfrac{i}{2}\sum dz_j\wedge d\bar z_j$, with potential $K = \tfrac{1}{2}|z|^2$.
  • $\mathbb{CP}^n$ with the Fubini–Study metric: $\omega_{FS} = \tfrac{i}{2\pi}\partial\bar\partial \log(1 + |z|^2)$ in an affine chart. Closed because $\partial\bar\partial$ kills $d$, and globally well-defined because $\log|f|$ for transition functions is harmonic.
  • Every Riemann surface ($n=1$): $\omega$ is a $2$-form on a real surface, automatically closed.
  • Any flat torus $\mathbb{C}^n/\Lambda$, descending the Euclidean form.
  • Submanifolds and products of Kähler manifolds are Kähler.

Compact non-Kähler complex manifolds exist but are special. The Hopf surface $S^1 \times S^3$ is complex (a quotient of $\mathbb{C}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ by $z \mapsto 2z$) but $b_2(S^1\times S^3) = 0$, so no nondegenerate closed $(1,1)$-form is available.

3. Kähler identities and the Hodge decomposition

The Kähler condition transforms into a torrent of identities relating the operators $d, \partial, \bar\partial$ to their formal adjoints $d^*, \partial^*, \bar\partial^*$ and to the Lefschetz operator $L = \omega \wedge -$ and its adjoint $\Lambda$ (contraction with $\omega$). The fundamental Kähler identities are: $$[\Lambda, \partial] = i\,\bar\partial^*, \qquad [\Lambda, \bar\partial] = -i\,\partial^*.$$ Both are pointwise identities of Hermitian linear algebra and in fact hold on any Hermitian manifold — the Kähler hypothesis $d\omega = 0$ is not used to derive them. The Kähler condition enters at the next step: only when $d\omega = 0$ do the operators interact cleanly enough to collapse the three Laplacians.

From these come the three Laplacians: $$\Delta_d = dd^* + d^*d, \qquad \Delta_\partial = \partial\partial^* + \partial^*\partial, \qquad \Delta_{\bar\partial} = \bar\partial\bar\partial^* + \bar\partial^*\bar\partial,$$ and the Kähler identities collapse them all together: $$\boxed{\;\Delta_d = 2\Delta_\partial = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}\;}$$ on a Kähler manifold. (On a general Hermitian manifold these three are unrelated.)

Compactness now does the rest. Hodge theory says that on a compact Riemannian manifold, every de Rham class has a unique harmonic representative $\Delta_d \alpha = 0$. Because $\Delta_d$ commutes with the bigrading on a Kähler manifold (it is a multiple of $\Delta_{\bar\partial}$, which manifestly preserves $(p,q)$), harmonic forms decompose by type. The result is the Hodge decomposition:

$$H^k(M;\mathbb{C}) \;=\; \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M), \qquad H^{p,q}(M) \cong H^q(M, \Omega^p_M).$$

Two symmetries are forced.

  • Hodge symmetry. Complex conjugation gives $\overline{H^{p,q}} = H^{q,p}$, so $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$ where $h^{p,q} = \dim_\mathbb{C} H^{p,q}$.
  • Serre duality. The Hodge $*$ operator pairs $H^{p,q}$ with $H^{n-p,n-q}$, so $h^{p,q} = h^{n-p,n-q}$.

Together these halve, then quarter, the Hodge diamond.

4. Lefschetz operator and hard Lefschetz

The Kähler form $\omega$ acts on differential forms by wedging: $$L\colon \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+2}(M), \qquad L(\alpha) = \alpha \wedge \omega.$$ Closedness $d\omega = 0$ means $L$ descends to cohomology. Its adjoint $\Lambda = L^*$ contracts against $\omega$ — the operators $L, \Lambda$, and the degree-counting operator $H = [L,\Lambda]$ satisfy the commutation relations of $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{R})$. So there is an $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-action on $H^*(M;\mathbb{R})$, and ordinary $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-representation theory takes over.

Hard Lefschetz theorem. For a compact Kähler manifold $M$ of complex dimension $n$, and for each $0 \le k \le n$, the iterated Lefschetz map $$L^k\colon H^{n-k}(M;\mathbb{R}) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } H^{n+k}(M;\mathbb{R})$$ is an isomorphism. Equivalently, the $\mathfrak{sl}_2$-action on $H^*(M;\mathbb{R})$ exhibits $H^{n-k}$ and $H^{n+k}$ as the two boundary weight spaces of a finite-dimensional representation.

Define primitive cohomology in degree $n-k$ as $$P^{n-k} = \ker\bigl(L^{k+1}\colon H^{n-k}(M;\mathbb{R}) \to H^{n+k+2}(M;\mathbb{R})\bigr).$$ The Lefschetz decomposition writes every cohomology class uniquely as a sum of primitive classes wedged with powers of $\omega$: $$H^m(M;\mathbb{R}) = \bigoplus_{k \ge \max(0,m-n)} L^k\,P^{m-2k}.$$ Combined with Hodge theory this is the source of the Hodge–Riemann bilinear relations, which polarise every primitive Hodge structure.

5. Ricci-flat Kähler and the Calabi conjecture

On a Kähler manifold the Ricci tensor of the Levi-Civita connection is symmetric and $J$-invariant, so it pairs with $J$ into a real $(1,1)$-form, the Ricci form: $$\rho(\omega)(X,Y) = \mathrm{Ric}(JX, Y).$$ A miracle: $\rho(\omega)$ is closed, and its de Rham class is independent of the chosen Kähler metric. Chern–Weil then identifies it with a topological invariant: $$[\rho(\omega)] = 2\pi c_1(M) \in H^{1,1}(M;\mathbb{R}).$$

So a Ricci-flat Kähler metric ($\rho \equiv 0$) is only possible if $c_1(M) = 0$. The first Chern class is a discrete topological invariant; vanishing of it is a necessary condition. Calabi conjectured in 1954 that it is also sufficient:

Calabi conjecture (Yau, 1977). Let $M$ be a compact Kähler manifold with $c_1(M) = 0$. Then every Kähler class on $M$ contains a unique Ricci-flat Kähler metric.

Yau's proof solves a complex Monge–Ampère equation $$(\omega + i\,\partial\bar\partial \varphi)^n = e^f \omega^n$$ with $\omega + i\,\partial\bar\partial\varphi$ positive — uniqueness is straightforward; existence requires the analytic heavy lifting. The Calabi–Yau manifolds produced this way are the geometric backbone of mirror symmetry, string compactifications, and special holonomy.

Examples and dimensions.

  • $n=1$: elliptic curves $\mathbb{C}/\Lambda$. Flat metric is already Ricci-flat.
  • $n=2$: K3 surfaces (e.g. a smooth quartic in $\mathbb{CP}^3$). Yau's metric is famously not explicit — only its existence is known.
  • $n=3$: the quintic threefold $\{f_5(x_0,\dots,x_4) = 0\} \subset \mathbb{CP}^4$, with $h^{1,1}=1$, $h^{2,1}=101$, $\chi = -200$.

For more on the structure of Calabi–Yau manifolds, mirror symmetry, and string compactifications, see calabi-yau-manifolds.html.

6. Kähler–Einstein and K-stability

Generalising Ricci-flat: a Kähler–Einstein metric satisfies $$\mathrm{Ric}(\omega) = c\,\omega$$ for a constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$. Taking cohomology classes, $2\pi c_1(M) = c\,[\omega]$, so the sign of $c$ matches the sign of $c_1(M)$.

$c_1 < 0$general type / canonically polarisedunconditionalAubin (1976), Yau (1977/78)
$c_1 = 0$Calabi–YauunconditionalYau (1977)
$c_1 > 0$Fanoiff K-polystableChen–Donaldson–Sun (2015)

The Fano case $c > 0$ is the hard one. Matsushima (1957) and Futaki (1983) found obstructions to existence — any Kähler–Einstein Fano must have reductive automorphism group and vanishing Futaki invariant. So for example the blow-up of $\mathbb{CP}^2$ at one or two points is Fano but admits no Kähler–Einstein metric.

The Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture, formulated in stages from the 1990s, proposes the precise replacement: a Fano manifold $X$ admits a Kähler–Einstein metric iff $X$ is K-polystable. K-polystability is an algebro-geometric condition: roughly, every test configuration $\mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{A}^1$ with central fibre a normal $\mathbb{Q}$-Fano variety has non-negative Donaldson–Futaki invariant, with equality only for trivial test configurations.

The conjecture was proved by Chen–Donaldson–Sun in 2015 using a continuity-method approach with Cheeger–Colding–Tian limits. The proof opened the door to using algebraic geometry to construct canonical metrics — and conversely, to using metric geometry to construct moduli of Fano varieties (the K-moduli program of Li–Wang–Xu and others).

One picture. The Fubini–Study metric on $\mathbb{CP}^n$ is Kähler–Einstein with $\mathrm{Ric}(\omega_{FS}) = (n+1)\,\omega_{FS}$. Its existence is "obvious" (homogeneity); but the Yau–Tian–Donaldson theorem says that if you blow up enough points in $\mathbb{CP}^n$ — past the K-stability threshold — the resulting Fano can stop admitting any KE metric, and the obstruction is detected purely by test configurations.

Connections

Kähler geometry is one of the most fertile crossroads in mathematics: it is where Riemannian geometry, complex analysis, symplectic topology, and algebraic geometry collapse to a common subject. Active frontiers include the K-moduli program for Fano varieties, the SYZ conjecture identifying mirror Calabi–Yau pairs as dual special-Lagrangian fibrations, and non-Kähler complex geometry — Hermitian metrics with prescribed torsion, Strominger systems, and pluriclosed flow — where the closedness of $\omega$ is relaxed in controlled directions.

  • Hodge theory — Hodge decomposition — Kähler is the geometric input that makes the Hodge decomposition theorem run; Hodge theory abstracts away the metric and keeps only the bigrading.
  • Calabi–Yau manifolds — Ricci-flat Kähler — the special Kähler class with $c_1 = 0$, where Yau's theorem promotes a topological condition to a unique Ricci-flat metric.
  • Riemannian geometry — Ricci curvature — Kähler–Einstein conditions specialise the Einstein equations to manifolds where the metric is built from a single potential.
  • Symplectic manifolds — every Kähler manifold is symplectic with $\omega$ the symplectic form; the converse fails (Thurston's Kodaira–Thurston manifold).
  • Mirror symmetry — mirror pairs of Calabi–Yau threefolds swap $h^{1,1}$ and $h^{2,1}$, exchanging Kähler (symplectic) data with complex-structure data.
