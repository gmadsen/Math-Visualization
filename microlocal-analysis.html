Microlocal analysis

Refines the singular support of a distribution from where it fails to be smooth to where × in which direction: the wavefront set lives on $T^*\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0$, pseudodifferential and Fourier integral operators move it by Hamiltonian flow, and the resulting calculus replaces ad-hoc PDE arguments with one geometric language.

1. The wavefront set: where × in which direction

For a distribution $u\in\mathcal{D}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$ the singular support $\mathrm{singsupp}(u)$ records the points $x_0$ near which $u$ fails to be $C^\infty$. Two distributions can have the same singular support yet behave differently along it: the step on $\{x_1=0\}$ and the surface measure on the unit sphere both fail to be smooth on a hypersurface, but their non-smooth content points in different directions in frequency space. Hörmander's wavefront set records exactly that direction.

The definition is phase-space localization. Pick a cutoff $\phi\in C^\infty_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with $\phi(x_0)\ne 0$. The product $\phi u$ is a compactly supported distribution; its Fourier transform $\widehat{\phi u}(\xi)$ is smooth and tempered. Say $(x_0,\xi_0)$ is a direction of regularity when there exists such a cutoff and an open cone $V\ni\xi_0$ in $\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0$ on which $\widehat{\phi u}$ decays faster than any polynomial: for every $N$, $|\widehat{\phi u}(\xi)|\le C_N(1+|\xi|)^{-N}$ as $\xi\in V$ goes to infinity. The complement

$$\mathrm{WF}(u) \;=\; \{(x_0,\xi_0)\in T^*\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0 \;:\; \xi_0\text{ is not a direction of regularity for }u\text{ at }x_0\}$$

is a closed conic subset of $T^*\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0$. Its projection onto the base is exactly $\mathrm{singsupp}(u)$, but the fiber over each base point — a cone in $\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0$ — sees direction.

Three diagnostic examples set the geometry:

  • $u=\delta_0$: $\widehat{\phi\delta_0}=\phi(0)$ is constant, so it has no rapid decay in any direction. $\mathrm{WF}(\delta_0)=\{(0,\xi):\xi\ne 0\}$ — singular at one point, in every direction.
  • $u=\mathbf{1}_{x_1>0}$ (Heaviside on a hyperplane): the Fourier transform is concentrated near $\xi_2=\cdots=\xi_n=0$. $\mathrm{WF}(u)$ is the conormal bundle of $\{x_1=0\}$ (the cotangent vectors that annihilate every tangent direction to the hyperplane — here the covectors $\xi\propto e_1$ at every base point on $\{x_1=0\}$); singular only along the hyperplane, only in the direction perpendicular to it.
  • $u\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$: $\widehat{\phi u}$ is Schwartz everywhere, $\mathrm{WF}(u)=\emptyset$. The wavefront set is empty exactly when $u$ is smooth.

Two structural facts shape the rest of the theory. First, $\mathrm{WF}(u)$ is invariant under multiplication by smooth functions and under linear changes of variable, so it is intrinsic to the manifold setting and lifts to $T^*M\setminus 0$ for $M$ smooth. Second, smoothing is microlocal: a $C^\infty$ kernel can erase $\mathrm{WF}(u)$ at any prescribed cone, and a non-smoothing operator can only shrink the wavefront set, never grow it (the basic inclusion $\mathrm{WF}(Au)\subset\mathrm{WF}(u)$ for any pseudodifferential $A$ in §3).

2. Pseudodifferential operators and the symbol calculus

A linear differential operator $P=\sum_{|\alpha|\le m} a_\alpha(x)D^\alpha$, with $D^\alpha=(-i\partial)^\alpha$, can be rewritten via the Fourier inversion formula as

$$Pu(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{(2\pi)^n}\int e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,p(x,\xi)\,\hat u(\xi)\,d\xi,\qquad p(x,\xi)\;=\;\sum_{|\alpha|\le m} a_\alpha(x)\,\xi^\alpha.$$

The function $p(x,\xi)$ is a polynomial of degree $m$ in $\xi$ — the full symbol of $P$. Pseudodifferential operators (ΨDOs) generalize this construction to arbitrary symbols: the Hörmander class $S^m$ (with $m\in\mathbb{R}$) consists of $C^\infty$ functions $p(x,\xi)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n\times\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying the symbol estimates

$$\bigl|\,\partial^\alpha_x\,\partial^\beta_\xi\, p(x,\xi)\,\bigr| \;\le\; C_{\alpha\beta}\,(1+|\xi|)^{m-|\beta|}\qquad\text{for all multi-indices }\alpha,\beta.$$

Each $\xi$-derivative drops the order by one; differentiating in $x$ does not. A symbol is classical if it admits an asymptotic expansion $p\sim p_m + p_{m-1}+\cdots$ where $p_{m-j}\in S^{m-j}$ is positively homogeneous of degree $m-j$ in $\xi$ for $|\xi|$ large. The leading term $p_m$ is the principal symbol; it transforms as a function on $T^*\mathbb{R}^n$ under coordinate changes (the lower-order pieces depend on the choice of quantization — Egorov's theorem, an FIO-conjugation statement, gives the correction terms when needed).

The basic calculus runs on three rules. Composition: if $P\in\Psi^{m_1}$ and $Q\in\Psi^{m_2}$ then $PQ\in\Psi^{m_1+m_2}$ with principal symbol $p_{m_1}q_{m_2}$ (full symbol $p\#q$ given by the asymptotic expansion $p\#q\sim\sum_\alpha (i^{|\alpha|}/\alpha!)\,\partial^\alpha_\xi p\,\partial^\alpha_x q$, with each successive term landing one order lower). Adjoint: $P^*\in\Psi^m$ with principal symbol $\overline{p_m}$. Mapping: every $P\in\Psi^m$ is bounded $H^s\to H^{s-m}$ for every $s$, where $H^s$ is the $L^2$-Sobolev scale. Differential operators have polynomial symbols and trivially satisfy the estimates; the larger ΨDO class is closed under inversion modulo smoothing (a symbol that is invertible at infinity admits an asymptotic inverse), which is what makes parametrices possible.

3. Microlocal regularity and propagation of singularities

Pseudodifferential operators interact with the wavefront set in two complementary ways. The trivial direction is the microlocal pseudo-locality bound

$$\mathrm{WF}(Pu) \;\subset\; \mathrm{WF}(u),$$

which is true for every $P\in\Psi^m$: a ΨDO can shrink the wavefront set (smooth out singularities) but never grow new ones. The non-trivial direction needs ellipticity. Define the characteristic variety and elliptic set of $P$ inside $T^*\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0$ (the cotangent bundle with the zero section removed, so $p_m$'s vanishing on $\xi=0$ doesn't trivially fill Char):

$$\mathrm{Char}(P) \;=\; \{(x,\xi)\in T^*\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0\,:\, p_m(x,\xi)=0\},\qquad \mathrm{Ell}(P) \;=\; (T^*\mathbb{R}^n\setminus 0)\setminus \mathrm{Char}(P).$$

Microlocal elliptic regularity says $\mathrm{WF}(u)\cap\mathrm{Ell}(P)\subset\mathrm{WF}(Pu)$: at any point where $P$ is invertible at top order, $u$ inherits the smoothness of $Pu$. Equivalently, $\mathrm{WF}(u)\setminus\mathrm{WF}(Pu)\subset\mathrm{Char}(P)$ — singularities of $u$ that survive applying $P$ must live on the characteristic variety.

The deep statement, Hörmander's propagation theorem, governs how those characteristic singularities move. The principal symbol $p_m$ is a function on $T^*\mathbb{R}^n$; its Hamiltonian vector field is

$$X_{p_m} \;=\; \sum_j\Bigl(\frac{\partial p_m}{\partial\xi_j}\partial_{x_j} - \frac{\partial p_m}{\partial x_j}\partial_{\xi_j}\Bigr),$$

and the integral curves of $X_{p_m}$ inside $\mathrm{Char}(P)$ are the null bicharacteristics. The theorem: if $p_m$ is real, the set $\mathrm{WF}(u)\setminus\mathrm{WF}(Pu)$ is invariant under the bicharacteristic flow inside $\mathrm{Char}(P)$. Singularities of solutions of $Pu=f$ propagate along null bicharacteristics.

The wave operator $\Box=-\partial_t^2+\Delta_x$ has principal symbol $p_2(t,x;\tau,\xi)=-\tau^2+|\xi|^2$. Its characteristic variety $\{\tau^2=|\xi|^2\}$ is the light cone in cotangent fibers, and the bicharacteristics project to null geodesics in spacetime — light rays. So Hörmander's theorem is the rigorous content of "wave fronts travel at the speed of light": a sharp edge in the initial data propagates exactly along the null geodesic launched in its conormal direction. The singular-support drawing one would draw with d'Alembert's formula coincides with the projection of the wavefront set under this flow.

4. Elliptic regularity via the parametrix

An operator $P\in\Psi^m$ is elliptic at $(x_0,\xi_0)$ when $p_m(x_0,\xi_0)\ne 0$; uniformly elliptic on $\Omega$ when this holds for every $(x_0,\xi_0)\in T^*\Omega\setminus 0$. The Laplacian's principal symbol is $p_2(x,\xi)=-|\xi|^2$, so $-\Delta$ is uniformly elliptic on every open set: $|\xi|^2$ vanishes only on the zero section, which we have already excluded.

The parametrix construction is the workhorse. If $P$ is elliptic with principal symbol $p_m\ne 0$, one builds a ΨDO $Q\in\Psi^{-m}$ — the parametrix — whose principal symbol is $1/p_m$, by extending $1/p_m$ smoothly off the zero section and inverting order-by-order using the composition expansion. The parametrix satisfies

$$QP \;=\; I + R_1,\qquad PQ \;=\; I + R_2,$$

where $R_1,R_2\in\Psi^{-\infty}$ are smoothing operators. Smoothing operators map $H^s\to C^\infty$ for every $s$ — they have $C^\infty$ Schwartz kernel, equivalently their symbols are Schwartz in $\xi$.

From $Pu=f$ multiply by $Q$: $u + R_1 u = Q f$, hence $u=Qf - R_1 u$. The right-hand side is as smooth as the data: $Qf\in H^{s+m}$ when $f\in H^s$ (because $Q\in\Psi^{-m}$), and $R_1 u\in C^\infty$. Iterating ($R_1$ of $R_1 u$ is also smoothing, but already $C^\infty$) gives the elliptic regularity theorem:

$$f\in H^s_{\mathrm{loc}}\;\Longrightarrow\; u\in H^{s+m}_{\mathrm{loc}}.$$

For $-\Delta u=f$ this is the classical statement $f\in H^s\Rightarrow u\in H^{s+2}$. Microlocally: $\mathrm{WF}(u)=\mathrm{WF}(Pu)$ on the elliptic set. Where $P$ is non-elliptic — e.g. on the characteristic variety $\tau^2=|\xi|^2$ of the wave operator — the parametrix breaks down and singularities can survive: that is what makes hyperbolic problems propagate signals while elliptic problems trap singularities at sources. The parametrix is also the launching point for index theory: $P:H^s\to H^{s-m}$ is Fredholm exactly on the symbol level, and $\mathrm{ind}(P)$ is computable from $p_m$ via Atiyah-Singer.

5. Fourier integral operators and Lagrangian distributions

Pseudodifferential operators arise from the special phase $\phi(x,y,\xi)=(x-y)\cdot\xi$. Replacing this with a more general phase function gives the larger class of Fourier integral operators (FIOs):

$$Au(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{(2\pi)^N}\int e^{i\phi(x,y,\xi)}\,a(x,y,\xi)\,u(y)\,dy\,d\xi,$$

where $a\in S^m$ and $\phi$ is a non-degenerate phase function — homogeneous of degree 1 in $\xi$, with $d\phi\ne 0$ and the differentials $d(\partial\phi/\partial\xi_j)$ linearly independent on the critical set $C_\phi=\{(x,y,\xi):\partial_\xi\phi=0\}$. The pair $(\phi,a)$ quantizes a Lagrangian submanifold $\Lambda\subset T^*X\times T^*Y$ defined by

$$\Lambda \;=\; \bigl\{\,(x,d_x\phi;\,y,-d_y\phi)\,:\,(x,y,\xi)\in C_\phi\,\bigr\}.$$

The non-degeneracy condition makes $\Lambda$ a Lagrangian — half-dimensional with the natural symplectic form $\omega_{T^*X}\ominus\omega_{T^*Y}$ vanishing on it. Distributions of the form $Au$ for $u\in C^\infty_c$ are Lagrangian distributions with WF contained in $\Lambda$; equivalently, FIOs propagate $\mathrm{WF}$ along the canonical relation: $\mathrm{WF}(Au)\subset \Lambda\circ \mathrm{WF}(u)$ as a composition of relations on $T^*Y\to T^*X$. When $\Lambda$ happens to be the graph of a symplectomorphism $\chi$ this collapses to the function $\chi(\mathrm{WF}(u))$; for general $\Lambda$ (e.g. the Radon transform of §6) the inclusion is genuinely set-valued.

Two structural perks:

  • FIO ↔ canonical transformation. When $\Lambda$ is the graph of a symplectomorphism $\chi:T^*Y\to T^*X$, FIOs quantize $\chi$. Composition of FIOs whose canonical relations compose transversally gives another FIO with canonical relation the composition $\Lambda_1\circ\Lambda_2$. ΨDOs are the special case $\chi=\mathrm{id}$, $\Lambda=\Delta_{T^*X}$.
  • Solutions of evolution equations are FIOs. For a real-principal-symbol $P$ of order 1 like $\partial_t-A(x,D_x)$ with $a$ real, the half-wave propagator $e^{itA}$ is an FIO whose canonical relation is the time-$t$ Hamiltonian flow of the principal symbol of $A$. Solutions of $\Box u = 0$ split as a sum of two FIOs (forward and backward half-waves), explaining geometric optics, the Maslov correction at caustics (the half-integer phase shift FIOs pick up each time the projection $\pi:\Lambda\to X$ becomes singular — caustics are the projected fold loci), and the WKB ansatz $a(x,t;\xi)e^{i\phi(x,t;\xi)}$ (a high-frequency asymptotic with slowly-varying amplitude $a$ and rapidly oscillating phase $\phi$) as the leading term of an FIO expansion.

6. Scattering, Radon transforms, and inverse problems

Microlocal analysis ships as a uniform language for problems that earlier needed a different ad-hoc tool per setting. Three flagship arenas:

Scattering theory. The reduced wave equation $(\Delta+k^2)u=f$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ has principal symbol $-|\xi|^2+k^2$ — characteristic on the sphere $|\xi|=k$ — so it is non-elliptic and its parametrix breaks. Existence of outgoing solutions requires the Sommerfeld radiation condition $r^{(n-1)/2}(\partial_r-ik)u\to 0$ as $r\to\infty$, which microlocally selects the half of the characteristic variety corresponding to outgoing bicharacteristics. Hörmander's propagation theorem governs how singularities (geometric-optics rays) reach infinity, and the resulting limiting absorption principle gives existence + uniqueness of the scattering solution. The same machinery delivers Strichartz estimates and dispersive bounds for $e^{it\sqrt{-\Delta}}$.

X-ray and Radon transforms. The Radon transform $Rf(L)=\int_L f$ over lines (or hyperplanes) inverts to recover $f$ from its line integrals — the mathematics of CT scanning. Microlocal analysis tells you exactly which singularities are recoverable: $R$ is an FIO whose canonical relation maps $\mathrm{WF}(f)$ at $(x,\xi)$ to lines through $x$ perpendicular to $\xi$. So a singularity of $f$ at $x$ in direction $\xi$ is detected only by the family of lines $L$ with $x\in L$ and $L\perp\xi$: missing data along that family produces a missing-cone artifact in reconstruction. The visible singularities for limited-angle CT are precisely those $(x,\xi)$ whose required lines lie in the measured set; this is the microlocal limited-data theorem of Quinto, Greenleaf-Uhlmann, and others.

Inverse problems and general relativity. The Calderón problem (recover conductivity from boundary measurements) and the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map at the boundary are studied with FIO + parametrix machinery. In general relativity, propagation of singularities across a Cauchy horizon is governed by Hörmander's theorem applied to the Lorentzian wave operator $\Box_g$: gravitational waves carry singularities of initial data along null geodesics of the metric, and the resulting smoothing/non-smoothing dichotomy underlies the strong cosmic censorship conjecture.

Connections

Microlocal analysis is the geometric refactor that the second half of the 20th century brought to PDE: every "wavefronts travel along characteristics" hand-wave becomes a precise statement on $T^*M\setminus 0$, and every parametrix construction gets a uniform symbol calculus. Open frontiers include semiclassical analysis (the $\hbar\to 0$ regime, where $\xi$-derivatives are weighted by $\hbar$ and the propagation theorem connects to quantum chaos and trace formulas), the analytic-wavefront refinement (decay below $C_N(1+|\xi|)^{-N}$ tightened to exponential decay, controlling Gevrey and analytic regularity), and FBI-Bargmann transforms / second microlocalization for problems where the standard wavefront set is too coarse. On the inverse-problem side, microlocal techniques drive ongoing work on limited-data tomography, Calderón-style boundary determination, and the propagation of gravitational-wave singularities across Cauchy horizons.

  • Sobolev spaces & distributions — supplies the distribution-theoretic substrate $\mathcal{D}'$ on which the wavefront set is defined and the Sobolev scale $H^s$ on which the symbol calculus operates.
  • Harmonic analysis & Fourier theory — Schwartz space, tempered distributions and the Fourier-multiplier viewpoint behind ΨDO quantization; the "phase-space localization" definition of $\mathrm{WF}$ is a Fourier-decay statement after a smooth cutoff.
  • Partial differential equations — d'Alembert's geometric picture of finite-speed propagation, upgraded by Hörmander's theorem from "singularities follow characteristics" to "$\mathrm{WF}(u)$ is invariant under the bicharacteristic flow".
  • Symplectic manifolds — Lagrangian submanifolds — the natural home of FIOs: their canonical relations are Lagrangians in $T^*X\times T^*Y$, and the $\Lambda$-quantization story is symplectic geometry written in operator-theoretic vocabulary.
  • Symplectic manifolds — Hamiltonian flow — supplies the propagation engine: $X_{p_m}$ is a Hamiltonian vector field, and singularities of $Pu=f$ flow along its integral curves inside $\mathrm{Char}(P)$.
  • Atiyah-Singer index theorem — index of an elliptic ΨDO depends only on its principal symbol on $T^*M\setminus 0$; the parametrix construction here is the analytic input that lets the topological index theorem land.
