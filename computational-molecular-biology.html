Computational molecular biology

Sequences as discrete strings, structure as a combinatorial object, evolution as a stochastic process. Dynamic programming, the Burrows–Wheeler transform, hidden Markov models, and free-energy minimisation are the four hammers that drive most of the field.

A genome is a string in the four-letter alphabet $\Sigma_{\mathrm{DNA}}=\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{C},\mathtt{G},\mathtt{T}\}$; a protein is a string in the 20-letter alphabet $\Sigma_{\mathrm{aa}}$. Computational molecular biology is the discipline that asks algorithmic questions about these strings — How similar are two of them? Where does a short query land in a long reference? What hidden state most likely generated the observed letters? How do we infer the unobserved tree from which a set of sequences descended? — and the answers turn out to be almost entirely dynamic programming and pairwise probabilistic graphical models. The same recurrences appear in alignment, in HMM decoding, in RNA folding, and in phylogenetic likelihood; each is the Bellman equation for a different combinatorial optimum.

1. Sequence alignment by dynamic programming

Given two strings $x\in\Sigma^n$ and $y\in\Sigma^m$, an alignment is a way to place them above each other allowing gaps, scored by a substitution matrix $s(a,b)$ on aligned letters and a penalty $g$ for each gap. The optimal global alignment is the path through an $(n{+}1)\times(m{+}1)$ grid whose corners are $(0,0)$ and $(n,m)$ — and the Bellman recurrence is

$H_{ij} = \max\!\bigl(H_{i-1,j-1} + s(x_i,y_j),\; H_{i-1,j} - g,\; H_{i,j-1} - g\bigr),$

with $H_{i,0}=-ig$, $H_{0,j}=-jg$ — the Needleman–Wunsch algorithm. Reading the answer at $H_{nm}$ and tracing pointers back to $(0,0)$ recovers the actual alignment in $O(n+m)$ extra time. Smith–Waterman changes one initial condition and one max — clamp every cell to $0$, and read the answer as $\max_{i,j}H_{ij}$ — to find the highest-scoring local alignment. The same DP table answers both problems, with different boundary conditions.

The biology lives in $s$. BLOSUM62 is a $20\times 20$ matrix of log-odds scores $s(a,b) = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\log \bigl(p_{ab}/(q_a q_b)\bigr)$ measured on conserved protein blocks at $\sim 62\%$ identity. Diagonal entries are positive ($s(a,a)\in\{4,\dots,11\}$); off-diagonal entries are negative; chemically similar amino acids (e.g. $\mathtt{I}$–$\mathtt{L}$, $\mathtt{D}$–$\mathtt{E}$) get the mildest negatives. Realistic alignments use affine gap penalties $g(k)=\alpha+\beta(k-1)$ — high cost to open a gap, low cost to extend — which need three DP layers ($M,\,I_x,\,I_y$) and the Gotoh recurrence.

The DP recurrence has a discrete-time Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman flavour: at each cell, pick the action (diagonal / horizontal / vertical) that maximises future reward. Continuous limits are an active topic in optimal-transport interpretations of biological string distance.

2. Suffix arrays, BWT, and the FM-index

Read alignment — locating a short ($\sim 100$ bp) query inside a $3$-gigabase reference — is the highest-volume problem in genomics. Naive substring search is $O(n+m)$ per query, but you want to amortise the index over millions of queries. The clever construction at the heart of Bowtie and BWA is the Burrows–Wheeler transform.

Append a sentinel $\$$ to the text $T$ (sorting before every letter), list all cyclic rotations, sort them lexicographically, and read off the last column. That last column is $\mathrm{BWT}(T)$. The first column $F$ is a sorted permutation of $T$ — trivial to encode in $|\Sigma|$ cumulative counts. The miracle is the LF-mapping: the $k$-th occurrence of character $c$ in the last column $L$ corresponds to the $k$-th occurrence of $c$ in the first column $F$. Concretely,

$\mathrm{LF}(i) \;=\; C[L[i]] \;+\; \mathrm{rank}_{L[i]}(i),$

where $C[c]$ counts characters strictly less than $c$ and $\mathrm{rank}_c(i)$ counts occurrences of $c$ in $L[0..i)$. From the BWT plus $C$ and constant-time rank queries (samples + bitcounting), one can invert the transform and one can perform backward search: for a pattern $p$ of length $m$, maintain an interval $[\ell,r]$ of suffix-array positions whose prefix matches $p[k..m-1]$, and update with each character — $\Theta(m)$ work per query, independent of the reference length.

3. Hidden Markov models and Viterbi

An HMM is a Markov chain $Z_1,\dots,Z_T$ on a finite hidden state space $\mathcal{Z}=\{1,\dots,K\}$ together with, at each time, an observation $X_t$ whose distribution depends only on $Z_t$:

$Z_{t+1}\mid Z_t \sim a_{Z_t,\cdot},\qquad X_t \mid Z_t \sim e_{Z_t}.$

Three inference problems, three dynamic programs:

  • Decoding (Viterbi): $\arg\max_z P(z\mid x)$ via $\delta_t(z) = \max_{z'}\, \delta_{t-1}(z')\,a_{z'z}\,e_z(x_t)$, $\Theta(TK^2)$.
  • Filtering / smoothing (forward–backward): $P(Z_t = z\mid x_{1:T})$ via $\alpha_t(z)=\sum_{z'}\alpha_{t-1}(z')a_{z'z}e_z(x_t)$ and the analogous backward $\beta_t(z)$, $\Theta(TK^2)$.
  • Parameter estimation (Baum–Welch): EM on $(a, e)$, where each iteration's E-step is one forward–backward sweep and the M-step is closed-form re-estimation of transition / emission counts.

In gene finders, hidden states are biological regions (exon, intron, intergenic) and observations are codons or nucleotides. In profile HMMs (the PFAM and HMMER tools), hidden states represent positions in a multiple sequence alignment, and Viterbi-vs-profile is the canonical way to assign a new sequence to a protein family. The same architecture powers speech recognition: phonemes are hidden, audio frames are observed.

4. Phylogenetic inference and Felsenstein pruning

A phylogeny is a rooted (or unrooted) binary tree $\tau$ with branch lengths $\ell_e$, decorated with a continuous-time Markov substitution model that propagates a $|\Sigma|$-state Markov chain along each branch. For DNA, three classical models:

ModelRate matrix $Q$Parameters
Jukes–Cantor (1969)$Q_{ij}=\alpha$ for $i\ne j$$1$ rate
Kimura two-parameter (1980)transitions $\alpha$, transversions $\beta$$2$ rates
HKY (Hasegawa–Kishino–Yano)transitions $\kappa$, base frequencies $\pi$$5$ free

Given a column of an alignment with leaf-labels $x$, the likelihood under tree $(\tau,\ell)$ is computed by Felsenstein's pruning. Post-order through the tree; at each node $v$ with children $c_1, c_2$ along branches $\ell_{vc_1}, \ell_{vc_2}$, compute the $|\Sigma|$-vector of partial likelihoods

$L_v(s) \;=\; \Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Sigma} P_{st}(\ell_{vc_1})\,L_{c_1}(t)\Bigr] \cdot \Bigl[\sum_{t\in\Sigma} P_{st}(\ell_{vc_2})\,L_{c_2}(t)\Bigr].$

Leaves are initialised as indicator vectors $L_{\mathrm{leaf}}(s) = \mathbf{1}[s=x_{\mathrm{leaf}}]$. The total column likelihood is $\sum_s \pi(s) L_{\mathrm{root}}(s)$. This is just the sum-product (belief propagation) algorithm on a tree — exact and linear in the number of leaves.

Three classes of tree-building method:

  • Distance-based (UPGMA, neighbor-joining): compute pairwise distances from sequences (e.g. Jukes–Cantor: $d_{xy} = -\tfrac{3}{4}\ln(1-\tfrac{4}{3}p_{xy})$ for proportion of differences $p_{xy}$), then cluster. UPGMA assumes a molecular clock; neighbor-joining handles any additive distance and is statistically consistent.
  • Maximum likelihood: optimise $(\tau, \ell, \theta)$ to maximise $\prod_{\mathrm{cols}} P(\mathrm{column}\mid \tau, \ell, \theta)$ via Felsenstein pruning at every step. The topology search is NP-hard; RAxML, IQ-TREE use heuristics (SPR / NNI moves).
  • Bayesian (MrBayes, BEAST): MCMC over $(\tau, \ell, \theta)$ with priors on topologies and branch lengths. Output: a posterior distribution on trees — usually summarised as a maximum-clade-credibility tree with support values.

Tree-space has a geometry of its own. The Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann (BHV) space is a CAT(0) cube complex whose cells are tree topologies and whose orthants meet at the lower-dimensional boundary where edges collapse. Geodesics, sample means, and confidence regions in this space are an active topic.

5. The coalescent in population inference

Population genetics meets data: from sequenced genomes of $n$ individuals from one population, infer demographic history. The mathematical scaffold is Kingman's coalescent (covered in mathematical biology §4) — a stochastic process on genealogies. While $k$ ancestral lineages remain, the next coalescence event happens after time $T_k \sim \mathrm{Exp}\bigl(\binom{k}{2}/(2N_e)\bigr)$, and the pair that coalesces is uniform among the $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs. Summing, the expected time to the most recent common ancestor is

$\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{MRCA}}] \;=\; \sum_{k=2}^{n} \frac{2}{k(k-1)} \;=\; 2\bigl(1 - 1/n\bigr) \text{ in units of }2N_e \text{ generations}.$

Mutations are added on top: each lineage accumulates substitutions at rate $\mu$, so the total mutation count $S$ in a sample is, conditional on the genealogy with total branch length $\ell$, Poisson with mean $\mu\ell$.

Three iconic summary statistics on the resulting alignment:

  • Watterson's $\hat\theta = S / H_{n-1}$, where $S$ is the number of segregating (polymorphic) sites and $H_k=\sum_{i=1}^k 1/i$. Estimates the compound parameter $\theta = 4N_e\mu$.
  • Average pairwise difference $\hat\pi$ — also an estimator of $\theta$, but weighted differently across the site-frequency spectrum.
  • Tajima's $D = (\hat\pi - \hat\theta_W) / \mathrm{sd}$ — under neutral, constant-size demography, $\mathbb{E}[D] \approx 0$. $D \ll 0$ flags either a recent selective sweep (excess of rare alleles) or rapid expansion; $D \gg 0$ flags balancing selection or a bottleneck.
  • $F_{ST}$ — partition of genetic variance between subpopulations: $F_{ST} = (\bar H_{\mathrm{tot}} - \bar H_S)/\bar H_{\mathrm{tot}}$.

6. RNA secondary structure by energy minimisation

An RNA strand $x \in \{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{C},\mathtt{G},\mathtt{U}\}^n$ folds back on itself; the canonical Watson–Crick pairs ($\mathtt{A}$–$\mathtt{U}$, $\mathtt{C}$–$\mathtt{G}$) and the wobble pair ($\mathtt{G}$–$\mathtt{U}$) create a secondary structure — a non-crossing matching on the positions $\{1,\dots,n\}$. (A genuine matching including pseudoknots — crossings — exists, but maximum-pair pseudoknotted folding is NP-hard, so most theory is built on the non-crossing class.)

Nussinov (1978) maximises the number of pairs by the DP recurrence

$W_{ij} = \max\!\Bigl(W_{i+1,j},\ W_{i,j-1},\ W_{i+1,j-1} + \delta(x_i,x_j),\ \max_{i

with $\delta(a,b)=1$ if $(a,b)$ is a legal pair and $-\infty$ otherwise. $O(n^3)$ time, $O(n^2)$ space.

Zuker's algorithm replaces the pair-count with a Turner free-energy model: every basic structural motif (stacked pair, hairpin loop, internal loop, multi-loop) has an experimentally measured $\Delta G$. The DP minimises $E(S) = \sum_{\mathrm{motifs}\,m\in S} \Delta G_m$.

McCaskill's partition function trades $\max$ for $\sum$:

$Z = \sum_{\text{non-crossing }S} e^{-E(S)/RT},\qquad P_{ij} = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{S\ni (i,j)} e^{-E(S)/RT}.$

$P_{ij}$ is the equilibrium probability that residue $i$ pairs with $j$ — a richer description than any single structure, especially when the free-energy landscape is rugged. Visualised as a dot plot (a triangular heatmap of $P_{ij}$), it surfaces multiple stable conformations side by side.

7. Protein contact maps and coevolution

A protein of length $L$ folds into a $3$D structure; its contact map is the $L\times L$ symmetric Boolean matrix $C_{ij} = \mathbf{1}[d(C_\alpha^i, C_\alpha^j) \le 8\,\mathrm{\AA}]$. A typical globular protein has $\sim 4L$ contacts — the matrix is sparse, with $\sim 4\%$ of upper-triangle entries non-zero — and the contact pattern (with sequence) determines the fold up to bond-rotation degrees of freedom.

Predicting contacts from sequence alone was a graveyard problem until coevolutionary analysis on deep multiple sequence alignments worked. The intuition: residues that interact physically co-vary in evolution — a substitution at one is more likely to be tolerated when paired with a complementary substitution at the other.

The first signal-extraction method was just mutual information $I(x_i;x_j)$ between alignment columns. But MI conflates direct couplings with indirect ones — if $i$ couples to $k$ and $k$ couples to $j$, then $i$ and $j$ are correlated without touching. Direct coupling analysis (DCA) fixes this by fitting a Potts model

$P(x_1,\dots,x_L) \;\propto\; \exp\!\Bigl(\sum_i h_i(x_i) + \sum_{i

to the column-frequency statistics of the alignment, by pseudo-likelihood or mean-field inversion of the covariance matrix. The Frobenius norm $\|J_{ij}\|_F$ then ranks contacts; with an MSA of $\gtrsim 5L$ effective sequences and the correct reweighting for phylogenetic redundancy, top-$L$ DCA predictions hit $\sim 60{-}80\%$ accuracy on monomeric proteins.

The Anfinsen hypothesis (a protein's sequence determines its structure as the global free-energy minimum) frames folding as the optimisation problem $\min_{\mathrm{conformation}} G$. The exact landscape has a corresponding non-convex optimisation with $\sim 3L$ degrees of freedom and an exponentially complex set of local minima — Levinthal's paradox. Practical solutions are now driven by AlphaFold's geometric attention transformer, whose input features are the DCA-style MSA covariation and whose output is iteratively refined atom coordinates. The math underneath is invariant point attention on an $\mathrm{SE}(3)$-equivariant representation — geometric deep learning meets statistical mechanics meets graph algorithms.

Connections

Computational molecular biology is the empirical face of finite-alphabet probability: most of its workhorses (alignment, BWT search, HMM decoding, Felsenstein pruning, RNA DP) are Bellman equations on combinatorial state spaces, and most of its inference problems are versions of MAP or marginal likelihood on a graphical model. The frontiers are (a) scale — exabyte-scale read alignment, whole-population coalescent inference on hundreds of thousands of genomes, ancient-DNA reconstruction — and (b) the geometric-deep-learning move on protein and RNA structure that subsumed half of the field in 2020–2024.

  • Mathematical biology — Kingman's coalescent — the genealogical scaffold on which every coalescent estimator (Watterson, Tajima's $D$, $F_{ST}$) lives.
  • Probability theory — Markov chains — every HMM, every substitution model, every BWT-LF traversal is a finite-state Markov chain at heart.
  • Information theory — mutual information — naive MI on alignment columns is the baseline contact predictor; entropy of the substitution model bounds the per-site information content of a phylogeny.
  • Stochastic processes — discrete martingales — the forward and backward algorithms of an HMM are evaluations of the same conditional-expectation martingale.
  • Spectral graph theory — clustering — spectral clustering on a sequence-similarity graph is the standard taxonomic-classification heuristic for novel metagenomic data.
  • Hidden Markov models — Baum–Welch (not in the notebook yet) — the EM ascent on $(a,e)$ that fits a profile HMM to a protein family.
