Causal inference

The mathematics of cause and effect — structural equations, the surgery of intervention, and counterfactual reasoning — that lets you climb Pearl's ladder from seeing to doing to imagining what would have been.

1. Structural causal models

A probability distribution tells you how variables co-occur; it is silent about what happens if you reach in and change one. To talk about cause and effect we need more structure than a joint. A structural causal model (SCM) supplies it: a directed acyclic graph $G$ over the variables together with a structural equation for each node,

$X_i \;:=\; f_i\big(\mathrm{pa}_i,\; U_i\big),\qquad U_1,\dots,U_n \text{ mutually independent},$

where $\mathrm{pa}_i$ are the graph parents of $X_i$ and $U_i$ is an exogenous noise term carrying everything outside the model. The assignment $:=$ is deliberately not an equation: it is a mechanism, read left-to-right, that says how $X_i$ is produced from its causes. The independent noises (a Markovian model) make every dependence in the data flow through the arrows.

The entailed distribution

Pushing the noise through the equations induces an observational distribution that factorizes exactly like a Bayes net:

$p(x_1,\dots,x_n) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} p\big(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i\big)$   (the causal Markov factorization).

So an SCM is a Bayes net plus a reading of the arrows as mechanisms. The catch — the whole reason the subject exists — is that the joint does not determine the SCM. Two models with reversed arrows, $X\to Y$ and $X\leftarrow Y$, are Markov-equivalent and can entail the very same $p(x,y)$, yet they answer questions about interventions in opposite ways. Causal claims live in the equations, not in the distribution they happen to produce.

Worked mini-example — a confounded chain. Take $Z := U_Z$, $X := f_X(Z, U_X)$, $Y := f_Y(X, Z, U_Y)$ on the graph $Z\to X$, $Z\to Y$, $X\to Y$. The entailed joint factorizes as $p(z)\,p(x \mid z)\,p(y \mid x,z)$. Read causally: $Z$ is a common cause (a confounder) of both $X$ and $Y$, and $X$ also affects $Y$ directly. The same factorization is a perfectly ordinary Bayes net — only the SCM's promise that these conditionals are autonomous mechanisms licenses us to mutilate one of them in the next section.

2. Interventions & the do-operator

The second rung of the ladder is doing. To express "set $X$ to the value $x$, by external force, regardless of its usual causes" we use the do-operator. An intervention $\operatorname{do}(X=x)$ is graph surgery: delete every arrow into $X$, replace its structural equation by the constant $X:=x$, and leave every other mechanism alone. The arrows out of $X$ stay — that is precisely how the intervention propagates to the outcome.

On the mutilated graph the entailed distribution is the truncated factorization (also the g-formula): drop the factor for the intervened node and clamp its value,

$p\big(v \mid \operatorname{do}(X=x)\big) \;=\; \prod_{i:\, V_i \neq X} p\big(v_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i\big)\bigg|_{X=x}.$

Conditioning is not intervening. In general $p\big(y \mid \operatorname{do}(X=x)\big) \neq p\big(y \mid X=x\big)$. Observing $X=x$ is filtering the world to cases where $X$ happened to equal $x$ — which also tells you about $X$'s causes, and through them about $Y$. Intervening manufactures $X=x$, severing it from its causes. The two coincide only when there is no backdoor from $X$ to $Y$ — that is, no confounding (next section).
Worked mini-example — surgery on the confounded chain. On $Z\to X$, $Z\to Y$, $X\to Y$ the observational law is $p(z)\,p(x \mid z)\,p(y \mid x,z)$. Intervening cuts $Z\to X$, dropping the factor $p(x \mid z)$: $p\big(z,y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)\big) = p(z)\,p(y \mid x,z)$, so $p\big(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)\big)=\sum_z p(y \mid x,z)\,p(z)$. Compare conditioning, $p(y \mid x)=\sum_z p(y \mid x,z)\,p(z \mid x)$, which weights $Z$ by the post-selection $p(z \mid x)$. The two differ exactly because $p(z) \neq p(z \mid x)$ when $Z\to X$.

3. Confounding & the backdoor criterion

The gap between $p(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x))$ and $p(y \mid x)$ has a name: confounding. It is created by a backdoor path — any path from $X$ to $Y$ that begins with an arrow into $X$, e.g. $X\leftarrow Z\rightarrow Y$. Such a path is a non-causal conduit of association: it makes $X$ and $Y$ move together for reasons that have nothing to do with $X$ causing $Y$.

The fix is to adjust — to compare treated and untreated units within levels of a well-chosen set $Z$. The backdoor criterion says exactly which sets work. A set $Z$ is admissible relative to $(X,Y)$ if

  • $Z$ blocks every backdoor path from $X$ to $Y$ (in the d-separation sense), and
  • no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$.

The second clause matters: adjusting for a descendant of $X$ (especially a collider or a mediator) can open new spurious paths or eat part of the very effect you want. When $Z$ is admissible, the interventional law is recovered by the adjustment formula:

$p\big(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)\big) \;=\; \sum_z p\big(y \mid x, z\big)\,p(z).$

Note the weight is the marginal $p(z)$, not $p(z \mid x)$ — the signature of surgery. (One subtlety from d-separation: a backdoor path that runs through a collider is already blocked when the collider is unobserved, so you must not adjust for it to "close" the path — doing so would open it.)

Simpson's paradox. A drug is given more often to sicker patients. Within each risk group it helps; pooled across groups it appears to hurt. Aggregating without adjusting for the risk confounder $Z$ lets the population mix masquerade as a treatment effect — and the aggregate trend can literally reverse the within-stratum trend.

4. Identification & do-calculus

The backdoor formula needs an admissible set you can actually measure. When the confounder is unobserved, no backdoor adjustment exists — yet the effect may still be computable from data. The general question is identification: can $p\big(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)\big)$ be rewritten using only the observational distribution $p$? If yes, passive data suffices; if no, you genuinely need an experiment or extra assumptions.

The three rules

Pearl's do-calculus is a complete proof system for this. Writing $G_{\overline{X}}$ for the graph with arrows into $X$ removed and $G_{\underline{X}}$ for arrows out of $X$ removed, the three rules let you manipulate expressions with $\operatorname{do}$ terms:

  • Rule 1 (insert/delete observations): $p(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x), z, w) = p(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x), w)$ when $Y$ and $Z$ are d-separated by $X,W$ in $G_{\overline{X}}$.
  • Rule 2 (action/observation exchange): a $\operatorname{do}(z)$ may be downgraded to a mere observation $z$ when $Y$ and $Z$ are d-separated by $X,W$ in $G_{\overline{X}\underline{Z}}$.
  • Rule 3 (insert/delete actions): a $\operatorname{do}(z)$ may be dropped when $Y$ and $Z$ are d-separated by $X,W$ in the suitably mutilated graph (no remaining causal path from $Z$ to $Y$).

The deep result (Shpitser–Pearl completeness): if an effect is identifiable, repeated application of these rules will reduce it to a $\operatorname{do}$-free expression; if no such reduction exists, the effect is provably non-identifiable (the "bow arc" — an unobserved confounder sitting directly across $X\to Y$ with no mediator — is the canonical unidentifiable case).

The front-door criterion

The signature payoff is the front-door criterion. Suppose $X\to M\to Y$ with an unobserved confounder $U$ feeding both $X$ and $Y$. The backdoor $X\leftarrow U\rightarrow Y$ cannot be blocked — $U$ is unmeasured. But if the mediator $M$ intercepts all of $X$'s effect on $Y$ and has no unblocked backdoor of its own, do-calculus identifies the effect through $M$:

$p\big(y \mid \operatorname{do}(x)\big) \;=\; \sum_m p\big(m \mid x\big)\,\sum_{x'} p\big(y \mid m, x'\big)\,p\big(x'\big).$

Read it as two identifiable hops chained together: $X\to M$ has no backdoor (so $p(m \mid x)$ is already causal), and the effect of $M$ on $Y$ is identified by adjusting for $X$ (the inner sum over $x'$).

Worked mini-example — smoking → tar → cancer. Let $X$ = smoking, $M$ = tar deposits in the lungs, $Y$ = cancer, with an unobserved genetic confounder $U$ that affects both the urge to smoke and cancer risk. We cannot adjust for $U$. But tar is (assume) caused only by smoking and causes cancer only through itself: $X\to M\to Y$. The front-door formula $\sum_m p(m \mid x)\sum_{x'} p(y \mid m,x')\,p(x')$ recovers the causal effect of smoking on cancer purely from observed smoking, tar, and cancer frequencies — no measurement of the genetic confounder required.

5. Counterfactuals

The top rung is imagining: "this patient took the drug and recovered — would they have recovered anyway, without it?" This is a counterfactual, written $Y_{x'}$, a statement about a world that did not happen, conditioned on the world that did. Interventions cannot answer it: $\operatorname{do}(X=x')$ gives a population distribution, averaged over a fresh draw of the noise, whereas a counterfactual is about this unit, whose hidden circumstances we partly learn from the facts.

Abduction – action – prediction

An SCM computes counterfactuals in three steps:

  1. Abduction. Use the observed evidence $E$ to update the distribution of the exogenous noise: $p(U) \rightarrow p(U \mid E)$. This is where the individual enters — the noise encodes everything idiosyncratic about this unit.
  2. Action. Apply $\operatorname{do}(X=x')$ to the model: replace $X$'s equation with the constant $x'$, keeping the updated noise.
  3. Prediction. Compute $Y$ in the modified model under $p(U \mid E)$. The result is $Y_{x'} \mid E$.

Potential outcomes

The Neyman–Rubin framework writes the same content as a pair of potential outcomes $\{Y_0, Y_1\}$ for each unit — what would happen under control and under treatment. Only one is ever observed (the "fundamental problem of causal inference"), linked to data by consistency:

$Y \;=\; Y_X \qquad\text{(the observed outcome is the potential outcome under the treatment actually received).}$

The two languages are equivalent: $Y_x$ is exactly the prediction output of abduction–action–prediction with $\operatorname{do}(X=x)$, and consistency is the prediction step run at the factual treatment.

Worked mini-example — a linear SCM, 3 steps. Take $Y := 2X + U_Y$ with the noise $U_Y$ unknown a priori. We observe a unit with $X=1$ and $Y=5$.
Abduction: from $5 = 2\cdot 1 + U_Y$ we infer $U_Y = 3$ for this unit (the evidence pins the noise exactly in this deterministic case).
Action: ask the counterfactual $X' = 0$, i.e. $\operatorname{do}(X=0)$, holding $U_Y = 3$.
Prediction: $Y_{0} = 2\cdot 0 + 3 = 3$. So this unit would have had $Y=3$ had it not been treated — the individual treatment effect is $5 - 3 = 2 = b$. Note we did not redraw the noise; that is what makes it a statement about this unit, not the population.

6. Estimating causal effects

So far the laws have been exact. In practice we have a finite sample and must estimate. The headline target is the average treatment effect (ATE), the population contrast of potential outcomes:

$\tau \;=\; \mathbb{E}\big[Y_1\big] - \mathbb{E}\big[Y_0\big].$

The naive plug-in $\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid X{=}1] - \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid X{=}0]$ estimates $\tau$ only when treatment is unconfounded. What licenses estimation in general are two assumptions:

  • Ignorability (no unobserved confounding): $\{Y_0, Y_1\} \perp X \mid Z$. Within a stratum of the covariates $Z$, treatment is as-good-as-random with respect to the potential outcomes.
  • Positivity / overlap: $0 \,<\, e(z) \,<\, 1$ for the propensity score $e(z) = P(X{=}1 \mid Z{=}z)$. Every covariate profile has some chance of either arm, so no comparison is pure extrapolation.

Under both, $\tau$ is identified, and the two workhorses are:

adjustment: $\;\widehat{\tau} = \dfrac1n\sum_z \big[\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid 1,z] - \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid 0,z]\big]\widehat{p}(z), \qquad$ IPW: $\;\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y_1] = \dfrac1n\sum_{i} \dfrac{X_i\,Y_i}{e(Z_i)}.$

The inverse-propensity estimator upweights treated units that were unlikely to be treated, manufacturing a pseudo-population in which $X \perp Z$. Doubly-robust estimators combine an outcome model with IPW and stay consistent if either model is right. A randomized experiment sidesteps all of this: randomization forces ignorability by design with $Z$ empty and $e(z) \equiv \tfrac12$, which is why the RCT is the gold standard.

Worked mini-example — the bias adjustment removes. Suppose high-$Z$ units recover more and are treated more. The naive contrast credits the treatment with the recovery that $Z$ would have delivered anyway — a positive bias. Stratifying on $Z$ (adjustment) or reweighting by $1/e(Z)$ (IPW) strips that bias out: each treated high-$Z$ unit, being over-represented, is shrunk back toward the population mix, and the estimate converges to the true $\tau$.

Connections

Causal inference grafts a layer of mechanism onto the probabilistic machinery: the same DAG that a graphical model uses to compress a joint becomes, under the structural reading, a calculus of interventions and counterfactuals. The open frontiers are mostly about doing this without a hand-drawn graph — causal discovery (learning the DAG from data, where Markov equivalence leaves the arrows only partly oriented), identification with latent confounders beyond the front/backdoor templates, and policy learning that treats interventions as decisions. A beginner is often surprised that more data never fixes confounding: bias from an unmeasured common cause does not shrink with $n$, which is exactly why the structural questions cannot be reduced to better statistics.

  • Probabilistic graphical models — Bayesian networks & d-separation — a causal graph is a Bayes net; the entailed factorization and the d-separation rules (especially the collider) are reused wholesale to read off backdoor paths.
  • Probability theory — conditional probability & Bayes' rule — the adjustment formula and the abduction step are Bayesian conditioning applied to the right (post-surgery) distribution.
  • Mathematical statistics — estimators, bias & variance — the ATE is a parameter, and IPW / doubly-robust estimators are studied with the same bias–variance and consistency tools.
  • Statistical learning theory — empirical risk minimization — outcome models and propensity models are fitted by ERM, so the capacity-control story carries straight over to the nuisance functions of doubly-robust estimation.
  • Reinforcement learning — Markov decision processes — an RL action is an intervention $\operatorname{do}(a)$, and off-policy evaluation is exactly the counterfactual question "what reward would a different policy have earned?", estimated with importance weights that are propensity ratios in disguise.

Forward to come: causal discovery and structure learning, instrumental-variable and regression-discontinuity designs, mediation analysis, and causal representation learning, where the latent variables of a deep generative model are themselves endowed with a causal graph.
