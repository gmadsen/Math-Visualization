Order theory and lattices

A partial order is just three axioms — reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive — but from them grow Hasse diagrams, meets and joins, the duality of chains and antichains, and fixed-point theorems that anchor everything from logic to denotational semantics.

1. Posets, Hasse diagrams, chains & antichains

A partial order on a set $P$ is a relation $\le$ that is

  • reflexive: $a \le a$ for all $a$;
  • antisymmetric: if $a \le b$ and $b \le a$ then $a = b$;
  • transitive: if $a \le b$ and $b \le c$ then $a \le c$.

The pair $(P, \le)$ is a partially ordered set, or poset. The word partial is the whole point: two elements $a, b$ may be incomparable, with neither $a \le b$ nor $b \le a$. Drop antisymmetry and you have a preorder; add the demand that any two elements be comparable (totality) and you have a chain.

Canonical examples: the divisors of an integer ordered by divisibility, the subsets of a set ordered by inclusion, the subspaces of a vector space, the open sets of a topology. A partial order is, structurally, just a special binary relation — the same raw material as an equivalence relation, but with antisymmetry replacing symmetry.

Hasse diagrams

Drawing every comparable pair clutters the picture, because transitivity forces most edges. The Hasse diagram instead draws only the covering relations: $y$ covers $x$ when $x < y$ and no $z$ satisfies $x < z < y$. Elements are placed so that larger ones sit higher, and we read off $x \le y$ by following an upward path. Formally the Hasse diagram is the transitive reduction of the order relation — the minimal set of edges whose transitive closure recovers $\le$.

Chains, antichains, height and width

A chain is a subset that is totally ordered (every two of its elements are comparable); an antichain is a subset of pairwise incomparable elements. The height of a finite poset is the size of its longest chain; the width is the size of its largest antichain. These two numbers are the protagonists of Dilworth and Mirsky in §3.

Worked mini-example. The divisor lattice of $30$ — divisors $\{1,2,3,5,6,10,15,30\}$ under divisibility — is isomorphic to the Boolean lattice $B_3$ of subsets of $\{2,3,5\}$ (send each divisor to its set of prime factors). A chain is $1 \mid 2 \mid 6 \mid 30$ (length $4$ = height); an antichain is $\{6,10,15\}$ — the three two-prime-factor divisors, matching the $\binom{3}{2}=3$ size-$2$ subsets of $B_3$. The two middle layers (sizes $\binom{3}{1}=\binom{3}{2}=3$) are the widest, so the width is $3$.

2. Lattices: meet & join

A poset is a lattice when every pair of elements $a, b$ has both

  • a meet $a \wedge b$ — the greatest lower bound, the largest element below both;
  • a join $a \vee b$ — the least upper bound, the smallest element above both.

On the divisor lattice these are $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$; on the Boolean lattice of subsets they are $\cap$ and $\cup$. A lattice with a bottom $\hat 0$ and top $\hat 1$ is bounded (every finite lattice is). Meet and join are commutative, associative, idempotent, and absorptive — so a lattice can equally be defined algebraically as a set with two such operations.

Distributive and modular lattices

The richest lattices satisfy the distributive law $a \wedge (b \vee c) = (a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge c)$ (equivalently its dual). A weaker condition is modularity: $a \le c$ implies $a \vee (b \wedge c) = (a \vee b) \wedge c$. Distributive $\Rightarrow$ modular, never the reverse.

What makes these conditions clean is that each is detected by a single forbidden sublattice:

a lattice is modular $\iff$ it has no sublattice isomorphic to the pentagon $N_5$;

a lattice is distributive $\iff$ it has no sublattice isomorphic to $N_5$ or to the diamond $M_3$.

$N_5$ is the five-element non-modular lattice (a chain of length $3$ with one extra element bypassing the middle); $M_3$ is the diamond with three incomparable atoms between $\hat 0$ and $\hat 1$. $M_3$ is modular but not distributive, which is exactly why distributivity must forbid both $M_3$ and $N_5$ while modularity forbids only $N_5$. (These are Birkhoff's and Dedekind's theorems.)

Worked mini-example ($M_3$ fails distributivity). Take distinct atoms $a,b,c$ in $M_3$. Then $a \wedge (b \vee c) = a \wedge \hat 1 = a$, but $(a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge c) = \hat 0 \vee \hat 0 = \hat 0 \ne a$. So $M_3$ is not distributive — yet it is modular, since it contains no pentagon $N_5$.

3. Dilworth's theorem

Chains and antichains are dual obstructions, and a finite poset balances them exactly.

Dilworth's theorem (1950). In any finite poset, the size of a largest antichain (the width) equals the minimum number of chains needed to cover all the elements.

One inequality is immediate: a single chain meets any antichain in at most one element, so if the width is $w$ you certainly need at least $w$ chains to cover the poset. The depth of the theorem is the reverse — that $w$ chains always suffice. The standard proof is by induction on $|P|$, or as a slick corollary of König's theorem on bipartite matchings.

Mirsky's dual

Swapping the roles of chains and antichains gives the dual statement.

Mirsky's theorem (1971). In any finite poset, the size of a longest chain (the height) equals the minimum number of antichains needed to cover all the elements.

Mirsky's direction is the easy one: rank each element by the length of the longest chain ending at it, and the elements of equal rank form antichains — a covering by exactly height-many antichains. Together, Dilworth and Mirsky say chains and antichains are perfectly dual covering resources.

Worked mini-example. On the divisor lattice of $12$ ($\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$), the largest antichain is $\{4,6\}$ (or $\{2,3\}$), so the width is $2$. Dilworth then promises a cover by $2$ chains, and indeed $1 \mid 2 \mid 4 \mid 12$ together with $3 \mid 6$ covers all six divisors. Dually the longest chain $1 \mid 2 \mid 4 \mid 12$ has length $4$ = height, and the rank levels $\{1\}, \{2,3\}, \{4,6\}, \{12\}$ are $4$ antichains covering the poset (Mirsky).

4. Sperner & the Boolean lattice

The Boolean lattice $B_n$ is the poset of all subsets of an $n$-element set ordered by inclusion; meet is $\cap$, join is $\cup$, bottom is $\varnothing$, top is the whole set. It is the universal distributive lattice and the natural home of Sperner's question: how large can a family of subsets be if none contains another? Such a family is precisely an antichain in $B_n$ — a Sperner family.

Sperner's theorem (1928). The largest antichain in $B_n$ has size $\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$, attained by the middle layer of all $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$-element subsets.

The subsets of $B_n$ split into $n+1$ layers by cardinality; layer $k$ has $\binom{n}{k}$ subsets and is itself an antichain (equal-size sets never contain one another). The binomial coefficients peak at the middle, so the middle layer is the obvious large antichain. Sperner's content is that you can do no better with a mixed family.

The LYM proof

The cleanest argument is the LYM inequality (Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin): for any antichain $\mathcal{F}$ in $B_n$,

$\displaystyle \sum_{A \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{1}{\binom{n}{|A|}} \le 1.$

Lubell's one-line proof: a uniformly random maximal chain $\varnothing \subset \cdots \subset [n]$ passes through each fixed set $A$ with probability $1/\binom{n}{|A|}$, and it can hit the antichain $\mathcal F$ at most once, so the probabilities sum to at most $1$. Since every term is at least $1/\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$, we get $|\mathcal{F}| \le \binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ — Sperner's bound. The full extremal-set development of LYM lives in extremal combinatorics; here we keep the poset framing and cross-link the proof rather than re-deriving it.

Worked mini-example. In $B_4$ the layer sizes are $1,4,6,4,1$. The middle layer (the six $2$-element subsets) is the largest antichain, of size $\binom{4}{2}=6$ — and no clever mix of small and large subsets beats $6$, by LYM. In $B_3$ the layers are $1,3,3,1$: the maximum antichain has size $\binom{3}{1}=3$, and there are two extremal middle layers.

5. Möbius functions & inversion

Inclusion–exclusion, the inversion formula for the number-theoretic $\mu$, and a dozen other "alternating-sign" identities are all one theorem in disguise — the Möbius function of a poset, introduced by Gian-Carlo Rota.

Let $P$ be a locally finite poset (every interval $[x,y]$ is finite). Its Möbius function $\mu : \{(x,y) : x \le y\} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is defined recursively by

$\mu(x,x) = 1, \qquad \mu(x,y) = -\!\!\sum_{x \le z < y} \mu(x,z) \quad (x < y).$

Equivalently $\sum_{x \le z \le y} \mu(x,z) = 0$ whenever $x < y$: the Möbius function is the inverse, in the incidence algebra of $P$, of the constant function $\zeta(x,y) = 1$.

Möbius inversion

The payoff is an inversion principle. If $g(x) = \sum_{y \le x} f(y)$ for all $x$, then

$\displaystyle f(x) = \sum_{y \le x} \mu(y,x)\, g(y).$

Two specializations recover classical theorems:

  • On the Boolean lattice $B_n$, $\mu(A,B) = (-1)^{|B \setminus A|}$, and Möbius inversion is exactly the inclusion–exclusion formula with its alternating signs.
  • On the divisor lattice (positive integers under divisibility), $\mu(1,n)$ is the number-theoretic $\mu(n)$ — $0$ if $n$ is not squarefree, else $(-1)^{(\text{number of prime factors})}$ — and inversion becomes classical Möbius inversion $g(n) = \sum_{d\mid n} f(d) \Leftrightarrow f(n) = \sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)g(d)$.
Worked mini-example. On $B_2$ (subsets of $\{a,b\}$): $\mu(\varnothing,\varnothing)=1$, $\mu(\varnothing,\{a\}) = -1$, $\mu(\varnothing,\{b\})=-1$, and $\mu(\varnothing,\{a,b\}) = -(1 + (-1) + (-1)) = 1 = (-1)^2$. The signs $+,-,-,+$ are exactly the inclusion–exclusion pattern $|A\cup B| = |A|+|B|-|A\cap B|$. On the divisor side, $\mu(1,12)=0$ because $12=2^2\cdot 3$ is not squarefree, while $\mu(1,30)=(-1)^3=-1$.

6. Complete lattices & Knaster–Tarski

A lattice gives meets and joins of pairs; a complete lattice gives them for arbitrary subsets. Formally, every $S \subseteq L$ has a meet $\bigwedge S$ and a join $\bigvee S$. Taking $S = L$ produces a global bottom $\hat 0 = \bigwedge L$ and top $\hat 1 = \bigvee L$, and taking $S = \varnothing$ gives $\bigvee \varnothing = \hat 0$, $\bigwedge \varnothing = \hat 1$. Every finite lattice is complete; the powerset $(\mathcal{P}(X), \subseteq)$ and the lattice of subgroups of a group are infinite examples.

Knaster–Tarski

Completeness is exactly the hypothesis that turns "monotone map" into "has a fixed point" — with no continuity, no metric, no contraction.

Knaster–Tarski theorem. Let $L$ be a complete lattice and $f : L \to L$ monotone (order-preserving: $x \le y \Rightarrow f(x) \le f(y)$). Then the set of fixed points of $f$ is itself a complete lattice. In particular $f$ has a least fixed point

$\displaystyle \operatorname{lfp}(f) = \bigwedge \{ x \in L : f(x) \le x \}$

(the meet of all pre-fixed points) and, dually, a greatest fixed point $\bigvee\{x : x \le f(x)\}$.

The proof is a two-line miracle: let $a = \bigwedge\{x : f(x) \le x\}$. For each such $x$, $a \le x$ so $f(a) \le f(x) \le x$; taking the meet over all $x$ gives $f(a) \le a$, so $a$ is a pre-fixed point. Then $f(a) \le a$ gives $f(f(a)) \le f(a)$, so $f(a)$ is also pre-fixed, whence $a \le f(a)$. Antisymmetry forces $f(a) = a$.

Galois connections

The same machinery powers Galois connections: a pair of monotone maps $f : P \to Q$, $g : Q \to P$ with $f(p) \le q \iff p \le g(q)$. The composites $g \circ f$ and $f \circ g$ are closure and kernel operators whose fixed points are the "closed" elements — the abstract skeleton behind Galois theory's field/group correspondence, and behind closure operators throughout algebra and logic.

Worked mini-example. On the complete lattice $[0,1]$ (an interval, ordered as usual), the monotone map $f(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x + x^2)$ has fixed points where $f(x)=x$, i.e. $x^2 = x$, so $x = 0$ or $x = 1$. The least fixed point is $0$ (iterating $f$ from $0$ stays at $0$); the greatest is $1$. Knaster–Tarski guarantees both without any limit argument, and Kleene's refinement says that for Scott-continuous $f$ the least fixed point is reached by iterating $f$ from $\hat 0$.

Connections

Order theory is the connective tissue of mathematics: wherever something is "built up" — sets by inclusion, integers by divisibility, propositions by entailment, types by subtyping, opens by containment — a poset is lurking, and meets/joins/fixed points become the load-bearing operations. The frontier is less about open problems than about reach: lattice theory underwrites the semantics of programming languages (domains and denotational fixed points), the algebraic side of logic (Heyting and Boolean algebras), and the formal-concept analysis of data, while the duality theorems (Birkhoff, Stone, Priestley) keep revealing that "spaces" and "lattices" are two views of one object.

  • Naive set theory — sets, functions & relations — a partial order is just a binary relation with three axioms; the powerset under inclusion is the prototype lattice.
  • Extremal combinatorics — Sperner & LYM — the extremal-set proof of the Boolean-lattice antichain bound, the engine behind §4.
  • Enumerative combinatorics — inclusion–exclusion — the special case of Möbius inversion on the Boolean lattice, with its alternating signs.
  • Category theory — Yoneda, limits & adjunctions — a poset is a thin category, meet and join are products and coproducts, and Galois connections are adjoint functors in miniature.
  • Fixed-point theorems — Banach contraction — the metric counterpart of Knaster–Tarski: a contraction gives a unique fixed point reached by iteration, where the order-theoretic theorem needs only monotonicity but gives a whole lattice of fixed points.
