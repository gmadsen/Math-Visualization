MMP and birational geometry

Two birational varieties differ by a sequence of blow-ups, flips, and contractions. The Minimal Model Program is the algorithm that walks this birational web toward the simplest representative — and the canonical bundle is its compass.

1. Kodaira dimension and the canonical ring

Birational geometry classifies varieties up to the relation generated by isomorphisms on a dense open subset. From the inside this looks vast — even $\mathbb{P}^2$ admits a six-parameter family of birational self-maps (the Cremona group). The first task is to extract a numerical invariant that survives every birational move. The natural candidate is built out of the canonical bundle.

Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. The canonical ring is the graded ring of pluricanonical sections,

$$R(X, K_X) \;=\; \bigoplus_{m \ge 0} H^0(X, K_X^{\otimes m}).$$

It is a $\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$-graded $k$-algebra, and one of the central theorems of birational geometry — finite generation of the canonical ring (BCHM 2010) — says it is finitely generated whenever $X$ is of general type. The growth rate of its graded pieces is recorded by a single invariant.

Definition (Kodaira dimension). The Kodaira dimension of $X$ is

$$\kappa(X) \;=\; \begin{cases} -\infty & \text{if } h^0(X, K_X^{\otimes m}) = 0 \text{ for every } m \ge 1, \\ \limsup_{m \to \infty} \dfrac{\log h^0(X, K_X^{\otimes m})}{\log m} & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

Equivalently, $\kappa(X) \in \{-\infty, 0, 1, \ldots, \dim X\}$ is the dimension of the image of the pluricanonical map $\varphi_{|mK_X|} : X \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}(H^0(K_X^{\otimes m})^\vee)$ for $m \gg 0$.

So $\kappa(X)$ measures how positive $K_X$ is, on a coarse $\{-\infty, 0, 1, \ldots, \dim X\}$ scale. The extremes are familiar:

  • $\kappa = -\infty$ ("Fano-like"). $K_X$ has no positive multiple with sections. Examples: $\mathbb{P}^n$, smooth Fano hypersurfaces of degree $\le n$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$, ruled surfaces.
  • $\kappa = 0$ ("Calabi-Yau-like"). Some power $K_X^{\otimes m}$ is trivial. Examples: abelian varieties, K3 surfaces, Calabi–Yau threefolds.
  • $\kappa = \dim X$ ("general type"). $K_X$ is big — pluricanonical sections grow at the maximal rate $m^{\dim X}$. Generic varieties of large degree are general type.

The intermediate values $0 < \kappa < \dim X$ correspond to varieties that fibre over a base of dimension $\kappa$ (the Iitaka fibration), with general fibre of $\kappa = 0$.

Birational invariance. If $X \dashrightarrow X'$ is a birational map of smooth projective varieties, then $\kappa(X) = \kappa(X')$.

The proof is the standard "Hartogs across codim $\ge 2$" argument. Pluricanonical forms are top-dimensional differentials raised to a tensor power, and on a smooth variety they extend uniquely across any subset of codimension $\ge 2$. A birational map of smooth projective varieties is an isomorphism on a common open whose complement has codimension $\ge 2$ on each side, so pullback identifies pluricanonical sections in both directions. The Iitaka conjecture ($C_{n,m}$) — that for a fibration $X \to Y$, $\kappa(X) \ge \kappa(Y) + \kappa(\text{generic fibre})$ — is a deeper birational-invariance statement that remains open in full generality.

2. The cone theorem and extremal contractions

To run the MMP, we need a well-defined "next move" at every step: contract some piece of $X$ that is interfering with $K_X$ being positive. Mori's cone theorem identifies these pieces precisely. The setting is the closed cone of curves $\overline{NE}(X) \subset N_1(X)_\mathbb{R}$ — the closed convex cone generated by classes $[C]$ of irreducible curves on $X$, sitting in the $\mathbb{R}$-vector space dual to the Néron–Severi group $N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}$.

Cone theorem (Mori 1982, Kawamata–Reid–Shokurov). Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety. Then

$$\overline{NE}(X) \;=\; \overline{NE}(X)_{K_X \ge 0} \;+\; \sum_{i \in I} \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\,[C_i],$$

where the $\overline{NE}(X)_{K_X \ge 0}$ piece is the half where $K_X \cdot \alpha \ge 0$, and the $C_i$ are rational curves with $0 < -K_X \cdot C_i \le \dim X + 1$. The set $\{[C_i]\}$ is locally finite in the open half-space $\{K_X < 0\}$: any compact subset meets only finitely many. Each ray $R_i = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}[C_i]$ is called a $K_X$-negative extremal ray.

The picture is striking: the $K_X$-positive half of $\overline{NE}(X)$ is allowed to be wild, but the $K_X$-negative half is a locally polyhedral (countable) cone whose extremal rays are spanned by rational curves — all of bounded "negativity" against $K_X$. This rigidity is the input to every MMP step.

Extremal contractions

The companion theorem says every $K_X$-negative extremal ray is geometrically realisable: there is a contraction morphism that crushes precisely the curves whose class lies on the ray.

Contraction theorem. For each $K_X$-negative extremal ray $R \subset \overline{NE}(X)$, there is a unique morphism $\phi_R : X \to Y$ to a normal projective variety $Y$ with $\phi_{R, *} \mathcal{O}_X = \mathcal{O}_Y$, such that an irreducible curve $C \subset X$ is contracted by $\phi_R$ (i.e. $\phi_R(C)$ is a point) iff $[C] \in R$.

The contractions come in three flavours, sorted by the geometry of the exceptional locus $\mathrm{Exc}(\phi_R)$:

Type$\dim Y$codim of $\mathrm{Exc}$example
fibre type$< \dim X$—$\mathbb{P}^1$-bundle $\to$ base; $\mathbb{P}^n \to \mathrm{pt}$
divisorial$= \dim X$$1$blow-down of a $(-1)$-curve on a surface
small$= \dim X$$\ge 2$contracting a $\mathbb{P}^1$ in a smooth threefold (only in $\dim \ge 3$)

For surfaces only the first two types occur — a birational morphism between smooth surfaces has exceptional locus of dimension $\ge 1$, hence codimension $\le 1$, ruling out the small case. The small case is precisely the obstruction that forces the introduction of flips in dimension $\ge 3$ (next section).

3. Surface MMP: Castelnuovo and Enriques classification

In dimension two the MMP is classical and complete. The basic move is contracting a $(-1)$-curve, and the algorithm always terminates at one of a small list of minimal surfaces. The output is the Enriques–Kodaira classification.

Castelnuovo's contractibility criterion. Let $X$ be a smooth projective surface and $E \subset X$ an irreducible curve. Then $E$ contracts to a smooth point of a smooth projective surface iff $E \cong \mathbb{P}^1$ and $E^2 = -1$.

Adjunction on a smooth surface gives $2g(E) - 2 = E^2 + K_X \cdot E$, so a $(-1)$-curve has $K_X \cdot E = -1 < 0$ — its class lies on a $K_X$-negative extremal ray. Castelnuovo's theorem says these are exactly the rays admitting divisorial contractions to smooth surfaces, and that the resulting variety is again smooth and projective. So one MMP step on a smooth surface is "find a $(-1)$-curve and blow it down."

Why does this terminate? Each blow-down decreases the Picard number $\rho(X) = \mathrm{rk}\,N^1(X)$ by exactly $1$, since it removes the class $[E]$ from $N^1(X)$. The Picard number is a positive integer, so any descending sequence stops.

The Enriques–Kodaira classification

Once no $(-1)$-curves remain, $X$ is a minimal surface. There are essentially three families:

  • $\mathbb{P}^2$ — Picard number $1$, $K_X = -3H$.
  • The Hirzebruch surfaces $\mathbb{F}_n$ for $n = 0$ or $n \ge 2$ — $\mathbb{P}^1$-bundles over $\mathbb{P}^1$, with $\mathbb{F}_0 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$. ($\mathbb{F}_1$ is excluded because it has a $(-1)$-curve.)
  • Surfaces with $K_X$ nef — the "real" minimal models. These are sorted further by Kodaira dimension.

For the third class, Enriques' table gives a complete list:

$\kappa(X)$class of minimal $X$character
$-\infty$rational, ruled$X \to \text{curve}$ a $\mathbb{P}^1$-bundle
$0$abelian, K3, Enriques, hyperelliptic (bielliptic)$mK_X = 0$ for some $m$, distinguished by $b_1$ and torsion of $K_X$
$1$properly elliptic surfaces$X \to C$ an elliptic fibration with non-trivial $j$-invariant
$2$surfaces of general type$K_X$ big — vast and not classified case-by-case

The dichotomy at $\kappa = -\infty$ (ruled and rational) versus $\kappa \ge 0$ has a sharp criterion: $X$ is birational to $C \times \mathbb{P}^1$ for some curve $C$ (or to $\mathbb{P}^2$) iff $h^0(X, K_X^{\otimes m}) = 0$ for some $m \ge 1$, iff $K_X$ is not pseudo-effective. The $\kappa = 0$ family has a famous fourfold split — abelian, K3, Enriques, hyperelliptic — distinguished by the Betti number $b_1$ and the order of the canonical class in $\mathrm{Pic}(X)$. The $\kappa = 2$ class is enormous and remains an active subject of moduli theory.

4. Threefold MMP: small contractions and flips

Move to dimension three and the surface program almost works — but with a twist. The contraction theorem still produces $\phi_R : X \to Y$ for every $K_X$-negative extremal ray. Two of the three contraction types (fibre and divisorial) behave as in the surface case: $X$ becomes simpler, $\rho$ drops or the dimension drops, and we continue. The third type, small contractions, is genuinely new.

The small-contraction problem. A small contraction $\phi : X \to Y$ has $\dim X = \dim Y$ but exceptional locus of codimension $\ge 2$ — for a threefold, the contracted curves are isolated. Then $Y$ is no longer $\mathbb{Q}$-Gorenstein at the image points: the canonical class $K_Y$ is not even a $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor, so $\phi^* K_Y$ does not make sense. Without a well-defined canonical divisor on $Y$, the MMP cannot iterate — we cannot ask "is the new $K_Y$ nef?"

The solution, due to Mori, is to replace the small contraction with a different small modification on the other side.

Definition (flip). Let $\phi : X \to Y$ be a small contraction with $-K_X$ $\phi$-ample. A flip of $\phi$ is a small birational morphism $\phi^+ : X^+ \to Y$ from a different normal projective variety $X^+$ such that $K_{X^+}$ is $\phi^+$-ample.

The composition $X \dashrightarrow X^+$ is birational, an isomorphism away from the contracted locus, and exchanges the sign of $K \cdot C$ on the contracted ray: where $K_X \cdot C < 0$ on the original side, $K_{X^+} \cdot C^+ > 0$ on the flipped side. The Picard number is preserved (both $\phi$ and $\phi^+$ have relative Picard rank $1$), so a flip is genuinely "the same complexity, but with $K$ now positive on the contracted ray."

Existence and termination

Two questions emerge: (1) does the flip $X^+$ always exist? (2) does the flip game ever terminate, or could we flip forever?

Mori (1988). Flips exist and terminate for klt threefolds. The threefold MMP runs.

This was Mori's Fields-Medal work (1990). Existence is proved by an explicit local analysis at the singular point of $Y$ — there is a finite list of analytic types of threefold flipping contractions, classified by Kollár and Mori. Termination is the assertion that no infinite sequence of flips can occur, which Shokurov proved using the "difficulty" — a non-negative integer-valued invariant of the pair $(X, \Delta)$ that strictly decreases under flips.

BCHM (2010). For klt pairs $(X, \Delta)$ with $K_X + \Delta$ big and $\Delta$ big, flips exist in arbitrary dimension, the MMP with scaling terminates, and as a consequence, the canonical ring $R(X, K_X + \Delta) = \bigoplus_{m \ge 0} H^0(X, \lfloor m(K_X + \Delta) \rfloor)$ is finitely generated.

Birkar, Cascini, Hacon, and McKernan's 2010 theorem is the high-water mark of higher-dimensional MMP. It does not prove general termination of flips in dimension $\ge 4$ — this remains an outstanding conjecture — but it gives existence and termination of one specific flavor (MMP with scaling) which is enough for finite generation of the canonical ring. Birkar received the 2018 Fields Medal partly for further work in this circle on boundedness of Fano varieties.

5. Singularities of pairs and discrepancies

BCHM does not run on smooth varieties — it requires klt singularities. Worse, the MMP itself creates singularities: contracting an extremal divisor or performing a flip generally produces a non-smooth output. To run the program, we must pre-emptively widen our category to admit certain mild singularities, and we must measure how mild they are. The numerical measurement is the discrepancy.

Setup. Let $(X, \Delta)$ be a log pair: $X$ is a normal variety, $\Delta = \sum a_i D_i$ a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor with $0 \le a_i \le 1$, and $K_X + \Delta$ a $\mathbb{Q}$-Cartier divisor. Let $f : Y \to X$ be a log resolution: $Y$ is smooth, $f$ is proper birational, and $f^{-1}(\Delta) \cup \mathrm{Exc}(f)$ is a simple normal crossing divisor. The discrepancies are the rational numbers $a_E(X, \Delta) \in \mathbb{Q}$ defined by

$$K_Y \;=\; f^*(K_X + \Delta) \;+\; \sum_E a_E(X, \Delta)\, E,$$

summed over prime divisors $E$ on $Y$ (with $a_E = -a_i$ if $E$ is the strict transform of $D_i$ and $E$ is not exceptional).

The discrepancy $a_E$ measures how much $K_Y$ exceeds the pullback of $K_X + \Delta$ along $E$. Bigger discrepancies mean milder singularities. The infimum $\inf_E a_E(X, \Delta)$ over all divisors on all log resolutions is the key invariant; varying the resolution does not change the infimum, so it is a property of the pair.

The four singularity classes

The classes nest: smooth $\subsetneq$ terminal $\subsetneq$ canonical $\subsetneq$ klt $\subsetneq$ lc. Two-dimensional canonical singularities are exactly the rational double points (Du Val ADE), so the classification on surfaces is concrete. In higher dimensions, terminal singularities are rare and rigid; klt is the natural category for the modern MMP.

Why klt? Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing — the cohomological engine driving every MMP step — holds for klt pairs but fails on the boundary lc-but-not-klt. Closing klt under MMP operations (cone theorem, contractions, flips) requires care: extremal contractions of klt pairs again produce klt pairs (after replacing $\Delta$ with $\phi_*\Delta$), and flips preserve klt — but a step out of klt into lc would lose the vanishing theorem and the program would stall.

The adjunction formula

The other technical pillar is adjunction for log pairs. If $S$ is a normal prime divisor on $X$ with coefficient $1$ in $\Delta$, then

$$(K_X + S + \Delta')|_S \;=\; K_S + \mathrm{Diff}_S(\Delta'),$$

where $\Delta' = \Delta - S$ and $\mathrm{Diff}_S(\Delta')$ is the "different" — a boundary $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor on $S$ encoding how $\Delta'$ meets $S$ and how $S$ wraps into the singularities of $X$ along $S$. Iterated adjunction lets us run an MMP on the boundary itself, and is what makes induction on dimension viable.

6. Mori fibre spaces and the Sarkisov program

The MMP terminates with one of two possible outputs. If $\kappa(X) \ge 0$, the program produces a minimal model: a klt variety $X_{\min}$ with $K_{X_{\min}}$ nef. If $\kappa(X) = -\infty$, then $K_X$ stays negative at every step, and the program ends with a fibration whose general fibre is Fano.

Definition (Mori fibre space). A Mori fibre space is a contraction $\phi : X \to S$ from a $\mathbb{Q}$-factorial klt projective variety $X$ to a normal projective $S$ with $\dim S < \dim X$, $\rho(X/S) = 1$ (relative Picard rank one), and $-K_X$ relatively ample over $S$.

When $\dim S = 0$, $X$ is itself a Fano variety. When $0 < \dim S < \dim X$, $\phi$ is a Fano fibration: the general fibre $X_s$ is a smooth Fano variety, and the relative Picard group $\mathrm{Pic}(X/S)$ has rank $1$. Surface examples are $\mathbb{P}^2 \to \mathrm{pt}$ (Fano case) and $\mathbb{F}_n \to \mathbb{P}^1$ (the Hirzebruch fibration).

Birational classification of Mori fibre spaces

If $X$ has $\kappa = -\infty$, the MMP gives some Mori fibre space birational to $X$, but typically not a unique one. Two Mori fibre spaces in the same birational class are related by a sequence of birational moves; the question is whether these moves can be standardised.

Sarkisov program (Corti 1995, Hacon–McKernan 2013). Any birational map $f : X/S \dashrightarrow X'/S'$ between Mori fibre spaces factors as a finite chain of Sarkisov links, each of one of four types (I, II, III, IV) determined by the comparative dimensions of $S, S'$ and the contraction structure of the link.

A Sarkisov link is the simplest possible birational modification between Mori fibre spaces: it is obtained by running a relative MMP, and consists of an extremal divisorial contraction or flip on one side, then an inverse step on the other. Together with Castelnuovo's surface theorem (which is the dimension-2 case), the Sarkisov program gives a structure theorem for the birational geometry of varieties with $\kappa = -\infty$.

Birational rigidity

The Sarkisov program frames a deep classification question: given a Fano variety $X$, how many distinct Mori fibre spaces are birational to $X$? The extreme answer is "only $X$ itself," in which case $X$ is called birationally rigid. By the Sarkisov framework, $X$ is rigid iff every Sarkisov link from $X/\mathrm{pt}$ comes back to $X/\mathrm{pt}$, which forces $\mathrm{Bir}(X) = \mathrm{Aut}(X)$.

The first non-trivial example is Iskovskikh–Manin's 1971 theorem: a smooth quartic threefold $X_4 \subset \mathbb{P}^4$ is birationally rigid. This was a landmark — $X_4$ is unirational (covered by a degree-2 map from $\mathbb{P}^3$ via projection from a line) but not rational, the first negative answer to the Lüroth problem in dimension three. Birational rigidity of smooth Fano hypersurfaces $X_n \subset \mathbb{P}^n$ for $n \ge 4$ was extended to all degrees $n$ by Pukhlikov, de Fernex, and others; the picture for moderate-degree hypersurfaces and complete intersections is still a frontier.

7. Connections

The MMP is the spine of higher-dimensional algebraic geometry. The threefold case is closed (Mori 1988, Kollár–Mori survey 1998); the higher-dimensional case is open in two crucial places — termination of flips in dimension $\ge 4$ and the abundance conjecture (that every nef minimal model has $|mK|$ basepoint-free for some $m$). In characteristic $p$ the analogue is largely missing: Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing fails (Raynaud), and the modern framework only began to take shape in dimension three with the work of Hacon–Witaszek and Kawakami in the 2020s.

  • Positivity and ample line bundles — Nef, big, and the cone of curves — the convex-geometric language ($\mathrm{Amp}, \mathrm{Nef}, \overline{NE}$) in which the cone theorem is stated.
  • Resolution of singularities — Applications and frontiers — log resolutions are the input format for every discrepancy computation; resolution is the entry point to the program.
  • Algebraic curves: higher genus — The canonical embedding — the dimension-1 story: $K_C$ ample iff $g \ge 2$, the canonical map embeds non-hyperelliptic curves, $\kappa = 1 = \dim C$.
  • Hodge theory — Hodge decomposition — Kodaira dimension is part of the Hodge-theoretic classification of varieties; pluricanonical sections are $H^0(X, \Omega^d_X)^{\otimes m}$.
  • Toric varieties — The toric dictionary — toric MMP is fully algorithmic: subdividing the fan corresponds to MMP steps, and toric varieties are the ground truth on which combinatorial intuitions are tested.
  • Moduli spaces — birational geometry of moduli spaces ($\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$, K-moduli of Fanos) is an active frontier where MMP techniques meet GIT.
