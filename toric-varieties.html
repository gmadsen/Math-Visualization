Toric varieties

Algebraic geometry where every theorem is a picture in $\mathbb{R}^n$: lattices and cones turn into varieties, and fan combinatorics turns into intersection numbers.

1. Lattices, cones, fans

The torus $T = (\mathbb{C}^\times)^n$ is the most symmetric of algebraic groups. A toric variety is a normal variety $X$ containing $T$ as an open dense subvariety, in such a way that the multiplication of $T$ on itself extends to an action of $T$ on all of $X$. So toric varieties are "varieties with torus symmetry baked in." The miracle is that they are classified — exactly and entirely — by a piece of combinatorial data living in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Fix two dual lattices: the cocharacter lattice $N \cong \mathbb{Z}^n$ (one-parameter subgroups $\mathbb{C}^\times \to T$) and its dual character lattice $M = \Hom(N,\mathbb{Z})$ (characters $T \to \mathbb{C}^\times$). Tensor with $\mathbb{R}$ to get the real vector spaces $N_\mathbb{R}, M_\mathbb{R}$. All the geometry will be drawn in these.

Cone. A strongly convex rational polyhedral cone $\sigma \subset N_\mathbb{R}$ is the non-negative span $\sigma = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} v_1 + \cdots + \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} v_k$ of finitely many lattice vectors $v_i \in N$, subject to the condition that $\sigma \cap (-\sigma) = \{0\}$ (no whole line lies inside).

Fan. A fan $\Sigma$ in $N_\mathbb{R}$ is a finite collection of such cones, closed under taking faces, and such that $\sigma \cap \tau$ is a face of each whenever $\sigma, \tau \in \Sigma$.

The closure-under-faces condition is the gluing condition. The face-intersection condition is the "schemes glue along open subschemes" condition. Together they force every fan to assemble into one global toric variety $X_\Sigma$.

Strong convexity is the constraint that the cone "points": its tip is a unique lowest point, not a whole line. This will translate, on the variety side, into the existence of a special torus-fixed point at the corresponding cone, rather than a free torus orbit you can never collapse.

2. Affine toric varieties $U_\sigma$

Now the bridge: how does a single cone $\sigma \subset N_\mathbb{R}$ become an actual variety?

The dual cone $\sigma^\vee \subset M_\mathbb{R}$ is the set of linear forms that are non-negative on $\sigma$:

$$\sigma^\vee \;=\; \{\,u \in M_\mathbb{R} \;:\; \langle u, v\rangle \ge 0 \text{ for all } v \in \sigma\,\}.$$

Intersect with the lattice: $S_\sigma := \sigma^\vee \cap M$ is a finitely generated semigroup (Gordan's lemma). Each $u \in S_\sigma$ is a Laurent monomial $\chi^u = x_1^{u_1}\cdots x_n^{u_n}$, viewed as a regular function on the torus $T = \Spec\mathbb{C}[M]$. The monomials in $S_\sigma$ are precisely the ones that extend from $T$ to a larger affine variety. That variety is the answer:

Definition. The affine toric variety attached to $\sigma$ is $$U_\sigma \;=\; \Spec \mathbb{C}[\sigma^\vee \cap M] \;=\; \Spec\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma].$$

The torus $T$ sits inside as the open dense subvariety where every $\chi^u$ is invertible. The boundary $U_\sigma \setminus T$ is precisely where the new monomials forced by $S_\sigma$ are allowed to vanish.

The model example: $\sigma = \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^2$ gives $\mathbb{A}^2$

Let $\sigma$ be the first quadrant in $N_\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2$, generated by $e_1 = (1,0)$ and $e_2 = (0,1)$. The dual cone is again the first quadrant in $M_\mathbb{R}$, with $S_\sigma = \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^2$. The corresponding semigroup ring is

$$\mathbb{C}[S_\sigma] \;=\; \mathbb{C}[\chi^{e_1^*}, \chi^{e_2^*}] \;=\; \mathbb{C}[x, y],$$

so $U_\sigma = \Spec\mathbb{C}[x,y] = \mathbb{A}^2$. The torus $T = (\mathbb{C}^\times)^2$ is exactly the locus $xy \neq 0$. The boundary is the union of the two coordinate axes — and these will turn out to be the toric divisors $D_{e_1}, D_{e_2}$.

The pattern is general. Smaller cones produce more Laurent monomials — at the extreme, $\sigma = \{0\}$ gives all of $M$, hence $U_{\{0\}} = T$. Bigger cones produce fewer monomials and a smaller variety with a richer boundary; at the extreme, a maximal-dimensional cone produces an affine variety with a single torus-fixed point.

3. The toric dictionary

Now we glue. Given a fan $\Sigma$, take the disjoint union of the affine pieces $U_\sigma$ for $\sigma \in \Sigma$ and identify $U_\sigma$ with $U_\tau$ along $U_{\sigma \cap \tau}$. The face condition makes the gluings consistent. The result is one variety

$$X_\Sigma \;=\; \bigcup_{\sigma \in \Sigma} U_\sigma,$$

and every $U_\sigma$ sits inside as an open affine, with $T = U_{\{0\}}$ the open dense torus orbit shared by all of them.

The miracle is how cleanly the geometry of $X_\Sigma$ reads off from the combinatorics of $\Sigma$. The book of dictionary entries is long; here are the most useful pages.

Two of these dictionary entries deserve to be unpacked.

smoothness A maximal cone $\sigma$ in dimension $n$ is generated by $n$ rays $u_1, \ldots, u_n$. Smoothness of $X_\Sigma$ at the corresponding fixed point is precisely the condition that $\{u_1, \ldots, u_n\}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-basis of $N$ — equivalently, that the determinant of the matrix whose rows are the $u_i$ has determinant $\pm 1$. If the determinant is some other integer $d$, the singularity is a finite quotient $\mathbb{A}^n / (\mathbb{Z}/d)$. The fan-builder widget in §1 reports this in real time.

refinement A refinement $\Sigma' \to \Sigma$ subdivides the cones of $\Sigma$. On the variety side, this is a proper birational morphism $X_{\Sigma'} \to X_\Sigma$. Resolution of singularities for toric varieties amounts to choosing a refinement that is smooth — and the existence of such refinements is constructive and combinatorial. (No general resolution theorem needed; the fan tells you exactly what to subdivide.)

4. Worked examples

Now the parade. Each fan below builds a familiar variety.

Five fans, five varieties
step through the buttons

Why $\mathbb{P}^n$ has the fan it has

Fix the lattice $N = \mathbb{Z}^n$ with standard basis $e_1, \ldots, e_n$. Define one extra vector $e_0 = -e_1 - \cdots - e_n$. The fan $\Sigma_{\mathbb{P}^n}$ has $n+1$ rays through $e_0, e_1, \ldots, e_n$ and a maximal cone for every $n$-subset of these rays. Every face is convex (since the $e_i$ sum to zero) and the fan covers all of $N_\mathbb{R}$ (so $\mathbb{P}^n$ is complete). The $n+1$ maximal cones give the $n+1$ standard affine charts $U_i = \{x_i \neq 0\} \cong \mathbb{A}^n$, glued exactly the way projective space is glued.

Hirzebruch surfaces $F_n$ as a one-parameter family

Take the fan of $\mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$ — four rays $\pm e_1, \pm e_2$ — and tilt the ray $-e_1$ to $-e_1 + n\,e_2$ for an integer $n \ge 0$. The result is the fan of $F_n$, the $n$th Hirzebruch surface, a smooth projective ruled surface. The toric divisor coming from the now-tilted ray has self-intersection $-n$. So $F_0 = \mathbb{P}^1 \times \mathbb{P}^1$, $F_1 = \mathrm{Bl}_p \mathbb{P}^2$, and $F_n$ for $n \ge 2$ have a unique negative-self-intersection curve, which obstructs them from being products. A whole arithmetic family generated by tilting one ray.

Blowing up the origin of $\mathbb{A}^2$

Start with $\sigma = \langle e_1, e_2 \rangle$ giving $U_\sigma = \mathbb{A}^2$. Add one new ray through the lattice point $(1,1)$, splitting $\sigma$ into $\sigma_1 = \langle e_1, (1,1) \rangle$ and $\sigma_2 = \langle (1,1), e_2 \rangle$. Both new cones are smooth (they're $\mathbb{Z}$-bases of $N$). The new ray contributes a new toric divisor — the exceptional $\mathbb{P}^1$. Computing self-intersection: from $e_1 + e_2 = (1,1) = 1\cdot(1,1)$ we read off $a = 1$, so $E \cdot E = -a = -1$. We have just rebuilt the blowup of $\mathbb{A}^2$ at the origin, completely combinatorially.

5. Cox ring and intersection numbers

The character ring $\mathbb{C}[M]$ is the ring of functions on the open torus. To go to global functions on $X_\Sigma$ — and in fact to the total coordinate ring — we package together the line bundles on $X_\Sigma$.

Each ray $\rho \in \Sigma^{(1)}$ contributes a torus-invariant divisor $D_\rho$. These divisors generate the divisor class group:

$$\mathrm{Cl}(X_\Sigma) \;=\; \bigoplus_{\rho \in \Sigma^{(1)}} \mathbb{Z}\,D_\rho \;\Big/\; \big\langle \,\sum_\rho \langle u, u_\rho\rangle\, D_\rho \,:\, u \in M\,\big\rangle.$$

The relations come from principal divisors: a character $\chi^u$ is a global rational function whose divisor is $\sum_\rho \langle u, u_\rho\rangle\, D_\rho$, where $u_\rho$ is the primitive lattice generator of $\rho$. Modding out gives $\mathrm{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$.

Cox ring (total coordinate ring). $$\mathrm{Cox}(X_\Sigma) \;=\; \bigoplus_{[D] \in \mathrm{Cl}(X_\Sigma)} H^0(X_\Sigma, \mathcal{O}_{X_\Sigma}(D)).$$ Concretely it is the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[x_\rho \,:\, \rho \in \Sigma^{(1)}]$ — one variable per ray — graded by $\mathrm{Cl}(X_\Sigma)$ via $\deg x_\rho = [D_\rho]$.

For $\mathbb{P}^n$, the rays are $e_0, \ldots, e_n$ with the relation $\sum e_i = 0$ giving $D_{e_0} + \cdots + D_{e_n} = 0$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{P}^n) = \mathbb{Z}\cdot H$, so each $D_{e_i} = H$ has degree $1$. Hence $\mathrm{Cox}(\mathbb{P}^n) = \mathbb{C}[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ graded by $\deg x_i = 1$ — exactly the homogeneous coordinate ring you already know.

Intersection numbers from the fan

Toric divisors intersect by a recipe entirely encoded in $\Sigma$. On a smooth complete toric surface $X_\Sigma$ with rays cyclically ordered $\rho_1, \rho_2, \ldots, \rho_d$ (with $\rho_{d+1} = \rho_1$):

  • $D_{\rho_i} \cdot D_{\rho_j} = 1$ if $\rho_i, \rho_j$ are adjacent rays (i.e. span a $2$-cone of $\Sigma$);
  • $D_{\rho_i} \cdot D_{\rho_j} = 0$ if $\rho_i, \rho_j$ are not adjacent;
  • $D_{\rho_i} \cdot D_{\rho_i} = -a_i$, where $a_i$ is the integer in the lattice relation $u_{\rho_{i-1}} + u_{\rho_{i+1}} = a_i\, u_{\rho_i}$ (Cox–Little–Schenck convention; Fulton writes the same sign).

The pencil-and-paper calculation $H \cdot H = 1$ on $\mathbb{P}^2$:

Take $H = D_{e_i}$ for any one $i$; all three agree in $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{P}^2) = \mathbb{Z}$. Compute $D_{e_0} \cdot D_{e_0}$: the rays $e_1, e_2$ neighbour $e_0$, and from $e_0 + e_1 + e_2 = 0$ we get $e_1 + e_2 = -e_0 = a_0\, e_0$ with $a_0 = -1$. Hence $H \cdot H = D_{e_0}^2 = -a_0 = 1$. ✓

6. Mirror symmetry preview

The most striking application of toric varieties to "exotic" geometry is Batyrev's construction of mirror Calabi-Yau pairs. The ingredients are mostly polytopes, and the output is two families of CY hypersurfaces whose Hodge numbers are swapped in a way that string theorists predicted on physical grounds.

Reflexive polytope. A lattice polytope $\Delta \subset M_\mathbb{R}$ containing the origin in its interior is reflexive iff the polar dual

$$\Delta^\circ \;=\; \{\, v \in N_\mathbb{R} \,:\, \langle v, m\rangle \ge -1 \text{ for all } m \in \Delta\,\}$$

is itself a lattice polytope.

Equivalently, $0$ is the unique interior lattice point of $\Delta$ and every facet of $\Delta$ has lattice distance $1$ from the origin. Reflexivity is rare and rigid — there are exactly $16$ reflexive polygons in dimension $2$ (corresponding to the $16$ Gorenstein toric Fano surfaces), $4319$ in dimension $3$, and a famous Kreuzer-Skarke list of $473\,800\,776$ in dimension $4$.

Batyrev's construction. A reflexive polytope $\Delta$ has a normal fan $\Sigma_\Delta$ (the rays are inner normals to facets, the maximal cones are normal cones at vertices). The toric variety $X_\Delta := X_{\Sigma_\Delta}$ is a Gorenstein toric Fano variety. A generic anticanonical hypersurface in $X_\Delta$ is a Calabi-Yau variety. Batyrev showed: the analogous hypersurface in $X_{\Delta^\circ}$ is the mirror Calabi-Yau, with Hodge numbers $h^{1,1}$ and $h^{n-1,1}$ swapped:

$$h^{1,1}\!\big(X_\Delta^{\mathrm{CY}}\big) \;=\; h^{n-1,1}\!\big(X_{\Delta^\circ}^{\mathrm{CY}}\big), \qquad h^{n-1,1}\!\big(X_\Delta^{\mathrm{CY}}\big) \;=\; h^{1,1}\!\big(X_{\Delta^\circ}^{\mathrm{CY}}\big).$$

The quintic threefold $\{f_5 = 0\} \subset \mathbb{P}^4$ is the historical poster child: $\mathbb{P}^4$ is $X_{\Delta_4}$ for the $4$-simplex $\Delta_4$ (a reflexive polytope), and $\Delta_4^\circ$ gives a different toric Fano whose generic anticanonical hypersurface is the mirror quintic, with the famous $h^{1,1}$/$h^{2,1}$ swap $(1, 101) \leftrightarrow (101, 1)$.

This combinatorial mirror was the first systematic recipe for producing mirror pairs and remains the single richest source of explicit examples. Going further — establishing mirror symmetry as an equivalence of categories (homological mirror symmetry) or matching enumerative invariants (Gromov-Witten = period integrals) — is the work of the next several pages.

Connections

Toric geometry is the cleanest test bed in algebraic geometry: every concept is a picture, every theorem becomes a check on lattice combinatorics, and counterexamples are easy to construct. The frontier today is in going beyond toric: log Calabi-Yau pairs, T-varieties (with smaller torus actions), tropicalizations of non-toric varieties, and combinatorial mirror constructions for spaces that aren't themselves toric.

  • Schemes — Proj construction — the alternative path to $\mathbb{P}^n$, here recovered as a toric variety glued from $n+1$ affine charts.
  • Intersection theory & Chow groups — $A^*(\mathbb{P}^n)$ — toric intersection numbers compute the Chow ring directly from the fan.
  • Calabi–Yau manifolds — examples — most explicit CY 3-folds in the literature are anticanonical hypersurfaces in toric Fano fourfolds.
  • Mirror symmetry — the quintic prediction — Batyrev's reflexive polytope swap is the source of the original combinatorial mirror.
  • Resolution of singularities — toric singularities are resolved by combinatorial subdivision; the general case is harder by orders of magnitude.
