Complex analysis — amplitwist, contours, residues

A Needham-flavored tour: holomorphic maps as local rotations, Cauchy's theorem as a curl-free vector field, residues as bookkeeping for poles, and conformal maps as the geometry of $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$.

1. Complex numbers

The complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ are $\mathbb{R}^2$ with multiplication added. A complex number $z=x+iy = re^{i\theta}$ is a point with a length and an angle. The metric is the Euclidean one: $|z-w| = \sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2+(y_1-y_2)^2}$.

Cartesian form $z=x+iy$ does the addition; polar form $z=re^{i\theta}$ does the multiplication. The two are linked by Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, and the multiplication rule is

$$(r_1 e^{i\theta_1})(r_2 e^{i\theta_2}) = r_1 r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}.$$

Multiplication is rotation × scaling. Multiplying by $z=re^{i\theta}$ scales the plane by $r$ and rotates it by $\theta$. Multiplying by $i$ rotates a quarter-turn; squaring doubles the angle and squares the radius. This single picture — every nonzero complex number is a similarity of the plane — is the seed from which the whole subject grows.

Conjugate, modulus, inverse. The conjugate $\bar z = x-iy$ is reflection across the real axis. Then $z\bar z = |z|^2 = r^2$, which gives $z^{-1} = \bar z / |z|^2$ — division reduces to multiplication by a conjugate.

Why $\mathbb{C}$ is the algebraic closure of $\mathbb{R}$. Adjoining a single root of $x^2+1=0$ to $\mathbb{R}$ already produces a field in which every polynomial of positive degree splits — the fundamental theorem of algebra (§13). One quadratic root is all the algebra needs; everything else is forced.

Geometrically, $\mathbb{C}$ is just $\mathbb{R}^2$ as a metric space. What's new is the multiplicative structure, and the surprise of complex analysis is how much rigidity that single binary operation buys. The next sections lift the picture to the Riemann sphere $\hat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$, where Möbius maps act as global rotations.

2. Riemann sphere

For complex analysis the right ambient space is not $\mathbb{C}$ but its one-point compactification, the Riemann sphere $\hat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$. Place the sphere of radius $\tfrac12$ tangent to the plane at the origin and project from the north pole; this is stereographic projection. The plane corresponds to the sphere minus the north pole, and the formal symbol $\infty$ is the north pole itself.

Two facts make stereographic projection the natural picture:

conformal it preserves angles, and circles ↔ circles it sends circles on the sphere to circles or lines in the plane (a line being a "circle through $\infty$").

3. Möbius transformations

A Möbius transformation is any map $T(z) = \dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}$ with $ad-bc\ne 0$. Three remarkable facts:

(i) Möbius transformations form a group, isomorphic to $\mathrm{PGL}_2(\mathbb{C})$.
(ii) On $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ they are precisely the conformal automorphisms — bijective angle-preserving maps.
(iii) On the sphere they are exactly the rigid rotations and reflections combined with dilations — but if you restrict to the subgroup that preserves the spherical metric, you recover $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ acting as the rotation group of the sphere, realized in coordinates as $T(z) = \dfrac{az - \bar c}{cz + \bar a}$ with $|a|^2+|c|^2=1$ (i.e.\ $\mathrm{SU}(2)/\{\pm 1\}$).

A Möbius transformation is determined by what it does to three points: given any two triples of distinct points $(z_1,z_2,z_3)$ and $(w_1,w_2,w_3)$ in $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$, there is a unique $T$ with $T(z_i)=w_i$. The "three points" are the analogue of "two points determine a line" in projective geometry.

The invariant of four points under all Möbius transformations is the cross-ratio

$$[z_1,z_2;z_3,z_4] \;=\; \frac{(z_1-z_3)(z_2-z_4)}{(z_1-z_4)(z_2-z_3)}.$$

Cross-ratio is real iff the four points are concyclic — a tidy way to detect "four points on a circle" using only complex arithmetic.

4. Amplitwist

A function $f:U\to\mathbb{C}$ on an open set $U\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ is holomorphic at $z_0$ if

$$f'(z_0) \;=\; \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}$$

exists as a complex limit — meaning the limit is the same no matter how $h\in\mathbb{C}$ approaches $0$. This is much more restrictive than $\mathbb{R}^2$-differentiability.

Amplitwist. Needham's word: near $z_0$, a holomorphic map acts on infinitesimal vectors as rotation by $\arg f'(z_0)$ and scaling by $|f'(z_0)|$. That single complex number $f'(z_0)$ encodes both. Wherever $f'(z_0)\ne 0$, $f$ is conformal: it preserves angles between curves through $z_0$.

Compare: the map $z\mapsto \bar z$ is real-differentiable but not holomorphic. Its Jacobian is a reflection, not a rotation+scaling. Reflections reverse orientation, so $\bar z$ is "anti-conformal" — it preserves angle magnitudes but flips signs.

5. Cauchy–Riemann equations

Write $f = u+iv$, viewed as a map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$. Real-differentiability gives a Jacobian

$$Df = \begin{pmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{pmatrix}.$$

For $f$ to be complex-differentiable, this Jacobian must commute with multiplication by $i$ — i.e.\ it must be a similarity matrix (a rotation composed with a scaling). A short calculation shows this is equivalent to the Cauchy–Riemann equations

$$u_x = v_y, \qquad u_y = -v_x,$$

and then $f'(z_0) = u_x + i v_x = v_y - i u_y$.

6. Holomorphic functions

A function $f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{C}$ on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}$ is holomorphic at $z_0$ if

$$f'(z_0) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(z_0+h) - f(z_0)}{h}$$

exists, with the limit taken over all ways $h\to 0$ in $\mathbb{C}$. The single complex limit packs in every approach direction at once — the source of the rigidity. Equivalently (§5), $f = u+iv$ with $u_x = v_y$ and $u_y = -v_x$ (Cauchy–Riemann); equivalently again, $Df(z_0)$ is a rotation–scaling, the amplitwist.

Examples. Polynomials and power series are holomorphic on their disk of convergence; $e^z$, $\sin z$, $\cos z$ are entire; $1/z$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$; the principal branch of $\log z$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]$. Non-examples. $\bar z$, $|z|^2$, and $\Re z$ are smooth as maps $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ but fail Cauchy–Riemann; their differential at every point includes a reflection or projection, not a similarity.

The amplitwist picture is what makes complex analysis qualitatively different from $\mathbb{R}^2$ analysis. Every theorem we are about to see is, in some sense, a topological consequence of "all infinitesimal motion is rotation + scaling." Cauchy's theorem (§9) says the closed-loop integral of a holomorphic function vanishes; the integral formula (§10) recovers interior values from boundary data; analyticity (§11) upgrades "once differentiable" to "infinitely differentiable, equal to its Taylor series." None of these have analogues for merely smooth $\mathbb{R}^2$-valued maps.

7. Domain coloring

A picture of a complex function $f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ requires four real dimensions. The standard 2D summary: encode $\arg f(z)$ as hue, $|f(z)|$ as lightness, and plot over the $z$-plane. Zeros become darkness sinks, poles become bright cones, and a winding rainbow around any point reveals its order — $n$ rainbows for a zero of order $n$, $n$ rainbows the other way for a pole of order $n$.

8. Contour integrals

A contour is a piecewise smooth curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}$. Given a continuous $f$ on its image, define

$$\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz \;=\; \int_a^b f(\gamma(t))\,\gamma'(t)\,dt.$$

Read $dz = dx + i\,dy$ as a complex-valued 1-form. Then $f\,dz = (u+iv)(dx+i\,dy) = (u\,dx - v\,dy) + i(v\,dx + u\,dy)$, two real line integrals.

The key observation: those two real 1-forms are closed exactly when $u,v$ satisfy Cauchy–Riemann. The "real part" 1-form $u\,dx - v\,dy$ has $d = (-v_x - u_y)\,dx\wedge dy$, which vanishes iff $u_y = -v_x$. The "imaginary part" 1-form $v\,dx + u\,dy$ has $d = (u_x - v_y)\,dx\wedge dy$, vanishing iff $u_x = v_y$. Both pieces are closed precisely when $f$ is holomorphic.

Curl-free, source-free. Think of $\overline{f(z)}$ as a vector field on the plane. Cauchy–Riemann equations say its divergence and curl both vanish: it is incompressible and irrotational. That is the physical content of "$f$ is holomorphic," and it is why integrals around closed loops vanish.

9. Cauchy's theorem

Cauchy's theorem (star-shaped form). If $f$ is holomorphic on a star-shaped open set $U$ and $\gamma\subset U$ is a closed contour, then $\oint_\gamma f\,dz = 0$.

Sketch: holomorphic $f$ on a star-shaped (in particular simply connected) domain has an antiderivative $F$ — define $F(z) = \int_{z_*}^z f$ along straight rays from the star centre. Then $\oint f\,dz = F(\text{end}) - F(\text{start}) = 0$ for a closed loop. Equivalently: by Stokes, $\oint_\gamma f\,dz = \iint_D df\wedge dz$, but $df = f'\,dz$ so $df\wedge dz = 0$.

The general (Goursat) version drops the $C^1$ hypothesis on $f'$ and works for any simply connected $U$. The proof bisects triangles and is elegant; we won't reproduce it here.

10. Cauchy integral formula

If $f$ is holomorphic on a domain containing the closed disk $\overline{D(z_0,r)}$, then for every interior point $a\in D(z_0,r)$,

$$f(a) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-z_0|=r}\frac{f(z)}{z-a}\,dz.$$

The values of $f$ on the boundary determine $f$ in the interior — a phenomenon with no analogue in real analysis. Differentiating under the integral $n$ times,

$$f^{(n)}(a) \;=\; \frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint \frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz.$$

Holomorphic functions are infinitely differentiable. That is the main miracle.

11. Analyticity = holomorphy

From Cauchy's integral formula nearly everything follows.

If $f$ is holomorphic on $D(z_0,R)$, then for any $a\in D(z_0,R)$, the geometric series in $1/(z-a) = 1/((z-z_0) - (a-z_0))$ converges uniformly on $|z-z_0|=r$ for $|a-z_0|

$$f(a) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n (a-z_0)^n, \qquad c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint \frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}dz = \frac{f^{(n)}(z_0)}{n!}.$$

Holomorphic ⟺ analytic, with radius of convergence equal to the distance from $z_0$ to the nearest singularity.

12. Liouville's theorem

An entire function is one that is holomorphic on all of $\mathbb{C}$. The next theorem is the most striking example of complex-analytic rigidity: in real analysis, $\sin x$ is bounded and nonconstant; over $\mathbb{C}$, no such function exists.

Liouville's theorem. A bounded entire function is constant.

Proof. Apply the CIF for $f'$ on a circle of radius $r$ around any $z_0$:

$$|f'(z_0)| = \left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-z_0|=r} \frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^2}\,dz\right| \;\le\; \frac{M}{r}.$$

Since $f$ is entire, $r$ may be taken arbitrarily large, forcing $f'(z_0)=0$. The point $z_0$ was arbitrary, so $f$ is constant.

Two quick consequences worth holding in mind. First, $\sin z$ and $\cos z$ are entire but unbounded on $\mathbb{C}$ (they grow like $e^{|y|}$ off the real axis). Second, every nonconstant polynomial is entire and unbounded — which is exactly what the next section, the FTA, will turn into a proof that polynomials must have roots.

Sharper version. If $|f(z)|\le A + B|z|^k$ for $|z|$ large, then $f$ is a polynomial of degree $\le k$. The same CIF estimate kills $f^{(k+1)}$.

13. Fundamental theorem of algebra

Fundamental theorem of algebra. Every nonconstant polynomial $p(z)$ has a complex root. Proof 1: if $p$ never vanishes, $1/p$ is bounded entire (it tends to $0$ at infinity), hence constant by Liouville — absurd.

The geometric proof of FTA. Consider a circle of huge radius $R$. On it, $p(z)\sim a_n z^n$, so $p$ wraps $|z|=R$ around $0$ exactly $n$ times. Now shrink the circle to a point. At radius $0$, $p$ is constant $p(0)$, wrapping $0$ times. Winding number is integer-valued and varies continuously, so somewhere along the way it must cross zero — and that means $p$ hits $0$ at some intermediate $z$.

14. Maximum modulus principle

Morera. If $f$ is continuous on $U$ and $\oint_\Delta f = 0$ for every triangle $\Delta\subset U$, then $f$ is holomorphic. (Converse to Cauchy.)

Maximum modulus. If $f$ is holomorphic on $U$ and $|f|$ attains a local max at an interior point, $f$ is constant. (Hence on a compact domain, $|f|$ achieves its sup on the boundary.)

Open mapping. A nonconstant holomorphic map sends open sets to open sets. (One way to see it: $f-w_0$ has finitely many zeros, none on a small circle, and Rouché shows $f-w$ has the same number for all $w$ near $w_0$.)

15. Open mapping theorem

Maximum modulus says a non-constant $f$ on a domain $\Omega$ never attains $|f|$-maxima inside. The topological dual is the open mapping theorem: $f$ doesn't just avoid pinning local maxima — it spreads every open set to another open set.

Open mapping theorem. Let $f$ be holomorphic on a connected domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{C}$. Then either $f$ is constant, or $f$ is an open map: $f(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{C}$ for every open $U \subseteq \Omega$.

Why. Pick $z_0 \in U$ with $w_0 = f(z_0)$. Choose $r > 0$ small enough that $\overline{D}(z_0, r) \subset U$ and $f(z) \ne w_0$ for $z \in \partial D(z_0, r)$ (possible since zeros of the non-constant holomorphic $f - w_0$ are isolated). Let $\delta = \min_{|z - z_0| = r} |f(z) - w_0| > 0$. For any $w$ with $|w - w_0| < \delta$, the argument principle (Rouché) shows $f(z) = w$ has the same number of solutions in $D(z_0, r)$ as $f(z) = w_0$, which is $\ge 1$. So $D(w_0, \delta) \subset f(U)$, and $f(U)$ is open at every point.

Two clean consequences.

  • Local biholomorphism criterion. If $f'(z_0) \ne 0$, then $f$ is a biholomorphism on a neighborhood of $z_0$. (Solutions of $f(z) = w$ are simple and unique near $z_0$ once $w$ is close to $w_0$.) Inverse function theorem on the holomorphic side.
  • Maximum-modulus reproof. If $|f|$ has an interior maximum at $z_0$, then $f(z_0)$ is a boundary point of $f(\Omega)$ — but openness forces $f(z_0)$ to be interior. Contradiction unless $f$ is constant.

Open mapping plus maximum modulus together encode the rigidity of holomorphy at the topological / algebraic level: holomorphic maps either freeze (constant) or spread (open). No middle behavior. The same dichotomy reappears one variable up as Hartogs's extension theorem in several complex variables — extension is forced because the alternatives are too rigid.

16. Schwarz lemma

Schwarz lemma. If $f\colon\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}$ is holomorphic with $f(0)=0$, then $|f(z)|\le |z|$ and $|f'(0)|\le 1$. Equality at any interior $z$ forces $f(z) = e^{i\theta}z$.

Proof. Define $g(z) = f(z)/z$ — holomorphic on $\mathbb{D}$ because $f(0)=0$ removes the apparent singularity (§18). On the circle $|z|=r<1$, $|g(z)|\le 1/r$. Maximum modulus (§14) propagates the bound to the interior, then $r\to 1$ gives $|g|\le 1$ everywhere. Equality at any interior point makes $|g|$ attain its max inside $\mathbb{D}$, forcing $g$ to be a constant of modulus $1$.

The lemma is short to state, but the consequences are sweeping. It is a rigidity result: a self-map of the disk fixing $0$ is either a rotation or strictly contracts the origin. There is no "dilation by $0.99$ near $0$ then expansion further out" — the boundary constraint nails everything down.

Schwarz–Pick. Removing the assumption $f(0)=0$ and conjugating by Möbius maps gives the version that respects the hyperbolic geometry of $\mathbb{D}$:

$$\left|\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{1-\overline{f(w)}f(z)}\right| \;\le\; \left|\frac{z-w}{1-\bar w z}\right|.$$

Holomorphic self-maps of the disk are nonexpanding in the Poincaré metric, and equality at one pair forces $f$ to be a Möbius automorphism — a holomorphic isometry. Every disk automorphism therefore has the form $z\mapsto e^{i\theta}(z-a)/(1-\bar a z)$ (§23), parameterized by a phase and a fixed point in $\mathbb{D}$. Schwarz is the seed of all uniformization-flavored arguments downstream: it powers the proof of the Riemann mapping theorem (§25) and the classification of $\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{D})$.

17. Laurent series

Drop the assumption that $f$ is holomorphic on the whole disc; allow an isolated singularity at $z_0$. On any annulus $r_1 < |z - z_0| < r_2$ on which $f$ is holomorphic, we have a unique Laurent expansion

$$f(z) \;=\; \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty c_n (z-z_0)^n,$$

convergent on the annulus and divergent outside. The coefficients are still given by Cauchy's formula:

$$c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-z_0|=r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-z_0)^{n+1}}\,dz, \qquad r_1 < r < r_2.$$

The radius $r$ inside the annulus does not matter — Cauchy's theorem says the integral is contour-independent in the holomorphic region. Split into the regular part $\sum_{n\ge 0} c_n(z-z_0)^n$ and the principal part $\sum_{n<0} c_n(z-z_0)^n$. The regular part is just the Taylor series you would get if $z_0$ were a nice point; the principal part is the bookkeeping for whatever singularity $f$ has at $z_0$, and §18 reads off the type of singularity directly from how many negative-power terms appear.

Worked example: $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{z(z-1)}$ around $z_0=0$. Partial fractions give $f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} - \frac{1}{1-z}$. On the annulus $0<|z|<1$, expand the second term as a geometric series:

$$f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} - \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n \;=\; -\frac{1}{z} - 1 - z - z^2 - \cdots.$$

Same $f$, different annulus $|z|>1$: now $\frac{1}{1-z} = -\frac{1}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{1-1/z} = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{-n-1}$, giving

$$f(z) = -\frac{1}{z} + \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^{-n-1} \;=\; \sum_{n=2}^\infty z^{-n}.$$

Same function, two utterly different Laurent series — uniqueness is per annulus, not per function.

Worked example: $e^{1/z}$ around $z_0=0$. Substitute into the Taylor series for $e^w$: $e^{1/z} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n! z^n}$. Infinitely many negative-power coefficients — an essential singularity (§18), and the residue is $c_{-1}=1$.

The coefficient $c_{-1}$ is the residue of $f$ at $z_0$, the only one that survives integration around a small loop. That observation drives §19, the residue theorem, which turns awkward real integrals into mechanical contour calculations.

18. Classification of singularities

An isolated singularity at $z_0$ is a point where $f$ is holomorphic on some punctured disk $0<|z-z_0|principal part $\sum_{n<0} c_n(z-z_0)^n$:

removable all $c_{n}=0$ for $n<0$ — $f$ extends holomorphically across $z_0$ (Riemann's removable-singularity theorem: this happens iff $f$ is bounded near $z_0$).

pole of order $k$ $c_{-k}\ne 0$ but $c_n=0$ for $n<-k$ — $f$ blows up like $1/(z-z_0)^k$, and $\lim_{z\to z_0}|f(z)| = \infty$ in every direction.

essential infinitely many $c_n\ne 0$ for $n<0$. Casorati–Weierstrass: $f$ comes arbitrarily close to every complex value in any neighbourhood of $z_0$. Picard sharpens this dramatically: $f$ hits every value (with at most one exception), infinitely often.

Worked examples around $z_0=0$. $\dfrac{\sin z}{z}$ has Laurent series $1 - \frac{z^2}{6} + \frac{z^4}{120} - \cdots$ — no negative powers, so the singularity is removable; defining $f(0)=1$ gives an entire function. $\dfrac{1}{z^3 - z^2}$ at $z_0=0$ behaves like $-\frac{1}{z^2}$, a pole of order $2$. And $e^{1/z}$ has the Laurent series $\sum_{n\ge 0} \frac{1}{n!\,z^n}$ from §17 — infinitely many negative powers, an essential singularity. Picard guarantees $e^{1/z}$ hits every nonzero complex value infinitely often as $z\to 0$ (the missing exception is $0$ itself).

Pole-vs-zero duality. $f$ has a pole of order $k$ at $z_0$ iff $1/f$ has a zero of order $k$ at $z_0$. This symmetry is what lets §20 count poles and zeros on equal footing in the argument principle.

Singularities and their orders are exactly the data the residue theorem (§19) needs: the residue at a pole of order $k$ is $c_{-1}$, computable from the Taylor expansion of $(z-z_0)^k f(z)$ without ever finding the full Laurent series.

19. Residue theorem

The residue of $f$ at $z_0$ is

$$\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0) \;=\; c_{-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint f\,dz.$$

For a simple pole $\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0) = \lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)f(z)$. For a pole of order $k$, $\operatorname{Res}(f,z_0) = \dfrac{1}{(k-1)!}\lim_{z\to z_0}\dfrac{d^{k-1}}{dz^{k-1}}\bigl((z-z_0)^k f(z)\bigr)$.

Residue theorem. Let $\gamma$ be a positively oriented simple closed contour bounding a region $D$, with $f$ holomorphic on a neighbourhood of $\overline D$ except for finitely many isolated singularities $z_k\in D$. Then $$\oint_\gamma f\,dz \;=\; 2\pi i\sum_k \operatorname{Res}(f, z_k).$$

Real definite integrals fall to this, almost mechanically. Famous example: closing $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ in the upper half-plane with a giant semicircle. The semicircular piece tends to $0$ (the integrand decays), the only pole inside is $z=i$ with residue $1/(2i)$, and so the integral is $2\pi i\cdot 1/(2i) = \pi$. Replace the integrand by $e^{ix}/(x^2+1)$ and the answer is $\pi/e$.

Try it: the contour widget in §contour-integral above already computes $\oint f\,dz$ as $2\pi i \sum\mathrm{Res}$ for the enclosed poles. Drag a vertex over a pole to see the sum jump.

20. Argument principle

Apply the residue theorem to the logarithmic derivative $f'/f$. If $f$ is meromorphic with zeros $a_j$ of order $m_j$ and poles $b_k$ of order $n_k$, then $f'/f$ has simple poles at each $a_j$ with residue $m_j$, and at each $b_k$ with residue $-n_k$. Hence

$$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma \frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz \;=\; Z - P,$$

where $Z = \sum m_j$ counts zeros enclosed by $\gamma$ (with multiplicity) and $P = \sum n_k$ counts poles. This is the argument principle.

The geometric meaning: the integrand is $d(\log f) = d(\log|f|) + i\,d(\arg f)$. The real part integrates to $0$ around a closed loop (it is exact). The imaginary part integrates to $2\pi$ times the winding number of the image curve $f\circ\gamma$ around $0$. So $Z - P$ is exactly how many times the image of $\gamma$ winds around the origin.

21. Rouché's theorem

The argument principle counts zeros by winding number. Rouché's theorem turns that counting into a perturbation tool: if $g$ is a small enough wiggle of $f$ on the boundary, the count cannot change.

Rouché's theorem. If $|f(z) - g(z)| < |f(z)|$ on a contour $\gamma$, then $f$ and $g$ have the same number of zeros inside. Picture: the image $g(\gamma)$ is "less than $|f(\gamma)|$ away" from $f(\gamma)$, so it cannot loop around $0$ a different number of times.

Worked example: FTA in one line. Let $p(z)=z^n + a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$. Take $f(z)=z^n$ and $g(z)=p(z)$ on the circle $|z|=R$. For $R$ large, $|p(z)-z^n| = |a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots| \le C R^{n-1} < R^n = |z^n|$, so $p$ has the same number of zeros as $z^n$ inside, namely $n$.

Worked example: zeros of $z^4 - 6z + 3$. On $|z|=2$, take $f(z)=z^4$: then $|{-6z+3}|\le 15 < 16 = |z^4|$, so all four zeros lie inside $|z|<2$. On $|z|=1$, take $f(z)=-6z+3$: then $|z^4|=1 < |{-6z+3}|\ge 3$, so exactly one zero lies inside $|z|<1$. Three zeros sit in the annulus $1<|z|<2$ — without computing a single root.

The strategy is always the same: pick a known $f$ whose zero count you already understand, choose a contour where one term dominates, then read off the count for the perturbation $g$. Rouché is the standard tool for ad-hoc location of zeros of polynomials and transcendental functions, and a recurring step in stability arguments — e.g. proving that a small holomorphic deformation of a function preserves the number of fixed points in a region. It is a strict refinement of the argument principle (§20) tailored for the comparison setting.

22. Conformal maps

A holomorphic map $f$ is conformal wherever $f'\ne 0$. Globally, a conformal isomorphism is a holomorphic bijection with holomorphic inverse.

Joukowski and Schwarz–Christoffel

The Joukowski map $J(z) = \tfrac12(z + 1/z)$ has the celebrated property that it sends a circle through $z=1$ (or $z=-1$) to an "airfoil" shape — historically used in early 20th-century aerodynamics to compute flow around wings.

Schwarz–Christoffel maps express the Riemann map from the upper half-plane to a polygon explicitly:

$$f(z) = \int_0^z \prod_{k=1}^n (\zeta - x_k)^{\alpha_k - 1}\,d\zeta,$$

where $x_k\in\mathbb{R}$ are the pre-images of the polygon vertices and $\pi\alpha_k$ are the interior angles at those vertices.

23. Disk automorphisms

The conformal automorphisms of the disk are exactly

$$\mathrm{Aut}(\mathbb{D}) = \left\{ z\mapsto e^{i\theta}\frac{z-a}{1-\bar a z} \;:\; \theta\in\mathbb{R},\, a\in\mathbb{D}\right\} \;\cong\; \mathrm{PSU}(1,1).$$

This is the isometry group of the hyperbolic plane, with the Poincaré metric $ds^2 = \dfrac{4|dz|^2}{(1-|z|^2)^2}$.

The Cayley transform $z\mapsto i\dfrac{1-z}{1+z}$ identifies the disk with the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H} = \{z : \mathrm{Im}\,z > 0\}$, whose automorphism group is $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ acting by Möbius transformations with real coefficients.

24. Normal families (Montel)

A family $\mathcal{F}$ of holomorphic functions on a domain $\Omega$ is normal if every sequence in $\mathcal{F}$ has a subsequence that converges locally uniformly on $\Omega$ — possibly to $\infty$. This is compactness in disguise: the right setting is the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets.

Montel's theorem. If $\mathcal{F}$ is locally bounded (uniformly bounded on each compact $K\subset\Omega$), then $\mathcal{F}$ is normal.

The proof is Arzelà–Ascoli applied to the right object. Local boundedness combined with Cauchy's estimate $|f'(z)|\le M_K/\mathrm{dist}(z,\partial K)$ gives equicontinuity for free; Arzelà–Ascoli then extracts a uniformly convergent subsequence, and the locally uniform limit of holomorphic functions is holomorphic (Weierstrass). So local boundedness is the only hypothesis you have to check.

Why this matters. Normality is the engine behind two of the deepest results in complex analysis. The Riemann mapping theorem (next section) extracts the desired conformal map as a limit of a maximizing sequence in a locally bounded family. Picard's theorem and the Schottky bound use normality of families omitting two values to control the wild oscillation of essential singularities. In dynamics, the Fatou set is by definition the largest open set on which the iterates of a rational map form a normal family — the Julia set is its complement.

Compare with the real-analytic situation: a uniformly bounded family of smooth functions need not be precompact, and there is no analogue of Cauchy's estimate to bound derivatives from values. Holomorphy is what makes the Heine–Borel-style miracle work.

25. Riemann mapping theorem

Riemann mapping theorem. Every simply connected proper open subset of $\mathbb{C}$ is conformally equivalent to the open unit disk $\mathbb{D}$. (And the equivalence is unique once you fix a base point and a tangent direction.)

Topology says the disk and any other simply connected domain are homeomorphic; the Riemann mapping theorem says they are holomorphically equivalent. The proof is non-constructive — the equivalence is the unique normalized solution to an extremal problem (maximize $|f'(z_0)|$ among holomorphic injections into $\mathbb{D}$).

26. Harmonic functions

If $f = u + iv$ is holomorphic, both $u$ and $v$ are harmonic: they satisfy Laplace's equation $\Delta u = u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0$. Conversely, any harmonic function on a simply connected domain is the real part of some holomorphic $f$, unique up to a real additive constant. The imaginary part $v$ is the harmonic conjugate of $u$; you reconstruct it by integrating the Cauchy–Riemann equations.

This identification is the bridge to potential theory: the steady-state heat distribution, the gravitational potential in vacuum, and the electrostatic potential in a charge-free region are all harmonic, hence locally real parts of holomorphic functions. Conformal maps then move boundary value problems between domains.

One step further: subharmonic functions. Subharmonic functions (continuous, satisfying $u(z_0) \le \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} u(z_0 + re^{i\theta})d\theta$) generalize $\log|f|$ for holomorphic $f$ and provide the right setting for value-distribution and potential theory. They underwrite the Perron method for the harmonic-functions topic's Dirichlet problem and the proof of the Bloch lower bound (advanced complex analysis §9) via comparison with metrics of curvature $\le -1$ — the Ahlfors–Schwarz lemma at work.

27. Analytic continuation

Analytic continuation. A holomorphic function on a small disk has a unique extension to any simply connected domain containing the disk on which the extension exists. (Identity theorem: two holomorphic functions agreeing on a set with an accumulation point agree everywhere on the connected component.) The natural domain of $\sqrt{z}$ or $\log z$ is not $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ but a bigger object — a Riemann surface. Walking $\log z$ around a loop encircling $0$ adds $2\pi i$; that increment is the monodromy.

Riemann surfaces are the natural homes of multi-valued analytic functions. The compact ones are classified by genus; the simply connected ones by uniformization (every compact Riemann surface of genus $\ge 2$ is a quotient of the upper half-plane by a Fuchsian group). Modular forms — automorphic functions on the upper half-plane — pick up here. Forward pointer: a future modular-forms.html will continue this thread.
