Mathematical chaos

Sensitive dependence, period doubling, strange attractors, KAM, and the deep order hiding inside seemingly random dynamics.

1. Sensitive dependence and Lyapunov exponents

"Chaos" is one of mathematics' most misused words. A working definition: a deterministic dynamical system is chaotic on an invariant set when it has (i) sensitive dependence on initial conditions, (ii) topological transitivity, and (iii) dense periodic orbits — Devaney's triple. Of these, sensitive dependence is the one a computer can quantify. Given a smooth map $f\colon\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$, the rate at which nearby orbits separate along the orbit of $x_0$ is the Lyapunov exponent

$\lambda(x_0; v) = \lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\,\log\|Df^n(x_0)\cdot v\|,\qquad v\in T_{x_0}\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\{0\}.$

By the chain rule $Df^n(x_0)=Df(x_{n-1})\cdots Df(x_0)$, so for a 1D map $\lambda = \lim\tfrac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log|f'(x_k)|$ — the time average of $\log|f'|$ along the orbit. Oseledec's multiplicative ergodic theorem (1968) is the precise sense in which this works: for any ergodic invariant probability $\mu$, the limit $\lambda(x;v)$ exists $\mu$-almost everywhere and takes at most $n$ distinct values $\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\cdots$ — the Lyapunov spectrum. Positive $\lambda_1$ is the operational signature of chaos.

For the logistic map $f_r(x)=rx(1-x)$, the exponent $\lambda(r)$ as a function of the parameter is striking. On the periodic windows (period-2 from $r=3$, period-4 from $r\approx 3.449$, period-3 from $r\approx 3.828$) $\lambda$ dips below zero. At the Feigenbaum point $r_\infty\approx 3.5699$ it crosses through zero into chaos. At $r=4$ exactly, the conjugation $h(x)=\sin^2(\pi x/2)$ takes $f_4$ to the tent map $T(y)=1-|2y-1|$, which has $|T'|\equiv 2$ — so $\lambda(4)=\log 2$. 2. The logistic map and Feigenbaum universality

The logistic family $f_r(x)=rx(1-x)$ on $[0,1]$ is the world's most-studied 1D dynamical system, and for good reason: as $r$ rises through $[1,4]$ it traverses every qualitative behaviour a smooth unimodal map can exhibit. At $r=1$ everything dies to $0$; at $r=3$ the fixed point $x^*=1-1/r$ loses stability via a period-doubling and a stable 2-cycle is born; past $r_2\approx 3.4495$ the 2-cycle bifurcates to a 4-cycle, then to 8, 16, 32, $\ldots$ The bifurcation values $r_n$ accumulate at $r_\infty\approx 3.56995$.

Drag the slider below to walk through the cascade. Watch the orbit: at $r=2.9$ a clean fixed point, at $r=3.2$ a 2-cycle, at $r=3.5$ a 4-cycle, at $r=3.83$ a stable 3-cycle (the famous "period-3 window" hidden inside the chaotic regime).

Mitchell Feigenbaum's 1978 discovery is what makes this section more than a curiosity. The successive gaps $\Delta_n = r_n - r_{n-1}$ shrink geometrically:

$\delta = \lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{\Delta_{n-1}}{\Delta_n} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{r_{n-1}-r_{n-2}}{r_n-r_{n-1}} \approx 4.66920\,16091\,02990\ldots$

And the spatial scaling: if $d_n$ is the distance from the critical point $x_c=1/2$ to the nearest point of the period-$2^n$ orbit, then $|d_{n-1}/d_n|\to \alpha\approx 2.50290\,78750\,95892\ldots$ These two ratios are universal. The same $\delta$ and $\alpha$ govern the period-doubling cascade of every smooth one-parameter family $g_r$ of unimodal maps with quadratic critical point. They depend only on the order of the critical point (replacing the quadratic max with a quartic gives a different pair of constants).

The mechanism is a renormalization fixed point: the operator $R$ that takes a unimodal $g$ to a rescaled iterate $R(g)(x)=-\alpha\,g\!\bigl(g(-x/\alpha)\bigr)$ has a hyperbolic fixed point $g^*$ in the space of unimodal maps, with a single unstable direction whose eigenvalue is $\delta$. The bifurcation cascade is the image of $g^*$'s unstable manifold under any 1-parameter slice transverse to it. Mitchell Feigenbaum did this numerically before the rigorous proofs (Lanford 1982 for the quadratic case, computer-assisted) arrived.

3. Strange attractors and the horseshoe

The logistic map is 1D, dissipative, and noninvertible. To reach the truly geometric picture of chaos — invariant sets with fractional dimension, woven from infinitely many sheets — we need at least three continuous variables. Edward Lorenz's 1963 paper "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow" stripped the equations of atmospheric convection down to a 3-variable ODE,

$\dot x = \sigma(y - x),\qquad \dot y = x(\rho - z) - y,\qquad \dot z = xy - \beta z,$

and discovered the eponymous butterfly. At $(\sigma,\beta,\rho)=(10,8/3,28)$ the system has three fixed points — the origin, and two symmetric saddle-foci $C_\pm$ at $(\pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)},\pm\sqrt{\beta(\rho-1)},\rho-1)$ — none stable, and yet trajectories never escape to infinity. They wander forever on a thin invariant set with Hausdorff dimension $\approx 2.062$, the Lorenz attractor. Tucker proved (computer-assisted, 1998) that the geometric Lorenz attractor is genuinely a robust strange attractor.

Below the subcritical Hopf $\rho_H = \sigma(\sigma+\beta+3)/(\sigma-\beta-1)\approx 24.74$ the symmetric pair $C_\pm$ is stable and trajectories spiral into one wing or the other. Above $\rho_H$ they are unstable saddle-foci; trajectories oscillate around each, then unpredictably switch wings. Two trajectories with initial separation $10^{-9}$ diverge to macroscopic distance after $\approx 25$ time units — the maximal Lyapunov exponent is $\approx 0.906$.

The Lorenz attractor's geometry is a Cantor-set foliation: locally a 2D surface, transverse to which it looks like a Cantor set in dimension $\approx 0.06$. Stephen Smale's horseshoe (1965) is the topological essence of why this works. Take a square, stretch it vertically by a factor $>2$, compress it horizontally by a factor $<1/2$, then fold the top back over the bottom and place the resulting "horseshoe" back over the original square. The points that stay inside the square under all forward and backward iterations form an invariant Cantor set $\Lambda\subset Q$; coding each point by which leg ($0$ or $1$) it occupies at each iterate gives a homeomorphism $\Lambda\cong\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}$ that conjugates $f|_\Lambda$ to the shift map $\sigma(s)_n = s_{n+1}$. The shift has periodic orbits of every period (one for each periodic binary sequence) and dense orbits — so $f$ does too.

4. Symbolic dynamics and topological entropy

The horseshoe's punchline — every orbit corresponds to a bi-infinite binary sequence — is the prototype of symbolic dynamics. Given an invariant partition $\{A_0,\ldots,A_{k-1}\}$ of phase space (a Markov partition), the itinerary $\sigma\colon X\to\{0,\ldots,k-1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}$ defined by $\sigma(x)_n = i \iff f^n(x)\in A_i$ conjugates $f$ to a subshift of finite type $\Sigma_A$ — a closed shift-invariant subset of $k^{\mathbb{Z}}$ specified by a $k\times k$ transition matrix $A\in\{0,1\}^{k\times k}$ (with $A_{ij}=1$ iff $f(A_i)\supset A_j$).

The combinatorial complexity of a chaotic system is measured by topological entropy:

$h_{\mathrm{top}}(f) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\#\mathrm{Fix}(f^n) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{n}\log\#\{n\text{-words in }\Sigma_A\} \;=\; \log\rho(A),$

where $\rho(A)$ is the spectral radius (largest eigenvalue, by Perron–Frobenius). The horseshoe has $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, so $\rho(A)=2$ and $h_{\mathrm{top}}=\log 2$. A period-3-forcing map ("period 3 implies all periods", Sharkovsky's punchline) has $h_{\mathrm{top}}\ge\log\bigl(\tfrac{1+\sqrt 5}{2}\bigr) > 0$.

Two structural theorems tie it all together. The variational principle: $h_{\mathrm{top}}(f) = \sup_\mu h_\mu(f)$ over all $f$-invariant probability measures $\mu$, where $h_\mu$ is the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy. Ruelle's inequality: $h_\mu(f)\le \sum_i \max(\lambda_i(\mu),0)$ — entropy is bounded by the sum of positive Lyapunov exponents. Pesin's formula: equality holds iff $\mu$ is an SRB measure (next section).

5. Ergodic theory and SRB measures

Chaos defeats predictability of individual orbits, but the statistics of orbits can still be perfectly predictable. Birkhoff's ergodic theorem (1931): if $T\colon X\to X$ preserves a probability measure $\mu$ and is ergodic (every $T$-invariant set has $\mu$-measure $0$ or $1$), then for every $f\in L^1(\mu)$,

$\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}f(T^k x) \;=\; \int_X f\,d\mu \qquad\mu\text{-almost every } x.$

Time averages equal space averages. The doubling map preserves Lebesgue measure on $[0,1)$ and is ergodic; running a long orbit and binning the visits recovers the uniform measure. The logistic map at $r=4$ preserves the measure $d\mu = \tfrac{1}{\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}}dx$ — orbits cluster near $0$ and $1$, not uniformly.

For a dissipative system the attractor is a measure-zero (Lebesgue) subset of phase space, so demanding "Lebesgue-a.e. initial conditions converge to $\int f\,d\mu$" doesn't fit — the attractor's own measure is mutually singular with Lebesgue. The right notion is the Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen measure (Sinai 1972, Ruelle 1976, Bowen 1975): the unique $f$-invariant measure $\mu_{\mathrm{SRB}}$ supported on the attractor whose conditional measures along unstable manifolds are absolutely continuous with respect to the unstable Lebesgue. This is the maximum of Pesin's formula and the physical-measure property combined.

The operational miracle: for Lebesgue-a.e. initial condition $x$ in the basin of attraction, $\tfrac{1}{N}\sum f(T^k x)\to \int f\,d\mu_{\mathrm{SRB}}$. An experimenter who picks an arbitrary microstate gets reproducible average behaviour. SRB measures are why "macroscopic" predictions from chaotic models — long-term climate statistics, the equilibrium distribution of a billiard ball on a Bunimovich stadium — are possible at all.

6. KAM theorem and Hamiltonian chaos

Hamiltonian dynamics offers a third register. A completely integrable Hamiltonian on $T^*Q$ has $n$ Poisson-commuting first integrals; Liouville–Arnold says regular compact level sets are tori $T^n$ on which the flow is conditionally periodic with frequency vector $\omega(I)=\partial H/\partial I$. Every orbit either closes (resonant $\omega$, rational dependence) or winds densely (non-resonant $\omega$). No chaos. Most real Hamiltonians are not integrable: $H_\varepsilon = H_0(I) + \varepsilon H_1(I,\theta)$ with $\varepsilon>0$ — a slight perturbation. What survives?

Kolmogorov (1954), Arnold (1963), Moser (1962) — the KAM theorem — proves that most invariant tori survive. Specifically, if $H_0$ is non-degenerate ($\det\partial^2 H_0/\partial I^2 \ne 0$) and the perturbation $H_1$ is sufficiently small and smooth, the tori with Diophantine frequency vectors

$|k\cdot\omega| \ge \gamma\,|k|^{-\tau}\qquad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}^n\setminus\{0\}$

persist as smoothly deformed Lagrangian tori in the perturbed system. The set of Diophantine $\omega$ has positive Lebesgue measure that tends to full measure as $\varepsilon\to 0$. The proof handles the "small-denominator problem" — the resonances $k\cdot\omega\approx 0$ that would naively destroy convergence of perturbation series — by an accelerated Newton-style iteration that uses superexponential convergence to defeat the polynomial small-denominator divergence.

In the gaps between Diophantine tori, KAM is silent — and Hamiltonian chaos lives there. Resonant tori shatter into pairs of elliptic and hyperbolic periodic orbits (Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem), and the unstable manifold of each hyperbolic orbit weaves into the stable manifold of others, creating Poincaré's homoclinic tangle: trajectories that look locally like a Smale horseshoe. 7. Sharkovsky's theorem and 1D order

In 1964 a young Ukrainian mathematician, Oleksandr Sharkovsky, proved one of the most surprising theorems in dynamics. For continuous self-maps of an interval, the periods that occur are totally ordered:

$3 \,\triangleright\, 5 \,\triangleright\, 7 \,\triangleright\, \cdots \,\triangleright\, 2\cdot 3 \,\triangleright\, 2\cdot 5 \,\triangleright\, \cdots \,\triangleright\, 4\cdot 3 \,\triangleright\, 4\cdot 5 \,\triangleright\, \cdots \,\triangleright\, 2^n \,\triangleright\, \cdots \,\triangleright\, 16 \,\triangleright\, 8 \,\triangleright\, 4 \,\triangleright\, 2 \,\triangleright\, 1$

Sharkovsky's theorem: If $f\colon I\to I$ is continuous and has a periodic orbit of period $m$, then $f$ has periodic orbits of every period $n$ with $m \triangleright n$. The order's first element is $3$, so a period-3 orbit forces orbits of every period — the famous "period three implies chaos" (Li and Yorke 1975; though Sharkovsky's stronger result preceded it by a decade). The order's last element is $1$ (fixed points); a continuous map of an interval into itself always has those, by Brouwer.

Connections

Chaos is no longer a frontier so much as a hub. The cutting edges are in partial hyperbolicity (relaxing Anosov's uniform expansion to a dominated splitting), chaotic billiards (Sinai's dispersing billiards, ergodicity of hard-sphere gases), quantum chaos (statistics of Laplacian eigenvalues on negatively curved manifolds matching GOE/GUE — random matrix universality), and the still-stubborn question of turbulence in the Navier–Stokes equations.

  • Dynamical systems — chaos and Lyapunov exponents — the direct parent: this page picks up where dynamical-systems ends, with sharper tools (entropy, SRB, KAM).
  • The three-body problem — Poincaré's tangle — Poincaré's 1889 discovery of homoclinic tangles in the restricted 3-body problem is the historical birthplace of mathematical chaos.
  • Hamiltonians and classical mechanics — KAM — the integrable case KAM perturbs.
  • Fixed-point theorems — Brouwer — Sharkovsky's theorem rests on intermediate-value-style fixed-point arguments applied to a directed graph of intervals.
  • Random matrix theory — universality — quantum chaos conjectures that eigenvalues of the Laplacian on chaotic systems follow GOE / GUE statistics, an empirically robust but still partly open universality.
  • Statistical mechanics — microstates and ensembles — ergodicity is the bridge: it is what makes the microcanonical ensemble's "uniform on the energy shell" assumption operationally true.
