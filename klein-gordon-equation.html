Klein-Gordon equation

The simplest manifestly Lorentz-invariant wave equation. Quantizing $E^2 = p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4$ produces a relativistic scalar field — and with it antiparticles, a probability problem, and the bridge to the Schrödinger limit.

1. From $E^2=p^2+m^2$ to a wave operator

Special relativity hands quantum mechanics a non-negotiable starting point: the on-shell relation $E^2 = |\vec p|^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4$ ("on-shell" = physical states satisfying this constraint, i.e. living on the relativistic mass-shell hyperboloid in 4-momentum space). The Schrödinger equation is built around the non-relativistic dispersion $E = |\vec p|^2/(2m)$, so it cannot be the right equation for a particle whose energy and momentum are comparable to $mc^2$. To get a relativistic wave equation, we apply the standard quantization rules

$$E \;\mapsto\; i\hbar\,\partial_t,\qquad \vec p \;\mapsto\; -i\hbar\,\vec\nabla$$

directly to the relativistic relation $E^2 - |\vec p|^2 c^2 = m^2 c^4$. Both sides become operators, both sides act on a scalar field $\phi(t,\vec x)$, and the result is

$$-\hbar^2\,\partial_t^2\phi \;=\; -\hbar^2 c^2\,\nabla^2\phi + m^2 c^4\,\phi.$$

Setting $\hbar = c = 1$ (natural units, used everywhere below) and writing the d'Alembertian $\Box = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$ (mostly-plus convention $\partial^\mu\partial_\mu$ on a scalar), this collapses to the Klein-Gordon equation

$$\boxed{\;(\Box + m^2)\,\phi \;=\; 0.\;}$$

It is second-order in time — a deliberate choice to keep both sides of $E^2=p^2+m^2$ symmetric. Schrödinger's $i\partial_t\psi = H\psi$ is first-order in time but only because $H = p^2/(2m)$ is a polynomial in $\vec p$ alone; the relativistic relation has $E^2$ on the left, so the time derivative comes squared. Dirac later extracted a first-order relativistic equation by formally taking the "square root" of the KG operator — at the cost of replacing $\phi$ with a 4-component spinor.

2. The Klein-Gordon equation

The fully covariant statement, in mostly-plus signature $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, is

$$\boxed{\;(\Box + m^2)\,\phi(x) \;=\; 0,\qquad \Box \;\equiv\; \eta^{\mu\nu}\,\partial_\mu\partial_\nu \;=\; -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2.\;}$$

(Some authors absorb the sign and write $\Box = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$; the equation is unchanged either way, since only $\Box + m^2 = 0$ matters.) This is the simplest second-order linear PDE that is (i) Lorentz-invariant — $\Box$ is a Lorentz scalar, $m^2$ is a constant, and $\phi$ is a scalar field; (ii) translationally invariant; (iii) free, in the sense that nothing nonlinear in $\phi$ appears. It is the relativistic analogue of the wave equation $\Box\phi = 0$ with a single dimensionful parameter $m$ (the Compton wavelength $\lambda_C = \hbar/(mc) = 1/m$ in natural units).

Its physical role in the Standard Model:

  • Higgs sector. The Higgs field is a scalar, and after symmetry breaking the physical Higgs boson satisfies a Klein-Gordon equation (with self-interactions and gauge couplings layered on, but the kinetic operator is $\Box + m_H^2$).
  • Pions and other spin-0 mesons. $\pi^\pm,\pi^0,K^\pm,\ldots$ — composite quark-antiquark states, but as effective fields they obey KG dynamics.
  • Gauge-fixed photon and gluon propagators. Each component of $A^\mu$ in Lorenz gauge satisfies $\Box A^\mu = 0$ (the massless KG equation).

3. Plane-wave solutions and the dispersion relation

Substitute the plane-wave ansatz $\phi(t,\vec x) = e^{-i(Et - \vec p\cdot\vec x)}$ (in natural units, $\hbar=c=1$). Both derivatives in $\Box + m^2$ act diagonally:

$$(-E^2 + |\vec p|^2 + m^2)\,e^{-i(Et - \vec p\cdot\vec x)} \;=\; 0,$$

so $\phi$ is a solution iff

$$\boxed{\;E^2 \;=\; |\vec p|^2 + m^2.\;}$$

This is the relativistic energy-momentum relation, recovered as the dispersion relation of the field. There are two branches:

$$E_{\pm}(\vec p) \;=\; \pm\sqrt{|\vec p|^2 + m^2}.$$

The positive branch is the expected one: its $\vec p\to 0$ limit is $E = m$ (rest energy), and for small $|\vec p|$ it expands as $E \approx m + |\vec p|^2/(2m)$ — the rest energy plus Newtonian kinetic energy. The negative branch is not spurious and cannot be discarded: any Lorentz-invariant field satisfying a second-order equation has both. We come back to it in §4.

Two velocities live on the dispersion curve. The phase velocity $v_\phi = E/|\vec p|$ exceeds $1$ for any massive mode ($v_\phi > c$); the group velocity at which a wave packet's centroid moves is

$$v_g \;=\; \frac{dE}{d|\vec p|} \;=\; \frac{|\vec p|}{\sqrt{|\vec p|^2+m^2}} \;=\; \frac{|\vec p|}{E} \;<\; 1.$$

4. Negative-energy solutions and antiparticles

The negative branch $E_{-}(\vec p) = -\sqrt{|\vec p|^2 + m^2}$ initially looks pathological. A particle with arbitrarily negative energy could keep emitting photons and tumble down forever; the spectrum has no minimum. In single-particle quantum mechanics this is fatal — yet the equation insists those modes are present, because a second-order PDE in time has a two-dimensional space of initial-data choices $(\phi(0,\vec x),\,\partial_t\phi(0,\vec x))$.

The Feynman-Stückelberg reinterpretation repairs the spectrum without amputating the equation. Pick a negative-energy plane wave

$$\phi_{-}(x) \;=\; e^{-i(-E)t + i\vec p\cdot\vec x} \;=\; e^{+i(Et - \vec p\cdot\vec x)},\qquad E = +\sqrt{|\vec p|^2+m^2} > 0,$$

and read off the right-hand side: it is the complex conjugate of a positive-energy plane wave with reversed 3-momentum. Stückelberg (1941) and Feynman (1948) reinterpret this mode as a positive-energy antiparticle propagating with reversed 4-momentum — same mass, opposite charge, opposite direction in time relative to the original. With this reading the spectrum is bounded below by $E = +m$ for every species (particle or antiparticle), and the field $\phi$ creates particles while $\phi^*$ creates antiparticles. For a real-valued KG field, particle = antiparticle (the $\pi^0$ is its own antiparticle); for a complex-valued field, the $U(1)$ phase symmetry distinguishes them (the $\pi^\pm$ pair).

This is one of the first cases in physics where antimatter falls out of mathematics rather than experiment: Klein and Gordon wrote down the equation in 1926, the negative branch was problematic for years, and Feynman-Stückelberg's reinterpretation predated by decades the routine experimental confirmation of antimatter as a generic feature of relativistic quantum systems. (Dirac's 1928 hole-theory argument got there first for spin-$\tfrac12$, but the KG reinterpretation generalizes cleanly to all spins.)

5. Conserved current and the probability problem

For a complex KG field $\phi$, multiply the equation by $\phi^*$ and subtract its conjugate to obtain

$$\partial_\mu\,j^\mu \;=\; 0,\qquad\text{where}\qquad j^\mu \;=\; i\bigl(\phi^*\,\partial^\mu\phi - \phi\,\partial^\mu\phi^*\bigr).$$

So $j^\mu$ is a conserved 4-current — exactly what one would hope to use as a probability current, by analogy with $j^\mu_\text{Schr.} = (|\psi|^2,\,\tfrac{1}{2mi}(\psi^*\nabla\psi - \psi\nabla\psi^*))$ in the non-relativistic theory. The catch is the time component:

$$j^0 \;=\; i\bigl(\phi^*\,\partial_t\phi - \phi\,\partial_t\phi^*\bigr).$$

Unlike $|\psi|^2$ in the Schrödinger theory, this is not positive-definite. Take a superposition

$$\phi(t,x) \;=\; A\,e^{-i(Et-px)} + B\,e^{+i(Et-px)}\quad\text{(positive- + negative-energy)};$$

then $j^0$ contains an interference term $2E\bigl(|A|^2 - |B|^2\bigr) + \text{cross terms}$ that flips sign when $|B| > |A|$. A probability density cannot be negative, so $j^0$ disqualifies itself as a single-particle probability density.

This was a real obstacle in the 1930s and forced the move from "single-particle wavefunction" to quantum field theory. The resolution: promote $\phi$ to an operator-valued field on a Fock space, define creation/annihilation operators $a_{\vec p}, b_{\vec p}^\dagger$ for particles and antiparticles, and reinterpret $j^\mu$ not as a probability current but as a conserved charge current — particle number minus antiparticle number, weighted by the $U(1)$ charge. With this reading $j^0$ is allowed to be negative, and indeed must be: a region populated by antiparticles carries the opposite charge density of one populated by particles. The Schrödinger probability interpretation survives only in the non-relativistic limit where the negative branch decouples (§6).

6. Non-relativistic limit and applications

How does the Klein-Gordon equation reduce to Schrödinger's at low momenta? Most of the rest energy $mc^2$ is just a constant offset, so factor it out: write

$$\phi(t,\vec x) \;=\; e^{-i m c^2 t/\hbar}\,\psi(t,\vec x).$$

Plug in. The first time derivative gives $\partial_t\phi = (-i m c^2/\hbar)e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}\psi + e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}\partial_t\psi$, and the second derivative produces three terms. After dividing through by $e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}$ and simplifying, the KG equation $(\Box + m^2c^2/\hbar^2)\phi = 0$ becomes (restoring $\hbar,c$ for clarity)

$$2imc^2\,\partial_t\psi \;=\; -\hbar^2 c^2\,\nabla^2\psi - \hbar^2\,\partial_t^2\psi.$$

Now invoke the non-relativistic regime: the kinetic energy $|i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi|$ is tiny compared with the rest energy $mc^2|\psi|$, so the second-time-derivative term $\hbar^2 \partial_t^2\psi \sim |i\hbar\partial_t\psi|^2/(mc^2|\psi|) \cdot mc^2$ is suppressed by exactly that small ratio and can be dropped at leading order. Dividing by $2mc^2$ gives

$$\boxed{\;i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi \;=\; -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\nabla^2\psi.\;}$$

This is the free Schrödinger equation. The negative-energy branch decouples in this limit (its $e^{+imc^2t/\hbar}$ phase counter-rotates against the prefactor and oscillates so fast that any non-relativistic probe averages over it). With this, we recover all of non-relativistic quantum mechanics — including the positive probability density $|\psi|^2$, since the negative-energy contamination of $j^0$ is gone.

Where is the KG equation actually used?

  • Pionic atoms. Atoms in which an orbital electron is replaced by a $\pi^-$ (or $K^-$). Because the pion is spin-0, its wavefunction satisfies KG (modified by the Coulomb potential), and the spectrum differs from hydrogen in measurable fine-structure-like ways.
  • Higgs and effective scalar fields. The Higgs propagator is the KG propagator with $m^2 \mapsto m_H^2$; perturbative scattering amplitudes are built from it via Feynman rules.
  • Path-integral building block. $\int e^{iS_\text{KG}/\hbar}\mathcal{D}\phi$ is the simplest bosonic functional integral, and it underlies the construction of more complicated theories (gauge bosons, gravitons via similar quadratic actions).
  • Cosmological perturbations. Inflaton and dark-matter scalar field perturbations on FRW backgrounds satisfy a KG equation with a Hubble-friction term — same operator, curved spacetime.

Connections

The Klein-Gordon equation is the entry point to relativistic field theory: every step beyond it — adding spin, gauge symmetry, interactions, or curvature — keeps the second-order Lorentz-invariant kinetic operator and adds structure on top. The open frontier is not the equation itself but the renormalization, non-perturbative dynamics, and curved-spacetime regimes (Hawking radiation, inflaton perturbations) that genuine interacting scalar fields exhibit; the equation is settled, the theories built on top of it are not.

  • Special relativity — relativistic energy-momentum — the on-shell relation $E^2 = p^2 + m^2$ is what we quantize to get $\Box + m^2$.
  • Schrödinger equation — the non-relativistic limit, recovered by factoring out $e^{-imc^2t/\hbar}$ and dropping $\hbar^2\partial_t^2\psi$.
  • Partial differential equations — wave equation — KG is the wave equation with a constant zero-th-order term; characteristics, Cauchy data, Green's functions all carry over.
  • Gauge theory — promote $\partial_\mu \to D_\mu = \partial_\mu + ieA_\mu$ and KG becomes the equation of a charged scalar minimally coupled to electromagnetism (the Higgs sector, scalar QED).
  • Riemann surfaces — the two branches $E = \pm\sqrt{p^2+m^2}$ are the two sheets of a Riemann surface; the analytic continuation across the branch cut is what threads the Feynman propagator's $+i\epsilon$ prescription.
