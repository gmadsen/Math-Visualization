Optimal control and dynamic programming

Two ways to ask the same question. Pontryagin: among all control trajectories, which one minimises cost? — answered by adjoint co-states and a pointwise Hamiltonian maximisation. Bellman: how good is the best policy from here? — answered by a value function that satisfies a first-order PDE, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation. The two views are dual: HJB characteristics are Pontryagin extremals. Together they give the optimal-feedback law that closes the loop on every modern controller from autopilots to portfolio rebalancing.

1. The optimal-control problem

A controlled dynamical system is an ODE whose right-hand side is steerable:

$$\dot x(t) \;=\; f(x(t),\,u(t),\,t), \qquad x(0) = x_0,\quad t\in[0,T].$$

Here $x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is the state (position, velocity, account balance, temperature profile…) and $u(t)\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^m$ is the control (steering angle, thrust, trading rate, valve setting). The admissible controls are measurable $u\colon[0,T]\to U$; the set $U$ may be a box, a sphere, or a finite menu of discrete settings.

The reward of running policy $u(\cdot)$ from $x_0$ is the cost functional in Bolza form:

$$J\bigl[u(\cdot)\bigr] \;=\; \int_0^T L\bigl(x(t),u(t),t\bigr)\,dt \;+\; \Phi\bigl(x(T)\bigr).$$

$L$ is the running cost (fuel burned per second, control effort squared, regret accumulated); $\Phi$ is the terminal cost (distance to target, payoff at expiry). The optimal-control problem is

$$u^*(\cdot) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{u(\cdot)\,\in\,\mathcal{U}_{\rm ad}} J[u(\cdot)] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \dot x = f(x,u,t),\ x(0)=x_0.$$

Existence, controllability, reachability

Two preliminaries decide whether the optimisation even makes sense.

  • Reachability. The reachable set $\mathcal{R}_T(x_0)$ is the set of states $x(T)$ producible by some admissible control. If the target lies outside $\mathcal{R}_T(x_0)$, the constrained problem is infeasible.
  • Controllability. A system is locally controllable at $x_0$ if $\mathcal{R}_T(x_0)$ contains a neighbourhood of $x_0$ for every $T>0$. For linear time-invariant $\dot x = Ax + Bu$, the Kalman rank condition $\operatorname{rank}[B \mid AB \mid \cdots \mid A^{n-1}B] = n$ is the textbook criterion.
  • Existence of an optimal control. By Filippov's theorem, if $U$ is compact, $f$ and $L$ are continuous with $f$ linear-in-$u$ (or $f(x,U,t)$ convex), and the reachable set is bounded, a minimiser exists. The convexity of $f$-in-$u$ is the optimal-control analog of weak lower semicontinuity in the calculus of variations.

One slogan to keep at hand: without controllability, the question is wrong; without convexity, the answer might not exist.

2. The Euler–Lagrange detour and Bolza form

Strip away the control constraint $u\in U$ and the problem reduces to calculus of variations: minimise $\int_0^T L(x,\dot x,t)\,dt$ over curves $x\colon[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}^n$ with fixed endpoints. The first-order necessary condition is the Euler–Lagrange equation

$$\boxed{\;\frac{d}{dt}\,\partial_{\dot x} L \;=\; \partial_x L.\;}$$

To see how it lives inside optimal control: take $f(x,u,t)=u$ (so $\dot x = u$ — the control is the velocity), no terminal cost, and impose no control constraint. Then the minimisation

$$\min_{u}\int_0^T L(x,u,t)\,dt \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \dot x = u$$

is precisely the calculus-of-variations problem $\min_x \int_0^T L(x,\dot x,t)\,dt$. The Euler–Lagrange equation governs every smooth extremal.

Bolza, Mayer, Lagrange — the same cost in three dresses

Three classical formulations of the cost are equivalent up to a state-augmentation:

namecostcharacter
Lagrange$\displaystyle\int_0^T L(x,u,t)\,dt$integral only
Mayer$\Phi(x(T))$terminal only
Bolza$\displaystyle\int_0^T L\,dt + \Phi(x(T))$both

The trick that converts among them: append the running integral as a new state. Let $x_{n+1}(t)=\int_0^t L(x,u,s)\,ds$ with $\dot x_{n+1}=L$, $x_{n+1}(0)=0$. Then a Lagrange cost becomes a Mayer terminal cost $\Phi(x(T),x_{n+1}(T)) = x_{n+1}(T)$. Conversely a Mayer cost is trivially Bolza with $L\equiv 0$. Bolza is the universal container.

Why control breaks Euler–Lagrange

The variational derivation rests on perturbing $x$ freely. When $u\in U$ is a closed set, you can't perturb $u^*$ in every direction — only into $U$. The Euler–Lagrange "set the derivative to zero" becomes "maximise (or minimise) over the admissible cone." The correct replacement is the Pontryagin maximum principle, which we meet next.

3. Pontryagin's maximum principle

Pontryagin's principle (Soviet school, 1956) is the central necessary condition for an optimal control. It introduces a co-state $\lambda(t)\in\mathbb{R}^n$ — the Lagrange multiplier dual to the dynamics constraint $\dot x = f$ — and packs the optimisation into pointwise Hamiltonian maximisation.

The control Hamiltonian

Define

$$H(x, u, \lambda, t) \;=\; \lambda^\top f(x,u,t) \;-\; L(x,u,t).$$

(Sign convention: many texts write $H = \lambda^\top f + L$ and minimise; we use the cleaner "maximise $H$" form. The two are equivalent under $\lambda \mapsto -\lambda$.)

Pontryagin's Maximum Principle. If $u^*(\cdot)$ is optimal with state trajectory $x^*(\cdot)$, then there exists an absolutely continuous co-state $\lambda^*(\cdot)$ such that $$\dot x^* \;=\; \partial_\lambda H \;=\; f(x^*,u^*,t),$$ $$\dot \lambda^* \;=\; -\partial_x H \;=\; -\lambda^{*\top}\partial_x f + \partial_x L,$$ $$u^*(t) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{u\in U} H(x^*(t),\,u,\,\lambda^*(t),\,t)\quad\text{a.e.}\,t,$$ with the transversality condition $\lambda^*(T) = -\partial_x \Phi(x^*(T))$.

The geometric content

Three observations make the principle memorable:

  1. It's Hamilton's equations. The state-costate flow $(\dot x, \dot\lambda) = (\partial_\lambda H, -\partial_x H)$ is the canonical Hamiltonian flow on the cotangent bundle $T^*\mathbb{R}^n$ with $H$ as Hamiltonian. The co-state $\lambda$ is the conjugate momentum; in mechanics this recovers Hamilton's principle of stationary action.
  2. It's pointwise. The optimisation over $u\in U$ happens independently at each $t$ — no integrals, no infinite-dimensional optimisation. This is what makes Pontryagin computable.
  3. It generates "bang-bang" controls. When $H$ is affine in $u$ on a box $U=[u_{\min},u_{\max}]$, the maximiser is at an endpoint: $u^*(t)\in\{u_{\min},u_{\max}\}$, switching when $\partial_u H$ crosses zero. The switching set is determined by the co-state.

Variational derivation in one frame

Suppose $(x^*,u^*)$ is optimal. Augment the cost by the dynamics multiplier:

$$\widetilde J = \int_0^T \!\!\bigl[\,L(x,u,t) + \lambda^\top(f(x,u,t)-\dot x)\,\bigr]\,dt + \Phi(x(T)).$$

Integrate the $\lambda^\top\dot x$ term by parts and identify the integrand as $\lambda^\top f - L = H$ (with sign), then collect coefficients of $\delta x$, $\delta u$, $\delta x(T)$:

  • Coefficient of $\delta x$ vanishes $\Rightarrow$ $\dot \lambda = -\partial_x H$.
  • Coefficient of $\delta x(T)$ vanishes $\Rightarrow$ $\lambda(T)=-\partial_x \Phi$.
  • $\delta u$ is constrained ($u\in U$); the directional derivative of $\widetilde J$ along admissible $\delta u$ must be non-negative $\Rightarrow$ $u^*$ maximises $H$ over $U$ pointwise.

The last is the only step that distinguishes the principle from a calculus-of-variations argument — the constraint $U$ becomes pointwise maximisation rather than $\partial_u H = 0$.

4. The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

Bellman's view inverts the direction of thought. Rather than "find the optimal trajectory from $x_0$," ask: for every state $x$ and time $t$, what is the optimal cost-to-go? Define the value function

$$V(x,t) \;=\; \inf_{u(\cdot)} \Big\{ \int_t^T L(x(s),u(s),s)\,ds + \Phi(x(T))\ \Big|\ x(t)=x \Big\}.$$

By Bellman's principle of optimality — "the tail of an optimal trajectory is optimal for the sub-problem" — for any $\delta t>0$,

$$V(x,t) \;=\; \inf_{u\,\in\,U}\Big\{ L(x,u,t)\delta t + V\bigl(x+f(x,u,t)\delta t,\ t+\delta t\bigr)\Big\} + o(\delta t).$$

Taylor-expand $V$ to first order: $V(x+f\delta t, t+\delta t) = V + \partial_t V\,\delta t + \partial_x V \cdot f\,\delta t + o(\delta t)$. Substitute, divide by $\delta t$, send $\delta t\to 0$:

$$\boxed{\;-\partial_t V(x,t) \;=\; \inf_{u\in U}\Big\{\, L(x,u,t) + \partial_x V(x,t)\cdot f(x,u,t)\,\Big\},\qquad V(x,T)=\Phi(x).\;}$$

This is the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation — a first-order nonlinear PDE in $V(x,t)$ over $\mathbb{R}^n\times[0,T]$, terminal condition at $t=T$. The infimand on the right is precisely the (negated) control Hamiltonian: $\inf_u\{L + \partial_x V\cdot f\} = -\sup_u\{ -L + (-\partial_x V)\cdot f\}$. Pontryagin reappears with $\lambda(t) = -\partial_x V(x^*(t),t)$.

From the value to the optimal feedback

If $V$ is known, the optimal control at $(x,t)$ is the pointwise minimiser:

$$u^*(x,t) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{u\in U}\Big\{ L(x,u,t) + \partial_x V(x,t)\cdot f(x,u,t)\Big\}.$$

This is a feedback law — a function of the current state, not a pre-computed open-loop schedule. Feedback is robust to noise and modelling error; open-loop schedules are not. The HJB approach natively yields feedback.

Two pathologies and one rescue

  • Classical solutions may not exist. Even smooth $f,L,\Phi$ can produce a $V$ that is only Lipschitz, with shocks in $\partial_x V$ where multiple trajectories collide.
  • The PDE is nonlinear. Closed-form $V$ is rare; numerical methods (semi-Lagrangian, fast marching) are standard.
  • Viscosity solutions. Crandall & Lions (1983) introduced the viscosity solution: a Lipschitz $V$ that satisfies one-sided differential inequalities at every test point. Existence + uniqueness + stability hold; the optimal-control $V$ is the unique viscosity solution of HJB.

Pontryagin and HJB are not alternatives — they are dual descriptions of the same flow. Pontryagin's $(x^*(t),\lambda^*(t))$ traces a characteristic curve of the HJB PDE; HJB is the "global" PDE whose pointwise method-of-characteristics gives Pontryagin's ODEs.

5. Linear-quadratic regulator (LQR)

The one case where everything collapses to closed form, and the workhorse of practical control: linear dynamics, quadratic cost.

$$\dot x = A(t)\,x + B(t)\,u, \qquad J = \tfrac12\int_0^T\!\!\bigl(x^\top Q\,x + u^\top R\,u\bigr)\,dt + \tfrac12\,x(T)^\top S\,x(T),$$

with $Q\succeq 0$, $R\succ 0$, $S\succeq 0$. The control is unconstrained ($U=\mathbb{R}^m$). Now plug a quadratic ansatz $V(x,t) = \tfrac12 x^\top P(t)\,x$ into HJB.

The Riccati ODE

$\partial_t V = \tfrac12 x^\top \dot P x$, $\partial_x V = P x$. The HJB minimisation $\min_u\{u^\top R u/2 + (Px)^\top(Ax + Bu) + x^\top Q x/2\}$ has unconstrained minimiser

$$u^*(x,t) \;=\; -R^{-1}B^\top P(t)\,x.$$

Substituting back and matching the $x^\top(\cdot)x$ coefficient:

$$\boxed{\;-\dot P \;=\; A^\top P + P\,A \;-\; P\,B R^{-1}B^\top P \;+\; Q,\qquad P(T)=S.\;}$$

This is the continuous-time Riccati differential equation. It is a matrix ODE running backward in time from $P(T)=S$; once solved, the optimal control is the state-linear feedback $u^*(t)=-K(t)\,x(t)$ with gain $K=R^{-1}B^\top P$.

Infinite horizon: the algebraic Riccati equation

If $A,B,Q,R$ are constant and we take $T\to\infty$ (with discount or a stabilisability hypothesis), $P(t)$ converges to a fixed point $\bar P$ satisfying the algebraic Riccati equation

$$A^\top \bar P + \bar P A - \bar P B R^{-1}B^\top \bar P + Q = 0,$$

and the feedback $u^* = -R^{-1}B^\top \bar P\,x$ is the steady-state LQR controller. Among all stabilising linear feedbacks, this one minimises the $\mathcal{H}_2$ cost. It's the answer your textbook autopilot is computing.

The closed-loop spectrum and root-locus

The closed-loop matrix is $A_{\rm cl} = A - BR^{-1}B^\top \bar P$. Its eigenvalues — the modes of $\dot x = A_{\rm cl}x$ — sit in the left half-plane (open-loop stable) for any controllable $(A,B)$ and weight $(Q,R)$. As $R\to 0$ ("cheap control") the modes race leftward; as $R\to\infty$ ("expensive control") they snap back to the open-loop eigenvalues, possibly unstable.

6. Discrete-time DP and the Bellman equation

Discrete time replaces the ODE $\dot x = f(x,u,t)$ with a recurrence $x_{n+1} = f(x_n, u_n)$, and the integral cost with a sum. The value function becomes

$$V_n(x) \;=\; \min_{u\in U}\Big\{\, c(x,u) + V_{n+1}\bigl(f(x,u)\bigr)\Big\},\qquad V_N(x) = \Phi(x).$$

This is Bellman's equation — the discrete-time HJB. It is solved by backward recursion: start with $V_N=\Phi$ and step $n=N-1, N-2, \ldots, 0$.

Why this is a big deal

Naïve search over all length-$N$ control sequences from a state space of size $|X|$ with $|U|$ controls per step costs $O(|U|^N)$. Backward DP reduces it to $O(N\cdot|X|\cdot|U|)$ — exponential to polynomial. This is Bellman's "principle of optimality" in action: never re-solve a subproblem you've already solved.

Infinite horizon: value iteration & policy iteration

Drop time-dependence and discount with $\gamma\in[0,1)$:

$$V(x) \;=\; \min_{u}\Big\{ c(x,u) + \gamma\,V\bigl(f(x,u)\bigr)\Big\}.$$

Define the Bellman operator $T\colon V\mapsto TV$ on the right. Then $T$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in sup-norm: $\|TV - TV'\|_\infty \le \gamma\|V-V'\|_\infty$. By the Banach fixed-point theorem, iterating $V_{k+1}=TV_k$ from any seed converges geometrically to the unique fixed point — the true value function. This is value iteration; convergence rate is $\gamma^k$.

An accelerated cousin: policy iteration alternates (i) policy evaluation: solve the linear system $V^\pi = c^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V^\pi$ for the value of the current policy $\pi$, and (ii) policy improvement: $\pi'(x) = \arg\min_u\{c(x,u)+\gamma V^\pi(f(x,u))\}$. Howard (1960): policy iteration converges in finitely many steps on finite state spaces, with monotone improvement at each step.

The contraction-mapping proof in one line

For any $V, V'$ and any state $x$, let $u^*$ achieve $\min$ in $TV(x)$:

$$TV(x) - TV'(x) \;\le\; \gamma\bigl(V(f(x,u^*)) - V'(f(x,u^*))\bigr) \;\le\; \gamma\|V-V'\|_\infty.$$

By symmetry $|TV(x)-TV'(x)|\le\gamma\|V-V'\|_\infty$, then take the sup over $x$. Banach gives the rest.

7. Stochastic dynamic programming

Real systems are noisy. The state equation becomes a controlled stochastic differential equation

$$dx_t \;=\; b(x_t, u_t, t)\,dt + \sigma(x_t, u_t, t)\,dW_t,\qquad x_0 = x,$$

driven by a Brownian motion $W_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$. The control $u_t$ is now adapted (depends only on the history of $W$ up to time $t$). The cost is

$$J[u] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\int_0^T L(x_t,u_t,t)\,dt + \Phi(x_T)\Big].$$

The controlled generator and stochastic HJB

For a fixed control $u$, the controlled diffusion has infinitesimal generator

$$\mathcal{L}^u \phi(x) \;=\; b(x,u,t)\cdot\nabla\phi(x) + \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}\!\bigl(\sigma\sigma^\top\,D^2\phi(x)\bigr).$$

Bellman's principle now uses Itô's formula in place of the chain rule: $V(x_{t+\delta},t+\delta) - V(x_t,t) = (\partial_t V + \mathcal{L}^u V)\delta t + \text{martingale}$. The optimality condition is

$$\boxed{\;-\partial_t V \;=\; \inf_{u\in U}\Big\{\, L(x,u,t) + \mathcal{L}^u V(x,t)\,\Big\}, \qquad V(x,T)=\Phi(x).\;}$$

This is the stochastic HJB equation — now a second-order nonlinear PDE because $\mathcal{L}^u$ carries the diffusion's Laplacian-like term. The infimand expands to

$$L(x,u,t) + b(x,u,t)\cdot\nabla V + \tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}(\sigma\sigma^\top D^2 V).$$

Deterministic control is the $\sigma\to 0$ limit — the second-order term vanishes and we recover the first-order HJB of §4. Curvature in the controls (Brownian noise) is paid for by curvature in the value (a Laplacian).

Merton's portfolio problem

Robert Merton's 1969 PhD example, the canonical entry point to mathematical finance. An investor has wealth $X_t$, splits it between a risk-free bond (rate $r$) and a risky asset (drift $\mu$, vol $\sigma$). Let $u_t\in[0,1]$ be the fraction in the risky asset; consumption $c_t\ge 0$. Wealth evolves

$$dX_t = \bigl((r + u_t(\mu-r))X_t - c_t\bigr)dt + u_t\sigma X_t\,dW_t.$$

Maximise expected discounted utility of consumption $\mathbb{E}\!\int_0^\infty e^{-\rho t}\frac{c_t^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\,dt$ (CRRA preferences, $\gamma\in(0,1)\cup(1,\infty)$). HJB plus the ansatz $V(X)=KX^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ collapses to closed form:

$$u^* \;=\; \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma\,\sigma^2},\qquad c^* = \text{constant fraction}\cdot X.$$

The optimal risky-asset fraction $u^*$ is independent of wealth and horizon — a striking consequence of the CRRA structure. Mean-variance investing recovers Markowitz; the $\gamma$ that calibrates risk aversion makes it the bridge between behaviour and dynamics.

Connections

Optimal control is the meeting place of calculus of variations, PDE theory, and probability. Its deepest open questions sit at the boundaries: high-dimensional HJB (the "curse of dimensionality") is partially tamed by deep-learning approximations; the regularity theory of viscosity solutions still has loose ends in degenerate-diffusion stochastic control; mean-field control on measure spaces (McKean–Vlasov dynamics, mean-field games) is an active frontier where optimal control meets statistical physics.

  • Variational methods — Euler–Lagrange equations — the unconstrained ancestor; Pontryagin reduces to E–L when $U$ is open and $H$ is differentiable in $u$.
  • Hamiltonians — Hamilton's equations — the state–costate pair $(x,\lambda)$ is the canonical phase-space pair; Pontryagin's flow is a Hamiltonian flow with $H$ as Hamiltonian.
  • PDEs — first-order nonlinear — HJB is exactly the kind of nonlinear first-order PDE that viscosity solutions were invented to handle.
  • Dynamical systems — fixed points and linearisation — the closed-loop LQR system $\dot x=A_{\rm cl}x$ is the linearised dynamics; LQR stabilises every controllable equilibrium.
  • Stochastic calculus — Feynman–Kac — the stochastic HJB's linearisation under a fixed control is a Feynman–Kac PDE; this is the bridge to mathematical finance and the Merton problem.
