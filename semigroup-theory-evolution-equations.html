Semigroup theory and evolution equations

Time-evolution of linear PDEs as operator semigroups: from C₀ semigroups and the Hille–Yosida characterization of generators, through analytic semigroups and parabolic regularity, to the abstract Cauchy problem and its nonlinear, control-theoretic, and stochastic descendants.

1. Strongly continuous (C₀) semigroups

A linear autonomous evolution equation $u'(t)=Au(t)$, $u(0)=u_0$, on a Banach space $X$ should have a solution operator: take initial data, return the state at time $t$. Encode the family of such operators as a map $T\colon[0,\infty)\to B(X)$ satisfying three properties:

  • $T(0)=I$ (zero time, no evolution);
  • $T(s+t)=T(s)\,T(t)$ for $s,t\ge 0$ (Chapman–Kolmogorov / time-translation invariance);
  • $t\mapsto T(t)x$ is continuous in $t$ for every fixed $x\in X$ (the strong-continuity axiom).

Such a family $\{T(t)\}_{t\ge 0}$ is a strongly continuous semigroup, or $C_0$-semigroup. The third axiom is the weakest reasonable one: norm continuity ($\|T(t)-T(s)\|\to 0$) is far too strong — it forces the generator to be bounded, ruling out most PDE examples. Pointwise-in-$x$ continuity is the right calibration: differential operators have $T(t)\to I$ strongly but never in operator norm.

The structural growth bound is automatic: there exist $M\ge 1$ and $\omega\in\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$\|T(t)\|_{B(X)} \;\le\; M\,e^{\omega t} \qquad \text{for all } t\ge 0.$$

The proof is short: strong continuity at $t=0$ plus the uniform-boundedness principle gives $\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\|T(t)\|\le M$, then the semigroup law extends this to every $t$ by writing $t=n+s$. The constants $(M,\omega)$ classify the semigroup: $\omega\le 0$ with $M=1$ is the contraction case, $\omega=0$ alone is uniformly bounded, and the infimum of admissible $\omega$ is the growth bound $\omega_0(T)$.

Three canonical PDE examples set the picture. The heat semigroup $T(t)=e^{t\Delta}$ on $X=L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a contraction: $\|T(t)f\|_{L^2}\le\|f\|_{L^2}$ by Plancherel applied to the Fourier multiplier $e^{-t|\xi|^2}$, which has modulus $\le 1$. The wave-equation propagator on $X=H^1\times L^2$ (the energy space, holding $(u,u_t)$) is a $C_0$-group, $\|T(t)\|\le 1$ in the energy norm, signed time. The Schrödinger group $T(t)=e^{-it\Delta}$ on $L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a unitary $C_0$-group: $\|T(t)f\|_{L^2}=\|f\|_{L^2}$ exactly, and reversibility means we can run time backwards.

2. The infinitesimal generator

The whole semigroup is encoded by its behavior at $t=0^+$. Given a $C_0$-semigroup $T(t)$ on $X$, define the infinitesimal generator $A$ by

$$Ax \;=\; \lim_{t\to 0^+}\frac{T(t)x - x}{t},\qquad D(A) \;=\; \Bigl\{x\in X \;:\; \text{this limit exists in }X\Bigr\}.$$

The basic structure of $A$ is forced by the semigroup axioms:

  • $D(A)$ is a dense linear subspace of $X$ (density follows from $\frac{1}{t}\int_0^t T(s)x\,ds\to x$ and the integrals lie in $D(A)$);
  • $A$ is closed: $\{(x_n,Ax_n)\}\to (x,y)$ in $X\times X$ forces $x\in D(A)$ and $Ax=y$;
  • $A$ is uniquely determined by $T$, and conversely $T$ is uniquely determined by $A$ — the generator is a complete invariant.

The fundamental differentiation identity is the operator analogue of $\frac{d}{dt}e^{tA}=Ae^{tA}=e^{tA}A$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}T(t)x \;=\; A\,T(t)x \;=\; T(t)\,A x \qquad \text{for }x\in D(A),\ t\ge 0.$$

So orbits starting in $D(A)$ are differentiable, and $u(t)=T(t)u_0$ is a classical solution of $u'=Au$, $u(0)=u_0$. For $u_0\in X\setminus D(A)$ the orbit need not be differentiable but is still continuous; we call $T(t)u_0$ a mild solution (the integrated form of the equation). The generator $A$ is bounded ($A\in B(X)$) iff $T$ is uniformly continuous, and in that case $T(t)=e^{tA}=\sum_{k\ge 0}(tA)^k/k!$ converges in operator norm.

Examples make the rough/smooth distinction concrete. For the heat semigroup on $L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $A=\Delta$ with $D(A)=H^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ — two derivatives in $L^2$. For the translation semigroup $T(t)f(x)=f(x+t)$ on $L^p(\mathbb{R})$, $A=d/dx$ with $D(A)=W^{1,p}$. The pattern is that $D(A)$ is the natural Sobolev space the differential operator $A$ likes; functions outside $D(A)$ don't have enough regularity for the strong limit to converge.

3. The Hille–Yosida theorem

Which closed densely-defined operators $A$ are generators of $C_0$-semigroups? The full classification is the Hille–Yosida theorem (Hille 1948, Yosida 1948 — independent proofs). Stated for contractions ($M=1$, $\omega=0$):

$A$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup of contractions iff $A$ is closed, densely defined, every $\lambda>0$ lies in the resolvent set $\rho(A)$, and

$$\bigl\|\,(\lambda I - A)^{-1}\,\bigr\|_{B(X)} \;\le\; \frac{1}{\lambda} \qquad \text{for all }\lambda>0.$$

The extension to general $C_0$-semigroups with $\|T(t)\|\le M e^{\omega t}$ requires the resolvent estimate at every $\lambda>\omega$ on every iterate: $\|(\lambda-A)^{-n}\|\le M/(\lambda-\omega)^n$ for $n=1,2,\ldots$. The single-power estimate suffices only for $M=1$.

The Lumer–Phillips theorem repackages this without the iterate condition. Call $A$ dissipative if $\|(\lambda - A)x\|\ge\lambda\|x\|$ for every $\lambda>0$ and $x\in D(A)$ (equivalently, on a Hilbert space, $\mathrm{Re}\langle Ax,x\rangle\le 0$). Lumer–Phillips: $A$ generates a contraction $C_0$-semigroup iff $A$ is dissipative and $\mathrm{range}(\lambda - A) = X$ for some (equivalently, every) $\lambda>0$. The range condition is the hard hypothesis to check; on a Hilbert space it follows from $A$ closed plus $A^*$ also dissipative.

The proof of the existence direction is the Yosida approximation: define

$$A_n \;=\; n\,A\,(n - A)^{-1} \;=\; n^2(n-A)^{-1} - n\,I,$$

which is a bounded operator on $X$ (the resolvent estimate guarantees $\|n(n-A)^{-1}\|\le 1$). Then $A_n x\to Ax$ for $x\in D(A)$, the bounded exponential $T_n(t)=e^{tA_n}=\sum_k(tA_n)^k/k!$ is well-defined for each $n$, and $T_n(t)\to T(t)$ strongly. The Yosida approximation is the operator-theoretic analogue of regularizing an unbounded function by a bounded approximant: trade strong convergence away from a singularity for the right to compute classical exponentials on the regularization.

4. Analytic semigroups and parabolic regularity

The heat semigroup is sharper than a generic $C_0$-semigroup in two visible ways: $T(t)u_0$ is instantaneously $C^\infty$ for any $u_0\in L^2$, and $T(t)$ extends from $t\in[0,\infty)$ to a holomorphic family on a complex sector. These two facts are equivalent. A $C_0$-semigroup $T(t)$ on $X$ is analytic if there is a sector

$$\Sigma_\delta \;=\; \{\,z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,z\ne 0,\ |\arg z|<\delta\,\},\qquad 0<\delta\le\pi/2,$$

and a holomorphic extension $T\colon \Sigma_\delta\to B(X)$ such that $T(t_1+t_2)=T(t_1)T(t_2)$ for $t_1,t_2\in\Sigma_\delta$ and $T(z)x\to x$ as $z\to 0$ in any subsector $\Sigma_{\delta'}$, $\delta'<\delta$.

The resolvent characterization is what one actually checks in practice. $A$ generates an analytic $C_0$-semigroup iff there is some $\delta\in(0,\pi/2]$ such that the resolvent set contains the sector $\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\lambda\ne 0,\ |\arg\lambda|<\pi/2+\delta\}$ and

$$\bigl\|\,(\lambda - A)^{-1}\,\bigr\| \;\le\; \frac{M}{|\lambda|}\qquad \text{on this sector.}$$

Compared to Hille–Yosida (which only needs $\lambda>0$), analyticity demands the resolvent estimate on a fattened half-plane that bulges past the imaginary axis. This is why second-order elliptic operators (Laplacian, more general uniformly elliptic $-\nabla\cdot(a\nabla)$) generate analytic semigroups but first-order skew-symmetric operators (like $i\,d/dx$ for Schrödinger) do not — the latter has spectrum on the imaginary axis, and no fattened sector contains it.

Two structural consequences of analyticity, both pivotal for parabolic PDEs:

  • Instant smoothing. For $t>0$ and any $k\ge 0$: $T(t)x\in D(A^k)$ for every $x\in X$, and $\|A^k T(t)\|\le C_k/t^k$. Apply this with $A=\Delta$: $T(t)L^2\subset H^{2k}$ for every $k$, so $T(t)L^2\subset C^\infty$ for every $t>0$. The heat equation regularizes data instantly.
  • Maximal $L^p$ regularity. If $A$ generates an analytic semigroup on a UMD space (a Banach space where the Hilbert transform is bounded — includes every $L^p$ with $1<p<\infty$), the inhomogeneous problem $u'-Au=f$ with $u(0)=0$ has a solution with $u', Au\in L^p(0,T;X)$ whenever $f\in L^p(0,T;X)$, with norm equivalent to $\|f\|$. This is the right $L^p$-existence theory for parabolic PDEs.

Comparison: the wave-equation propagator on $H^1\times L^2$ is a $C_0$-group with $\|T(t)\|=1$ (energy conservation) but not analytic — initial singularities propagate forever, never smoothing. The Schrödinger group $e^{-it\Delta}$ has the same behaviour: singularities disperse but the wavefront set (the phase-space record of singular directions) is preserved, no instant smoothing.

5. Evolution equations as abstract Cauchy problems

Recast a linear PDE in evolutionary form by choosing a Banach space $X$ and a generator $A$ on $X$ so the equation becomes

$$u'(t)\;=\;A\,u(t)\;+\;f(t),\qquad u(0)\;=\;u_0,\qquad t\in[0,T].$$

This is the abstract Cauchy problem. The benefit is uniformity: every linear time-evolution PDE in this notebook fits the same template, with $X$ and $A$ chosen to fit the equation. Heat: $X=L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $A=\Delta$, $D(A)=H^2$. Wave (first-order form): $X=H^1\times L^2$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\\Delta&0\end{pmatrix}$. Schrödinger: $X=L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $A=-i\Delta$. Advection-diffusion: $X=L^2$, $A=\Delta-b\cdot\nabla$, $D(A)=H^2$.

Two notions of solution are useful. A strict (classical) solution on $[0,T]$ is $u\in C^1([0,T];X)\cap C([0,T];D(A))$ satisfying the equation pointwise. A mild solution is the integrated form: by the variation-of-parameters / Duhamel formula,

$$u(t) \;=\; T(t)\,u_0 \;+\; \int_0^t T(t-s)\,f(s)\,ds.$$

If $A$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup $T$, the homogeneous part contributes $T(t)u_0$ for any $u_0\in X$ (continuous in $t$, classical only when $u_0\in D(A)$); the inhomogeneous part demands an integrability condition on $f$. Standard sufficient conditions: $f\in C([0,T];X)$ + $u_0\in D(A)$ gives a mild solution that is automatically continuous; $f\in C^1$ or $f\in C([0,T];D(A))$ promotes mild → classical. The mild form is the right generality: it gives existence under minimum hypotheses, and the classical regularity then follows from extra conditions on $f$.

Uniqueness is automatic given $A$ closed (generators of $C_0$-semigroups are): two mild solutions differ by an orbit of the homogeneous problem starting at zero, which is identically zero by the semigroup law. The result: once $A$ generates a $C_0$-semigroup, the abstract Cauchy problem $u'=Au+f$ with $u(0)=u_0$ has a unique mild solution for every $u_0\in X$ and every continuous $f$, given by Duhamel's formula.

6. Applications: nonlinear PDEs, control, and stochastic flows

Three downstream uses of the semigroup framework — each uses Duhamel's formula plus an extra ingredient.

(a) Nonlinear PDEs by fixed-point iteration. Consider $u'=Au+F(u)$, $u(0)=u_0$, with $A$ generating a $C_0$-semigroup and $F\colon X\to X$ locally Lipschitz. The Duhamel form

$$u(t)\;=\;T(t)u_0\;+\;\int_0^t T(t-s)F(u(s))\,ds$$

turns the equation into a fixed-point problem $u=\Phi(u)$ on $C([0,T_*];X)$. For $T_*$ small, $\Phi$ is a contraction (Lipschitz constant of $F$ times $T_*\sup_t\|T(t)\|$); Banach's theorem gives a unique mild solution on $[0,T_*]$. The maximal interval of existence is determined by a blow-up criterion: either the solution exists for all $t\ge 0$, or $\limsup_{t\to T_{\max}}\|u(t)\|=\infty$. This recipe handles semilinear heat ($A=\Delta$, $F=u^p$ — Fujita's theorem on critical exponents), Navier–Stokes (mild solutions of Kato), reaction-diffusion, and nonlinear Schrödinger.

(b) Control theory. The controlled system $u'=Au+Bv$, $u(0)=u_0$, with $B$ a control operator and $v(t)$ the control input, has $u(T)=T(T)u_0+\int_0^T T(T-s)Bv(s)\,ds$. Approximate controllability from $u_0$ at time $T$: the reachable set $\{T(T)u_0+\int_0^T T(T-s)Bv\,ds:v\in L^2(0,T;U)\}$ is dense in $X$. Exact controllability: it equals $X$. For the heat equation on a bounded domain $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ with control on a subdomain $\omega\subset\Omega$, $\omega$ open and nonempty, the system is approximately controllable in any time $T>0$ — a consequence of analyticity (controllability inherits from the Hahn–Banach dual problem about unique continuation, which holds for analytic semigroups). For wave equations, exact controllability requires $\omega$ to satisfy the geometric control condition: every billiard trajectory must enter $\omega$ in finite time (Bardos–Lebeau–Rauch).

(c) Stochastic flows. An Itô SDE $dX_t=b(X_t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t)\,dW_t$ with smooth $b,\sigma$ defines the Markov semigroup $T(t)f(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(X_t)\mid X_0=x]$ on bounded measurable functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$. Itô's formula computes its generator: at smooth $f$,

$$Af(x)\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(\sigma\sigma^\top)_{ij}(x)\,\partial_i\partial_j f(x)\;+\;\sum_i b_i(x)\,\partial_i f(x).$$

This is a second-order elliptic operator (degenerate when $\sigma\sigma^\top$ is rank-deficient). For $\sigma=I$ and $b=0$ — Brownian motion — $A=\frac{1}{2}\Delta$, recovering the heat semigroup. The link is the Feynman–Kac formula: solutions of parabolic PDEs $u_t=Au+Vu$ have stochastic representations $u(t,x)=\mathbb{E}_x[e^{\int_0^t V(X_s)ds}f(X_t)]$, turning analytic estimates on $T(t)$ into probabilistic statements about path integrals (and vice versa).

7. Connections

Semigroup theory is the operator-theoretic spine that makes "evolution PDE" a single subject rather than a list of equations. Open frontiers include nonautonomous (time-dependent generators $A(t)$) and the corresponding evolution families, semigroups on quasi-Banach / Fréchet spaces (kinetic / Boltzmann theory), the link between dispersive and parabolic regularity (smoothing estimates for Schrödinger), and the analytic-semigroup theory of stochastic PDEs (Da Prato–Zabczyk's mild-solution machine for $du = A u\,dt + dW$).

  • Functional analysis — Bounded operators & adjoints — the Banach-space scaffolding that holds $T(t)$ and $A$.
  • Partial differential equations — Heat equation & heat kernel — the canonical analytic-semigroup example, fully visible at the level of the Gaussian kernel.
  • Sobolev spaces & distributions — $W^{k,p}$ and $H^s$ — the natural domains $D(A^k)$ for differential generators.
  • Spectral theory — spectrum of a self-adjoint operator — diagonalizes self-adjoint generators so $T(t)=e^{tA}$ is computed mode-by-mode.
  • Microlocal analysis — wavefront set — explains why Schrödinger and wave propagators preserve singularities (no instant smoothing) while heat erases them.
  • Stochastic calculus — Itô formula — derives the second-order elliptic generator of a Markov semigroup from an SDE.
  • Stochastic processes & martingales — continuous martingales — Markov semigroup acts on bounded measurable functions of the diffusion's path.
