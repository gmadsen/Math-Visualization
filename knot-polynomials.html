Knot polynomials

Tangled circles in $S^3$, projected to a plane and reduced by three local moves, then turned into Laurent polynomials. From Alexander to Jones to HOMFLY to the modern quantum picture: each step trades more topology for more algebra.

1. Knots and links

A knot is an embedding $K\colon S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3$ — a circle sitting inside a 3-sphere without self-intersections. A link is the same but with finitely many disjoint circles. We never just look at the image: two embeddings count as the same knot when one can be continuously deformed into the other through embeddings, i.e. when there is an ambient isotopy of $S^3$ carrying one to the other. The equivalence class of the round circle is the unknot; everything else is a genuine tangle.

Why $S^3$ and not $\mathbb{R}^3$? Because the one-point compactification adds nothing topologically — every knot in $\mathbb{R}^3$ misses some point at infinity, and any ambient isotopy can be pushed away from that point. Working in $S^3$ makes the symmetry between a knot and its complement cleaner: the knot complement $S^3 \setminus K$ is a 3-manifold whose homotopy type detects (almost) everything about $K$.

Knot table starter pack. The simplest non-trivial knots are the trefoil $3_1$ (3 crossings, two mirror forms), the figure-eight $4_1$ (4 crossings, equal to its mirror), and the $5_1$ and $5_2$ knots. The simplest non-trivial link is the Hopf link, two circles linked through each other once.

The round circle: the simplest equivalence class. Every knot is its diagram modulo Reidemeister moves; the unknot is the class of every diagram that can be reduced to a circle.

Diagrams are how we actually compute. Project a knot onto a plane in general position: you get a 4-valent planar graph (the projection) decorated at every vertex with which strand crosses over the other. The number of crossings depends on the projection — the crossing number $c(K)$ is the minimum over all diagrams. For the trefoil, $c(3_1) = 3$; for the figure-eight, $c(4_1) = 4$.

2. Reidemeister moves

Two diagrams represent the same knot iff one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of three local moves and planar isotopy. This is Reidemeister's theorem, and it converts a continuous 3D problem (ambient isotopy) into a combinatorial rewrite system on planar diagrams. The three moves are:

  • R1 — add or remove a single twist (a kink). Changes writhe by $\pm 1$.
  • R2 — slide one strand under another and back (a "poke"). Adds or removes one $+$ and one $-$ crossing; writhe unchanged.
  • R3 — slide a strand across a crossing of two other strands. Same crossings, different positions; writhe unchanged.
Reidemeister moves
switch between the three moves · before / after on each side
R1 — adding or removing a single twist (kink). Changes the writhe by ±1.

Reidemeister's theorem is the engine behind every diagrammatic invariant on this page. To prove that some assignment $D \mapsto P(D)$ is a knot invariant, you only need to check three local pictures: that $P$ is unchanged under R1, R2, R3. Many invariants are nearly invariant — only failing under R1 — and you have to multiply by a writhe-correction factor to get a true invariant. The Jones polynomial is exactly this story.

3. The Alexander polynomial

Alexander (1928) was the first knot polynomial, and it is the most "topological" — it comes from algebraic topology of the knot complement. Pick a Seifert surface $\Sigma$ for $K$: an oriented surface in $S^3$ with $\partial \Sigma = K$. Such a $\Sigma$ exists for every oriented knot (Seifert's algorithm constructs one from a diagram). The first homology $H_1(\Sigma) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{2g}$ where $g$ is the genus of $\Sigma$, and on it lives the Seifert form $V_{ij} = \mathrm{lk}(\alpha_i^+, \alpha_j)$ — the linking number of a basis curve pushed up off the surface with another basis curve.

The Alexander polynomial is then

$\displaystyle \Delta_K(t) = \det(V - tV^T) \in \mathbb{Z}[t, t^{-1}]$,

well-defined up to a unit $\pm t^k$. Different choices of Seifert surface give $V$'s related by elementary stabilization, and the determinant changes by such a unit.

Δ(t) = 1 — the unknot has trivial Alexander polynomial.

Alexander's polynomial has a beautiful symmetry — $\Delta_K(t^{-1}) = \Delta_K(t)$ up to units — reflecting the fact that the knot complement has a Poincaré-duality structure. It satisfies a skein relation

$\Delta(L_+) - \Delta(L_-) = (t^{1/2} - t^{-1/2})\,\Delta(L_0),$

where $L_+, L_-, L_0$ are the standard $+/-/$smoothing diagrams at one chosen crossing — and this relation, plus $\Delta(\mathrm{unknot}) = 1$, recursively determines $\Delta$ for any knot or link.

Limitation. The Alexander polynomial is invariant under mirroring: $\Delta_{K^*} = \Delta_K$. So it cannot distinguish the right trefoil from the left trefoil. It also fails to detect the unknot — there are infinitely many knots (the Conway knot, the Kinoshita–Terasaka knot, …) with $\Delta = 1$.

4. The Jones polynomial

Vaughan Jones (1984) discovered a different polynomial — coming from operator algebras on subfactors — that turned out to detect chirality. The cleanest combinatorial route is via Kauffman's state-sum bracket. Given an unoriented diagram $D$, the bracket $\langle D \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}[A^{\pm 1}]$ is defined by three rules:

  • Skein. $\langle L_\times \rangle = A \langle L_0 \rangle + A^{-1} \langle L_\infty \rangle$ — at each crossing, smooth in two ways and weight by $A^{\pm 1}$.
  • Loop. $\langle \bigcirc \sqcup L \rangle = (-A^2 - A^{-2}) \langle L \rangle$ — adding a disjoint circle multiplies by $\delta = -A^2 - A^{-2}$.
  • Empty. $\langle \varnothing \rangle = 1$.

To compute $\langle D \rangle$ on a diagram with $n$ crossings, expand each crossing both ways: you get $2^n$ states, each a planar collection of disjoint circles. Sum $A^{a-b} \delta^{|s|-1}$ over all states $s$, where $a$ is the number of $A$-smoothings, $b$ the number of $A^{-1}$-smoothings, and $|s|$ the number of circles.

The bracket is invariant under R2 and R3 but not R1: an R1 twist multiplies it by $-A^{\pm 3}$. Fix this with the writhe $w(D) = \sum_{\text{crossings}} \pm 1$. The combination

$X(D) = (-A)^{-3 w(D)} \langle D \rangle$

is invariant under all three Reidemeister moves; setting $A = q^{-1/4}$ converts it to the Jones polynomial $V_K(q) \in \mathbb{Z}[q^{1/2}, q^{-1/2}]$.

Chirality detection. $V_{3_1}(q) = -q^{-4} + q^{-3} + q^{-1}$ for the right trefoil; the left (mirror) trefoil has $V = -q^4 + q^3 + q$. They are different polynomials, so Jones distinguishes the trefoil from its mirror — a feat Alexander cannot perform.

The Jones polynomial obeys its own skein relation

$q^{-1} V(L_+) - q\, V(L_-) = (q^{1/2} - q^{-1/2})\, V(L_0),$

and is also categorified: Khovanov homology assigns to each link $L$ a bigraded chain complex $\mathrm{Kh}^{i,j}(L)$ whose graded Euler characteristic is exactly $V_L(q)$ (up to normalization). Knots have $\mathrm{Kh}$, much like topological spaces have singular homology — a richer invariant whose graded Euler characteristic recovers the polynomial.

5. The HOMFLY polynomial

HOMFLY (Hoste, Ocneanu, Millett, Freyd, Lickorish, Yetter — and independently Przytycki, Traczyk: HOMFLYPT) is a two-variable polynomial $P_K(\alpha, z) \in \mathbb{Z}[\alpha^{\pm 1}, z^{\pm 1}]$ uniquely determined by:

  • $P(\text{unknot}) = 1$,
  • the skein relation $\alpha\, P(L_+) - \alpha^{-1}\, P(L_-) = z\, P(L_0)$.

The two parameters $\alpha, z$ make HOMFLY strictly more powerful than either Alexander or Jones — it specialises to both:

Why two variables? The skein relation has three terms, and after fixing the value on the unknot, one variable's worth of freedom remains. This freedom is exactly what is needed to remember whether you are sitting in the $\mathfrak{sl}_n$ representation theory for varying $n$ — see the next section.

6. Quantum invariants

The unifying picture, due to Reshetikhin and Turaev (1990), is that every knot polynomial is a quantum invariant: a number obtained by reading an oriented tangle diagram top-to-bottom as a morphism in a ribbon category $\mathcal{C}$, with each elementary piece sent to a fixed morphism:

diagram piecemorphism in $\mathcal{C}$
vertical strand labelled $V$$\mathrm{id}_V \colon V \to V$
positive crossingbraiding $R_{V,V}\colon V \otimes V \to V \otimes V$
negative crossing$R_{V,V}^{-1}$
cap (left) / cup (right)evaluation $\mathrm{ev}\colon V \otimes V^* \to \mathbf{1}$ / coevaluation $\mathrm{coev}\colon \mathbf{1} \to V \otimes V^*$

The compatibility of these morphisms is exactly what a ribbon category encodes. Reidemeister moves translate to algebraic identities:

  • R3 $\Leftrightarrow$ Yang–Baxter equation: $R_{12} R_{13} R_{23} = R_{23} R_{13} R_{12}$.
  • R2 $\Leftrightarrow$ $R \cdot R^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$.
  • R1 $\Leftrightarrow$ ribbon-twist compatibility.

This is the grand unification: knot polynomials, statistical-mechanics partition functions, and 3-dimensional topological quantum field theories are facets of one combinatorial-categorical object. Witten's interpretation makes them path integrals for Chern–Simons theory; Reshetikhin–Turaev makes them honest combinatorial invariants. The volume conjecture relates the $N \to \infty$ asymptotics of the colored Jones polynomial to the hyperbolic volume of the knot complement — a still-open bridge between quantum and hyperbolic topology.

7. Vassiliev (finite-type) invariants

So far every invariant has been valued on actual knots. Vassiliev's idea (1990) is to extend any such invariant $V$ to singular knots: smooth immersions $S^1 \looparrowright S^3$ with finitely many transverse double points. The extension is forced by the local rule

$$V(K_\times) \;=\; V(K_+) \;-\; V(K_-),$$

where $K_\times$ has one specified double point and $K_\pm$ are its two crossing resolutions. Iterating replaces an $n$-fold singular knot by an alternating sum of $2^n$ ordinary knots — values at the singular end behave as derivatives of $V$.

Say $V$ is of finite type of order $\le n$ if it vanishes on every singular knot with $\ge n+1$ double points. Let $\mathcal{V}_n$ be the $\mathbb{Q}$-space of all such invariants. Then $\mathcal{V}_0 \subset \mathcal{V}_1 \subset \mathcal{V}_2 \subset \cdots$ is an exhaustive filtration, and each quotient $\mathcal{V}_n / \mathcal{V}_{n-1}$ is finite-dimensional.

Why finite-dimensional? A type-$n$ invariant on a singular knot with exactly $n$ double points depends only on the underlying chord diagram: the circle $S^1$ with $n$ unordered chords pairing double-point preimages. Chord diagrams modulo the 4T relation (a four-term identity forced by sliding a chord across a strand) form a finite-dimensional space in each order, and $\mathcal{V}_n / \mathcal{V}_{n-1}$ embeds into its dual.

The connection to existing invariants is striking. Substitute $q = e^h$ in the Jones polynomial and expand:

$$V_K(e^h) \;=\; \sum_{n\ge 0} v_n(K)\, h^n.$$

Each $v_n: \{\text{knots}\} \to \mathbb{Q}$ is a Vassiliev invariant of order $\le n$ (Bar-Natan). The same holds for Conway $\nabla_K(z)$ and HOMFLY. So Vassiliev's filtration contains the polynomial invariants — and conjecturally separates all knots (the open Vassiliev conjecture).

8. Khovanov homology

The Jones polynomial $V_L(q)$ assigns an integer to each power of $q$. Khovanov's idea (1999) is to upgrade those integers to vector spaces and the formal $\pm$ signs in the Kauffman state sum to a differential, producing a bigraded chain complex whose chain-homotopy type is a link invariant. Decategorifying — taking graded Euler characteristic — recovers Jones, but the upgrade is strict: Khovanov sees pairs of links Jones cannot.

Fix an oriented diagram $D$ with $n_+$ positive and $n_-$ negative crossings. Each crossing has a $0$-smoothing and a $1$-smoothing; picking one at every crossing gives a state $s \in \{0,1\}^n$ whose smoothed diagram is a union of $c(s)$ circles. Replace each circle by a 2-dim graded space $A = \mathbb{Q}\langle 1, X\rangle$ with $\deg(1) = 1$, $\deg(X) = -1$:

$$\widetilde{C}^{i,j}(D) \;=\; \bigoplus_{|s| - n_- = i} A^{\otimes c(s)}\{j - i + n_+ - 2n_-\}.$$

The differential $d: C^{i,j} \to C^{i+1,j}$ comes from flipping one $0$ to a $1$: a merge of two circles uses multiplication $A \otimes A \to A$ (with $X^2 = 0$); a split uses comultiplication $A \to A \otimes A$. Then $d^2 = 0$, and $H^{i,j}(L)$ is invariant under R1, R2, R3 up to bigraded chain-homotopy equivalence.

Decategorification. $\sum_{i,j} (-1)^i q^j \dim H^{i,j}(L) = (q + q^{-1})\, V_L(q^2)$, the unnormalised Jones polynomial. Each graded slice of the state sum has been replaced by a vector space; the $\pm 1$ signs by $(-1)^i$.

What does categorification gain? Three headline applications:

  • Strictly stronger than Jones. Bar-Natan's tables exhibit pairs of knots with identical $V_L(q)$ but different $H^{*,*}(L)$.
  • Unknot detection (Kronheimer–Mrowka, 2010). $H^{*,*}(L) \cong \mathbb{Q}[X]/X^2$ iff $L$ is the unknot. Whether Jones detects the unknot remains a celebrated open problem; Khovanov resolves it.
  • Functoriality under cobordism. An oriented surface cobordism $\Sigma: L_0 \to L_1 \subset \mathbb{R}^3 \times [0,1]$ induces a map $H^{*,*}(L_0) \to H^{*,*}(L_1)$ of bidegree $(0, \chi(\Sigma))$, well-defined up to sign. Jones has no such 4-dimensional refinement.

Khovanov homology was the first link homology and the start of a flood: Lee homology and Rasmussen's $s$-invariant (a combinatorial proof of the Milnor torus-knot conjecture), knot Floer homology, $\mathfrak{sl}_n$ Khovanov–Rozansky theories, HOMFLY homology. The slogan is every quantum invariant should be the Euler characteristic of a homology theory — categorification (Crane–Frenkel) lifts polynomial invariants to functors out of a cobordism category.
