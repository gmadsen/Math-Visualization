Kernel methods and RKHS

A single positive-definite kernel turns linear algorithms into nonlinear ones, builds an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of functions you can still optimize over a finite sample, and unifies ridge regression, support vector machines, and Gaussian processes under one inner product.

1. Positive-definite kernels & the kernel trick

A great many learning algorithms — least squares, the perceptron, PCA, ridge regression — touch the data only through inner products $\langle x_i,x_j\rangle$. The kernel trick is the observation that we may replace every such inner product by a kernel $k(x_i,x_j)$, and so long as $k$ is a legitimate inner product in some feature space the algorithm runs unchanged — but now nonlinearly in the original inputs.

Formally, fix a feature map $\varphi\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{F}$ into a Hilbert space and set $k(x,y)=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\rangle_{\mathcal{F}}$. The point of the trick is that we never build $\varphi$ — for the Gaussian kernel $\mathcal{F}$ is infinite-dimensional — yet we can compute $k$ in closed form.

Which $k$ are inner products?

A symmetric $k\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ is a positive-definite (Mercer) kernel iff for every finite set $x_1,\dots,x_n$ the Gram matrix

$K=[\,k(x_i,x_j)\,]_{i,j=1}^{n}\quad\text{is positive semidefinite:}\quad c^\top K c=\Big\lVert\textstyle\sum_i c_i\varphi(x_i)\Big\rVert^2\ge 0\ \ \forall c.$

The equality on the right is the whole story: $c^\top K c$ is the squared norm of $\sum_i c_i\varphi(x_i)$, which cannot be negative. Conversely (Moore–Aronszajn, §2) every PSD-Gram kernel is an inner product in a canonical feature space. PD kernels form a convex cone closed under sums, nonnegative scalings, and pointwise products.

The standard kernels

kernelformulafeature space
linear$k(x,y)=x^\top y$$\varphi(x)=x$ (no lift)
polynomial, degree $d$$k(x,y)=(x^\top y+c)^{d}$all monomials up to degree $d$
RBF / Gaussian$k(x,y)=\exp\!\big(-\lVert x-y\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)\big)$infinite-dimensional

The bandwidth $\sigma$ controls locality: small $\sigma$ makes $k(x,\cdot)$ a sharp bump (each point influences only its neighbors); large $\sigma$ makes it broad and smooth. Note $k(x,x)=1$ for the RBF, so its Gram matrix has unit diagonal.

Worked mini-example — the RBF Gram is PSD. For any points and coefficients, $c^\top K c=\sum_{i,j}c_i c_j e^{-\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. Bochner's theorem (§6) writes $e^{-\lVert x-y\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)}=\mathbb{E}_\omega[\cos(\omega^\top(x-y))]=\mathbb{E}_\omega[\langle z_\omega(x),z_\omega(y)\rangle]$ for explicit features $z_\omega$, so $c^\top K c=\mathbb{E}_\omega\lVert\sum_i c_i z_\omega(x_i)\rVert^2\ge 0$. The RBF is PD for every $\sigma > 0$.

Pick a kernel, set its parameters, and watch $k(x_0,\cdot)$ as a function of a moving point — and read off the smallest eigenvalue of a sample Gram matrix to confirm it stays $\ge 0$.

2. Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

The kernel trick promised a feature space; the RKHS makes it canonical. A reproducing kernel Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_k$ is a Hilbert space of functions $f\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}$ in which the evaluation functional $\delta_x\colon f\mapsto f(x)$ is bounded for every $x$ — small changes in $f$ (in norm) cannot produce wild swings in any pointwise value.

The reproducing property

Boundedness of $\delta_x$ is exactly the hypothesis of the Riesz representation theorem: a bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space is inner product against a unique vector. Call that vector $k(x,\cdot)$. Then for every $f$,

$f(x)=\delta_x(f)=\langle f,\,k(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k}\qquad\text{(reproducing property).}$

Apply it to $f=k(y,\cdot)$ to get $k(y,x)=\langle k(y,\cdot),k(x,\cdot)\rangle$ — the kernel is the Gram of its own representers, so $\varphi(x):=k(x,\cdot)$ is the canonical feature map and $k$ is positive-definite automatically.

Moore–Aronszajn. The correspondence runs both ways: every positive-definite kernel $k$ is the reproducing kernel of one and only one RKHS. Build it by taking finite combinations $f=\sum_i\alpha_i k(x_i,\cdot)$, defining $\langle\sum_i\alpha_i k(x_i,\cdot),\sum_j\beta_j k(y_j,\cdot)\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\beta_j k(x_i,y_j)$, and completing. PD-ness makes this a genuine inner product; uniqueness is then forced.

The norm penalizes wiggliness

For $f=\sum_i\alpha_i k(x_i,\cdot)$ the squared norm is $\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}^2=\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j k(x_i,x_j)=\alpha^\top K\alpha$. For the RBF kernel this norm grows with the high-frequency content of $f$, so $\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}$ is a smoothness penalty — the seed of regularization in §3. Not every function space is an RKHS: $L^2$ is not, because two functions equal almost everywhere have the same norm yet different pointwise values, so $\delta_x$ is not even well defined.

Worked mini-example. Place two RBF bumps with $\alpha=(1,-1)$ at $x_1=-1,x_2=1$, so $f=k(-1,\cdot)-k(1,\cdot)$. Evaluate at $x=0$ two ways: directly, $f(0)=k(-1,0)-k(1,0)=e^{-1/2}-e^{-1/2}=0$; or as the inner product $\langle f,k(0,\cdot)\rangle=\alpha_1 k(-1,0)+\alpha_2 k(1,0)=0$. They agree — that is the reproducing property.

3. The representer theorem

We want to learn a function $f\in\mathcal{H}_k$ from data by regularized empirical risk minimization:

$\min_{f\in\mathcal{H}_k}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} L\big(f(x_i),y_i\big)+\lambda\,\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_k}^{2},\qquad\lambda > 0.$

The search space $\mathcal{H}_k$ is infinite-dimensional. The miracle is that the solution is not.

Representer theorem (Kimeldorf–Wahba; Schölkopf–Herbrich–Smola). For any loss $L$ and any strictly increasing regularizer of $\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}$, a minimizer admits the finite representation $$f^\star=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\,k(x_i,\cdot),\qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{R}^n.$$ Proof sketch. Split $f=f_\parallel+f_\perp$, with $f_\parallel\in\mathrm{span}\{k(x_i,\cdot)\}$ and $f_\perp$ orthogonal to it. By the reproducing property $f(x_j)=\langle f,k(x_j,\cdot)\rangle=\langle f_\parallel,k(x_j,\cdot)\rangle=f_\parallel(x_j)$, so $f_\perp$ never affects the loss term. But $\lVert f\rVert^2=\lVert f_\parallel\rVert^2+\lVert f_\perp\rVert^2$, so any $f_\perp\neq 0$ strictly inflates the penalty. The optimum sets $f_\perp=0$. $\square$

An infinite-dimensional variational problem collapses to $n$ real numbers — and the data enter only through the Gram matrix $K$.

Kernel ridge regression

Take squared loss $L(f(x_i),y_i)=(f(x_i)-y_i)^2$. Writing $f=\sum_i\alpha_i k(x_i,\cdot)$ gives $f(x_j)=(K\alpha)_j$ and $\lVert f\rVert^2=\alpha^\top K\alpha$, so the objective is $\lVert K\alpha-y\rVert^2+\lambda\,\alpha^\top K\alpha$. The gradient vanishes at $K(K+\lambda I)\alpha=Ky$, and since $K\succeq 0$ the canonical solution is

$\boxed{\ \alpha=(K+\lambda I)^{-1}y\ }.$

The $\lambda I$ shift moves every eigenvalue of $K$ up by $\lambda > 0$, so the inverse always exists and is numerically stable even when $K$ is singular — the regularizer and the conditioning fix are the same act.

Worked mini-example. Two points with $k(x_1,x_2)=0.5$, $y=(1,0)^\top$, $\lambda=0.5$. Then $K+\lambda I=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1.5&0.5\\0.5&1.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, $\det=2$, inverse $\tfrac12\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1.5&-0.5\\-0.5&1.5\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, so $\alpha=(0.75,-0.25)^\top$. The negative $\alpha_2$ pulls the fit down near $x_2$ to honor its $0$ label against the smoothing pressure of the shared bump.

Slide $\lambda$ and watch the fit $f=\sum_i\alpha_i k(x_i,\cdot)$ go from interpolating wiggles ($\lambda\to 0$) to a flat, heavily smoothed curve (large $\lambda$). The coefficients are recomputed live via $(K+\lambda I)^{-1}y$.

4. Support vector machines

For binary labels $y_i\in\{\pm1\}$, the maximum-margin classifier seeks the separating hyperplane $w^\top\varphi(x)+b=0$ that stands as far as possible from both classes. The geometric margin of a separating $(w,b)$ is $1/\lVert w\rVert$, so maximizing margin is minimizing $\lVert w\rVert$. Allowing slack $\xi_i\ge 0$ for non-separable data gives the soft-margin primal:

$\min_{w,b,\xi}\ \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2+C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_i\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i\big(w^\top\varphi(x_i)+b\big)\ge 1-\xi_i,\ \ \xi_i\ge 0.$

The penalty $C$ trades margin width against violations: large $C$ punishes misclassification hard (narrow, hard margin); small $C$ tolerates it for a wider margin.

The dual — where the kernel enters

Forming the Lagrangian and eliminating $w,b,\xi$ via the KKT conditions yields the dual QP:

$\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_{i}\alpha_i-\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\,y_i y_j\,k(x_i,x_j)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad 0\le\alpha_i\le C,\ \ \sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0.$

The inputs appear only through $k(x_i,x_j)=\langle\varphi(x_i),\varphi(x_j)\rangle$. Swap the linear kernel for an RBF and the same QP returns a curved boundary — the kernel trick at work. The solution recovers $w=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\varphi(x_i)$, and the decision function

$g(x)=\operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\,k(x_i,x)+b\Big)$

also touches the data only through kernels.

Support vectors

Complementary slackness forces $\alpha_i=0$ for every point strictly outside the margin. The points with $\alpha_i > 0$ — on the margin or violating it — are the support vectors; they alone determine the boundary. Discard the rest and the solution is unchanged. The margin generalization bound (Rademacher complexity $\le BR/\sqrt m$) explains why a large-margin SVM generalizes even in a huge feature space: the rate is dimension-free.

Worked mini-example — a 2-point dual. One positive at $x_+$, one negative at $x_-$, hard margin ($C=\infty$). The constraint $\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$ forces $\alpha_+=\alpha_-=:\alpha$. The objective becomes $2\alpha-\tfrac12\alpha^2\big(k(x_+,x_+)+k(x_-,x_-)-2k(x_+,x_-)\big)=2\alpha-\tfrac12\alpha^2\lVert\varphi(x_+)-\varphi(x_-)\rVert^2$. Maximizing gives $\alpha=2/\lVert\varphi(x_+)-\varphi(x_-)\rVert^2$ and a margin of $1/\lVert w\rVert=\tfrac12\lVert\varphi(x_+)-\varphi(x_-)\rVert$ — exactly the perpendicular bisector, as it must be.

5. Gaussian processes

The same kernel that ran ridge regression also defines a prior over functions. A Gaussian process $f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)$ is a collection of random variables $\{f(x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}$ such that every finite subset is jointly Gaussian: $(f(x_1),\dots,f(x_n))\sim\mathcal{N}\big(m(X),\,K\big)$ with $K=[k(x_i,x_j)]$. The kernel is the covariance — it encodes how strongly nearby function values co-vary, hence the smoothness and length scale of the prior.

GP regression

Observe noisy data $y=f(X)+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)$. The joint distribution of the training targets $y$ and a test value $f(x_*)$ is Gaussian, and conditioning gives a closed-form Gaussian posterior with

$m(x_*)=k_*^\top(K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}y,\qquad \mathbb{V}[f(x_*)]=k(x_*,x_*)-k_*^\top(K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}k_*,$

where $k_*=(k(x_*,x_1),\dots,k(x_*,x_n))^\top$. The posterior mean is identical to kernel ridge regression with $\lambda=\sigma^2$ — the representer theorem in disguise. What GP regression adds for free is the predictive variance: it shrinks to near $\sigma^2$ at the data and swells back to the prior $k(x_*,x_*)$ far from any observation, giving calibrated error bars.

Worked mini-example. A single observation $y_1$ at $x_1$ with RBF kernel ($k(x,x)=1$), noise $\sigma^2$. Then $m(x_*)=\dfrac{k(x_*,x_1)}{1+\sigma^2}\,y_1$ and $\mathbb{V}[f(x_*)]=1-\dfrac{k(x_*,x_1)^2}{1+\sigma^2}$. At $x_*=x_1$ the mean is $y_1/(1+\sigma^2)$ (shrunk toward $0$ by the noise) and the variance is $1-1/(1+\sigma^2)=\sigma^2/(1+\sigma^2)$ — small. As $x_*$ moves away, $k(x_*,x_1)\to 0$, the mean reverts to the prior $0$, and the variance climbs back to $1$.

Place observations, slide the bandwidth and noise, and watch the posterior mean curve plus its $\pm 2$ standard-deviation band — and draw posterior sample functions to see the distribution itself.

6. Mercer's theorem & feature maps

We have used "$k(x,y)=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\rangle$ for some $\varphi$" repeatedly. Mercer's theorem makes $\varphi$ explicit by diagonalizing the kernel as an operator. Define the integral operator on $L^2(\mathcal{X},\mu)$ for a compact domain,

$(T_k f)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}} k(x,y)\,f(y)\,d\mu(y).$

For a continuous PD kernel, $T_k$ is compact, self-adjoint, and positive — exactly the hypotheses of the spectral theorem. So it has a discrete spectrum $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge 0$ with orthonormal eigenfunctions $\varphi_j$, and

$\boxed{\ k(x,y)=\sum_{j\ge 1}\lambda_j\,\varphi_j(x)\,\varphi_j(y)\ }\quad\text{(uniformly convergent).}$

The explicit feature map

Read off $\varphi(x)=\big(\sqrt{\lambda_1}\,\varphi_1(x),\sqrt{\lambda_2}\,\varphi_2(x),\dots\big)$, and indeed $\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\rangle=\sum_j\lambda_j\varphi_j(x)\varphi_j(y)=k(x,y)$. The eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ measure how much of the kernel lives along each eigen-direction; rapidly decaying $\lambda_j$ (as for the RBF) means the effective feature space is low-dimensional even though it is formally infinite — the basis of low-rank Nyström approximations.

Random Fourier features

For shift-invariant kernels $k(x,y)=\kappa(x-y)$, Bochner's theorem says $\kappa$ is the Fourier transform of a nonnegative measure $p(\omega)$. For the RBF, $p$ is itself Gaussian, and

$k(x,y)=\mathbb{E}_{\omega\sim p}\big[\cos(\omega^\top(x-y))\big]\approx\frac1D\sum_{r=1}^{D} z_{\omega_r}(x)\,z_{\omega_r}(y),\quad z_\omega(x)=\sqrt2\cos(\omega^\top x+b).$

Sampling $D$ frequencies gives an explicit, finite, $D$-dimensional feature map whose inner product is an unbiased estimate of $k$, converging as $D\to\infty$ at rate $O(1/\sqrt D)$. This is how kernel methods scale to millions of points: approximate the infinite feature map by a random finite one.

Worked mini-example. The eigenvalues of the RBF integral operator under a Gaussian input density decay geometrically, $\lambda_j\propto r^{j}$ with $r < 1$. Truncating after a handful of terms reconstructs $k$ to high accuracy — quantifying the "effectively low-dimensional" intuition. Random Fourier features reach the same accuracy by Monte Carlo instead of eigen-decomposition.

Slide the number of random features $D$ and watch the approximation $\frac1D\sum_r z_{\omega_r}(x)z_{\omega_r}(0)$ converge to the true RBF kernel $k(x,0)$.

Connections

Kernels are the bridge from the linear theory to its nonlinear, infinite-dimensional cousin — and yet the same RKHS view that explains classical kernel machines is now central to deep-learning theory: the neural tangent kernel shows that infinitely wide networks train as kernel regression, so the representer theorem and these generalization bounds resurface where you least expect them. Open frontiers include learning the kernel itself (multiple-kernel learning, deep kernels), scaling exact GP inference past the $O(n^3)$ cost of $(K+\sigma^2 I)^{-1}$, and understanding when the random-feature approximation suffices.

  • Statistical learning theory — empirical risk minimization — kernel methods are regularized ERM in an RKHS; the margin and Rademacher bounds there are exactly what makes large-margin SVMs generalize.
  • Functional analysis — Riesz representation — boundedness of evaluation plus Riesz is what conjures the reproducing representer $k(x,\cdot)$ in the first place.
  • Functional analysis — the spectral theorem — Mercer's expansion is the spectral decomposition of the kernel integral operator, supplying the explicit feature map.
  • Convex optimization — Lagrangian duality — the SVM dual QP and its support-vector sparsity come straight from KKT and complementary slackness.
  • Probability theory — the Gaussian — a Gaussian process is the function-space lift of the multivariate normal, with the kernel as covariance.

Forward to come: deep-learning theory (the neural tangent kernel and what replaces uniform convergence) and scalable inference (inducing-point and random-feature approximations) build directly on this RKHS foundation.
