Diffusion and score-based models

Modern generative diffusion turns sampling into geometry: a fixed process noises data into a Gaussian, and running it backward — guided only by the score $\nabla\log p_t$ — generates new samples from nothing but noise.

1. The score function

Generative diffusion is built on one object: the (Stein) score of a probability density,

$s(x)\ =\ \nabla_x\log p(x).$

It is a vector field on the data space, and at every point it tells you the direction of steepest increase of the log-density — it points uphill, toward where the data lives. Where $p$ has a mode the score vanishes; on the flanks it points back inward.

The normalizer disappears. Suppose you only know an unnormalized density $\tilde p$, with the true density $p=\tilde p/Z$ and $Z=\int\tilde p\,dx$ an intractable constant. Then $$\nabla_x\log p(x)\ =\ \nabla_x\big[\log\tilde p(x)-\log Z\big]\ =\ \nabla_x\log\tilde p(x),$$ because $\log Z$ does not depend on $x$. The score sees the shape of the density and is blind to its scale. This is what makes score-based methods work where likelihood-based ones stall: you never have to compute the partition function.

Worked mini-example: the Gaussian score is linear

For $p=N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\log p(x)=-\tfrac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}-\log(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, so

$s(x)\ =\ \nabla_x\log p(x)\ =\ -\,\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^2}.$

It is a straight line through $\mu$ with slope $-1/\sigma^2$: zero at the mean, negative to its right, positive to its left — always pushing back toward $\mu$. For a two-component mixture $p=w\,N(\mu_1,\sigma^2)+(1-w)\,N(\mu_2,\sigma^2)$ the score is $s(x)=p'(x)/p(x)$, a curve that vanishes at each mode and at the valley between them and points toward whichever mode is nearer.

2. Langevin dynamics

If the score is all you have, can you still sample from $p$? Yes — through Langevin dynamics, the overdamped stochastic differential equation

$dx_t\ =\ \tfrac12\,s(x_t)\,dt\ +\ dW_t\ =\ \tfrac12\,\nabla\log p(x_t)\,dt\ +\ dW_t,$

where $W_t$ is standard Brownian motion. The drift climbs the log-density; the noise keeps the walker exploring instead of collapsing onto a single mode.

$p$ is the stationary distribution. The Fokker–Planck (forward Kolmogorov) equation for drift $b=\tfrac12\nabla\log p$ and unit diffusion is $$\partial_t\rho\ =\ -\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(b\,\rho\big)+\tfrac12\nabla^2\rho.$$ Plug in the candidate $\rho=p$. Since $b\,p=\tfrac12 p\,\nabla\log p=\tfrac12\nabla p$, the right-hand side is $-\tfrac12\nabla^2 p+\tfrac12\nabla^2 p=0$. The drift exactly cancels the diffusion: $p$ is invariant, and under mild conditions the law of $x_t$ converges to $p$ from any start. The probability current vanishes, the hallmark of detailed balance.

So sampling reduces to integrating an SDE whose only input is the score — no $Z$, no inverse-CDF, no rejection. Discretizing with step $\Delta t$ gives the update $x\leftarrow x+\tfrac12 s(x)\,\Delta t+\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim N(0,1)$. This is exactly the inner loop that turns a learned score into samples.

Worked mini-example: crossing between two modes

Take the symmetric mixture $\tfrac12 N(-1.8,\,0.7^2)+\tfrac12 N(1.8,\,0.7^2)$. Pure gradient ascent on $\log p$ would trap a walker in whichever basin it started in. The Langevin noise term lets it occasionally hop the valley between the modes, so the long-run empirical distribution recovers both bumps in the right $50/50$ proportion — not just the nearest one.

3. The forward noising process

Diffusion models flip the problem around. Instead of sampling the data directly, they fix a forward process that gradually destroys the data — turning it into pure noise — and then learn to run that process in reverse. The forward process is a simple, data-independent SDE

$dx_t\ =\ f(x,t)\,dt\ +\ g(t)\,dW_t,\qquad x_0\sim p_{\text{data}},$

chosen so the marginal $p_t$ of $x_t$ flows from the data distribution at $t=0$ toward a tractable Gaussian as $t\to T$. The canonical choice is the variance-preserving (VP), or Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, process

$dx_t\ =\ -\tfrac12\,\beta(t)\,x_t\,dt\ +\ \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t.$

The perturbed marginal is a closed-form Gaussian. Because $f$ is linear in $x$ and $g$ depends only on $t$, the transition kernel stays Gaussian for all $t$. Writing $\bar\alpha_t=e^{-\int_0^t\beta(s)\,ds}$, $$x_t\mid x_0\ \sim\ N\!\Big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,I\Big).$$ As $t$ grows, $\bar\alpha_t\to0$: the mean shrinks to $0$ and the variance rises to $1$, so $p_t\to N(0,I)$ regardless of the data. The closed form is the engine of efficient training — you can jump straight to $x_t$ in one step, no simulation needed.

So the marginal $p_t$ is the data distribution convolved with a Gaussian of growing width (after a deterministic shrink): the modes blur and merge, drifting toward the origin, until nothing of the data structure survives. That $N(0,I)$ endpoint is the prior we will sample from when we generate.

Worked mini-example: a two-spike data distribution melting

Take data concentrated on two narrow spikes near $\pm 2.3$. With constant $\beta$, at time $t$ the mean factor is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}=e^{-t/2}$ and each spike has variance $(1-\bar\alpha_t)+\bar\alpha_t\sigma_0^2$. Early on, two distinct bumps remain; once $\bar\alpha_t\approx0$ they have merged into a single $N(0,1)$ bell. The transition is smooth and entirely determined by $\bar\alpha_t$.

4. The reverse-time SDE

The forward process is easy and useless on its own — it only destroys. The payoff is a remarkable theorem: every diffusion has a reverse-time companion, another SDE whose marginals are the forward ones run backward in time. This is Anderson's reverse-time SDE (1982). If the forward process is $dx=f(x,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t$, then

$dx_t\ =\ \big[\,f(x,t)\ -\ g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\,\big]\,dt\ +\ g(t)\,d\bar W_t,$

integrated backward from $t=T$ to $t=0$, where $\bar W_t$ is a Brownian motion running in reverse time.

Generation = solving the reverse SDE. Start with a draw from the easy prior $x_T\sim N(0,I)$ and integrate the reverse SDE down to $t=0$; the result is a sample from $p_{\text{data}}$. The only new ingredient relative to the forward process is the time-dependent score $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ — the score of each perturbed marginal — appearing in the drift correction $-\,g(t)^2\,\nabla\log p_t$. Note the weight: the score enters at the full $g(t)^2$ (the probability-flow ODE of §6 will use half this).

This is the whole architecture of a diffusion model. The forward process gives a curriculum of progressively noisier marginals $p_t$; the reverse SDE walks back up that curriculum, and the score $\nabla\log p_t$ is the compass at every noise level. Everything left to do is to obtain those scores — which is the training problem of §5.

Worked mini-example: reconstructing the two spikes

For our VP process with a Gaussian-mixture data law, the perturbed marginal $p_t$ stays a Gaussian mixture, so its score is available in closed form. Starting $4000$ particles at the $N(0,1)$ prior ($t=T=4$) and integrating the reverse SDE downward, the cloud splits and migrates back to the two data spikes — exactly inverting the melting of §3. Because the score is exact here, the recovered histogram matches the true $p_t$ at every stopping time.

5. Training: denoising score matching

The reverse SDE needs $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$, but $p_t$ — the data convolved with noise — is exactly what we do not know in closed form. The fix is to learn a score network $s_\theta(x,t)$. The naive objective, explicit score matching $\mathbb{E}\,\lVert s_\theta(x)-\nabla\log p_t(x)\rVert^2$, is useless because it contains the unknown target. The breakthrough is denoising score matching (Vincent, 2011).

Match the conditional score instead. Noise each datapoint, $\tilde x=x+\sigma\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\sim N(0,I)$, so the perturbation kernel is $q_\sigma(\tilde x\mid x)=N(\tilde x;x,\sigma^2 I)$. Minimize $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DSM}}(\theta)\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{x,\tilde x}\Big\lVert\, s_\theta(\tilde x)\ -\ \nabla_{\tilde x}\log q_\sigma(\tilde x\mid x)\,\Big\rVert^2 .$$ Vincent's theorem says this has the same minimizer as explicit score matching: the optimum is $s_\theta(\tilde x)=\nabla\log p_\sigma(\tilde x)$, the true marginal score. We replaced an intractable target by a per-sample target we can write down.

And that target is beautifully simple. For the Gaussian kernel, $\log q_\sigma(\tilde x\mid x)=-\lVert\tilde x-x\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)+\text{const}$, so

$\nabla_{\tilde x}\log q_\sigma(\tilde x\mid x)\ =\ -\,\frac{\tilde x-x}{\sigma^2}\ =\ -\,\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma}.$

Predicting the score is predicting the noise. Since the target is $-\varepsilon/\sigma$, you can reparameterize the network to output $\varepsilon_\theta(\tilde x,t)$ and train a plain mean-squared error $\mathbb{E}\lVert\varepsilon_\theta(\tilde x,t)-\varepsilon\rVert^2$. This $\varepsilon$-prediction form is what DDPM and its descendants actually optimize — and it is identical, up to the scale $-1/\sigma$, to learning the score.

Worked mini-example: fitting the mixture score

Generate clean samples from the mixture, noise each by $\sigma$, and regress a small basis onto the conditional targets $-(\tilde x-x)/\sigma^2$. The averaging over the random clean source $x$ behind each noised $\tilde x$ is exactly what makes the learned function approach the marginal score $\nabla\log p_\sigma$, not any single conditional. With enough basis functions the fit (cyan) tracks the true perturbed score (yellow).

6. The probability-flow ODE

The reverse SDE injects fresh noise at every step. Surprisingly, there is a fully deterministic sampler with the very same marginals — the probability-flow ODE

$\frac{dx_t}{dt}\ =\ f(x,t)\ -\ \tfrac12\,g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x).$

No $dW$ term; the score now enters at half the weight it has in the reverse SDE.

Same marginals, from the Fokker–Planck identity. The density $p_t$ of the forward SDE obeys the Fokker–Planck equation, which can be rewritten as a continuity equation $$\partial_t p_t\ +\ \nabla\!\cdot\!\big(p_t\,v_t\big)\ =\ 0,\qquad v_t(x)=f(x,t)-\tfrac12 g(t)^2\nabla\log p_t(x).$$ A continuity equation is precisely the statement that the deterministic flow $\dot x=v_t(x)$ transports the densities $p_t$. So the ODE and the SDE share every time-marginal $p_t$ — only their individual sample paths differ (smooth vs. jittery).

This unlocks two things the stochastic sampler cannot offer. First, a deterministic generator: fix the initial noise and you get a reproducible output, the basis of DDIM-style fast samplers and of latent interpolation. Second, an exact likelihood: the ODE is a continuous normalizing flow, so the instantaneous change-of-variables formula $\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t)=-\nabla\!\cdot v_t(x_t)$ integrates to a tractable $\log$-density — diffusion models can report exact bits-per-dim. Black-box adaptive ODE solvers also let you generate in far fewer function evaluations.

Worked mini-example: two paths to the same distribution

Run both samplers from the same noise draws at $t=T$. The reverse-SDE trajectories rattle stochastically as they descend; the PF-ODE trajectories are smooth deterministic curves. Yet the cloud of endpoints at $t=0$ has the same two-mode law under both — the marginals are identical even though no single path is. The fraction landing in the right-hand mode agrees between the two samplers up to sampling noise.

Connections

Score-based diffusion is among the most active areas in machine learning, and the mathematics is far from settled. Sharp convergence rates for the discretized reverse SDE, the geometry of the learned score field, optimal noise schedules, the few-step distillation of the PF-ODE, and the bridge to flow-matching and stochastic interpolants are all under active development. What surprises a newcomer is how little is needed: a single learned vector field, the score, drives an entire generative model — and the same object connects to estimation theory, statistical physics, and optimal transport.

  • Stochastic calculus — stochastic differential equations — the forward, reverse, and Langevin processes are all SDEs; existence/uniqueness and the OU solution come from here.
  • Stochastic calculus — Itô's formula — the Fokker–Planck/continuity machinery behind the reverse SDE and the probability-flow ODE rests on Itô calculus.
  • Probability theory — Brownian motion — the $dW_t$ driving every diffusion is the Wiener process, and its Gaussian increments make the VP marginals closed-form.
  • Statistical learning theory — empirical risk minimization — denoising score matching is an ERM problem: minimize an empirical mean-squared loss over a hypothesis class of score networks.
  • Deep learning theory — non-convex optimization — in practice the score $s_\theta$ is a deep network (a U-Net or transformer), trained by the same optimization story.
  • Information geometry — statistical manifolds — the score $\nabla_x\log p$ here and the Fisher score $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta$ there are siblings; both make the log-density's gradient the central object.
