Abelian varieties

A connected projective group variety. In dimension $1$ this is an elliptic curve; in dimension $g$ it is a $2g$-real-dimensional torus that happens to be cut out by polynomial equations. From complex tori and Riemann forms to Jacobians, Néron models, Mordell–Weil, and Faltings' finiteness theorem.

1. Definition and complex tori

An abelian variety over a field $k$ is a connected projective group variety $A/k$ — that is, a smooth projective variety equipped with $k$-morphisms $m\colon A\times A\to A$ and $\iota\colon A\to A$ and an identity section $e\colon\Spec k\to A$ that satisfy the group axioms. The dimension of $A$ as a variety is its dimension as an abelian variety; an elliptic curve is precisely an abelian variety of dimension $1$.

Rigidity lemma + theorem of the cube. Every morphism of abelian varieties is the composition of a translation and a homomorphism; consequently the group law on $A(\bar k)$ is automatically commutative. Theorem of the cube further forces the existence of an isomorphism $\mathcal{L}_{x+y+z}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{x+y}^{-1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{y+z}^{-1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{x+z}^{-1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_x\otimes\mathcal{L}_y\otimes\mathcal{L}_z\cong\mathcal{O}_A$ for every line bundle $\mathcal{L}$ on $A$ — the "cube" relation that controls the entire theory of line bundles, polarizations, and Néron models.

Over $\mathbb{C}$ there is a transparent description. Let $V=T_0A(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^g$ be the holomorphic tangent space at the identity and $\Lambda=\ker(\exp\colon V\to A(\mathbb{C}))$. Then

$$A(\mathbb{C})\;\cong\;V/\Lambda$$

as complex Lie groups, with $\Lambda$ a rank-$2g$ lattice in $V$. So as a manifold, $A(\mathbb{C})$ is a $2g$-real-dimensional torus.

Caution. Not every complex torus $V/\Lambda$ is algebraic. For $g=1$ every torus is an elliptic curve (Weierstrass $\wp$-theory); for $g\ge 2$, a generic lattice produces a complex torus with no nonconstant meromorphic functions, hence not embeddable in projective space. The condition that picks out the algebraic ones — a positive Riemann form — is the subject of §2.

2. Polarizations and the dual abelian variety

The line bundles on an abelian variety split into two pieces. The connected component $\mathrm{Pic}^0(A)$ — line bundles with trivial first Chern class — is itself an abelian variety, the dual:

$$A^\vee \;=\; \mathrm{Pic}^0(A).$$

It has the same dimension as $A$. The remaining component $\mathrm{Pic}(A)/\mathrm{Pic}^0(A) = \mathrm{NS}(A)$, the Néron–Severi group, is finitely generated and counts polarisation classes.

Polarization. A polarization on $A$ is an isogeny $\lambda\colon A\to A^\vee$ of the form $\lambda=\phi_{\mathcal{L}}$ where $\phi_{\mathcal{L}}(x)=t_x^*\mathcal{L}\otimes\mathcal{L}^{-1}\in\mathrm{Pic}^0(A)$ for an ample line bundle $\mathcal{L}$ on $A$. The polarization is principal iff $\lambda$ is an isomorphism — equivalently $\mathcal{L}$ has a single global section up to scaling, $h^0(A,\mathcal{L})=1$.

Over $\mathbb{C}$ this rephrases as classical data on the lattice. A line bundle $\mathcal{L}$ on $V/\Lambda$ corresponds (Appell–Humbert) to a Hermitian form $H$ on $V$ with $\operatorname{Im}H(\Lambda,\Lambda)\subset\mathbb{Z}$, plus a semicharacter on $\Lambda$. $\mathcal{L}$ is ample iff $H$ is positive definite. The pair $(\Lambda,H)$ is then a polarized lattice; the elementary divisors $(d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_g)$ of $\operatorname{Im}H|_\Lambda$ are the polarization type, and $\deg\lambda=(d_1\cdots d_g)^2$.

Why $g\ge 2$ generically fails. A random Hermitian form on $\mathbb{C}^g$ generically has irrational entries on a random lattice; the rationality condition $\operatorname{Im}H(\Lambda,\Lambda)\subset\mathbb{Z}$ cuts out a positive-codimension locus inside $\mathrm{Mat}_g(\mathbb{C})$. So the moduli space $\mathcal{A}_g$ of polarized abelian varieties has dimension $g(g+1)/2$ — much smaller than the $g^2$ dimensions of generic complex tori.

For $g=1$ every torus is an elliptic curve. For $g \ge 2$ the codimension grows quadratically — abelian varieties are special complex tori.

The Riemann–Roch theorem on $A$ gives the sharp dimension count: for a polarization $\mathcal{L}$ of type $(d_1,\dots,d_g)$,

$$h^0(A,\mathcal{L}) \;=\; d_1 d_2 \cdots d_g.$$

So principal polarizations are exactly those with a one-dimensional space of global sections — and a single (up to scaling) section vanishes on a divisor $\Theta$, the theta divisor. The pair $(A,\Theta)$ recovers the polarized abelian variety up to isomorphism.

3. Isogenies, Tate modules, and Galois action

An isogeny is a surjective morphism $\phi\colon A\to B$ of abelian varieties of the same dimension, with finite kernel. The most important examples are the multiplication-by-$n$ maps:

$$[n]\colon A\longrightarrow A,\qquad \deg[n] \;=\; n^{2g}.$$

When $n$ is invertible in $k$ (e.g. for any $n$ when $k$ has characteristic $0$), $[n]$ is étale and

$$A[n](\bar k) \;=\; \ker[n] \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{2g}.$$

So $A$ has, geometrically, $n^{2g}$ points of order dividing $n$. In characteristic $p$ dividing $n$, $[p]$ is purely inseparable on a piece, and $A[p^\infty]$ acquires a Newton-polygon classification (ordinary, supersingular, etc.) — a story we leave to deformation theory.

Tate module. Fix a prime $\ell\ne\mathrm{char}\,k$. Pass to the inverse limit: $$T_\ell A \;=\; \varprojlim_n A[\ell^n](\bar k) \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}_\ell^{2g}.$$ $T_\ell A$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$-module of rank $2g$, and the absolute Galois group $G_k=\Gal(\bar k/k)$ acts continuously and $\mathbb{Z}_\ell$-linearly. We get a continuous representation $$\rho_{A,\ell}\colon G_k\longrightarrow\mathrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_\ell).$$

For an elliptic curve ($g=1$) this is the familiar $2$-dimensional $\ell$-adic Galois representation; for higher $g$ it is its $2g$-dimensional analogue. The Weil pairing makes $\rho_{A,\ell}$ symplectic (after a polarization): it factors through $\mathrm{GSp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$.

Frobenius eigenvalues — the Weil conjectures (proved by Weil, 1948). Let $A/\mathbb{F}_q$ be an abelian variety of dimension $g$ and $\mathrm{Frob}_q\in G_{\mathbb{F}_q}$ the geometric Frobenius. The characteristic polynomial $$P_A(T) \;=\; \det\!\big(T - \mathrm{Frob}_q\bigm| T_\ell A\otimes\mathbb{Q}_\ell\big)\;\in\;\mathbb{Z}[T]$$ has degree $2g$, integer coefficients independent of $\ell$, and roots $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_{2g}$ that are Weil $q$-numbers: $$|\alpha_i| \;=\; \sqrt{q}\quad\text{in every embedding }\bar{\mathbb{Q}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}.$$ The number of points satisfies $|A(\mathbb{F}_{q^n})|=\prod_i(1-\alpha_i^n)$, so $|A(\mathbb{F}_q)|=P_A(1)$.

Three deep theorems pin the structure down further. Tate's isogeny conjecture (proved by Tate over finite fields in 1966 and by Faltings over number fields in 1983): the natural map

$$\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_\ell \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{Hom}_{G_k}(T_\ell A, T_\ell B)$$

is an isomorphism. So the Galois module $T_\ell A$ recovers the abelian variety up to isogeny. Semisimplicity: the action on $V_\ell A=T_\ell A\otimes\mathbb{Q}_\ell$ is semisimple. Independence of $\ell$: the trace of $\mathrm{Frob}_v$ at any unramified place of a number field is the same integer for all good $\ell\ne\ell(v)$.

4. Jacobians of curves

Where do principally polarized abelian varieties come from in the wild? The first, and historically the only, examples are Jacobians.

Jacobian of a curve. Let $C$ be a smooth projective curve of genus $g$ over a field $k$. The Picard variety $$\mathrm{Jac}(C) \;=\; \mathrm{Pic}^0(C)$$ is a $g$-dimensional abelian variety over $k$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $$\mathrm{Jac}(C) \;\cong\; H^0(C,\Omega^1_{C})^\ast / H_1(C,\mathbb{Z}),$$ i.e. holomorphic $1$-forms modulo periods.

The integration map gives the lattice: a basis $\omega_1,\dots,\omega_g$ of $H^0(C,\Omega^1)$ and a symplectic basis $a_1,\dots,a_g,b_1,\dots,b_g$ of $H_1(C,\mathbb{Z})$ produce a $g\times 2g$ period matrix

$$\Omega \;=\; \big(\textstyle\int_{a_j}\omega_i \;\big|\; \int_{b_j}\omega_i\big).$$

Choosing the $a$-periods to be the identity (always possible) gives $\Omega=(I_g\mid\tau)$ with $\tau\in\mathfrak{H}_g$ the Siegel upper half-space — exactly the polarised period matrices of §1.

Abel–Jacobi map. Fix a basepoint $p_0\in C$. The map $$\mathrm{AJ}\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Jac}(C),\qquad p\mapsto[p-p_0]$$ is a closed immersion (for $g\ge 1$) and embeds the curve in its Jacobian. Symmetric powers extend this to $\mathrm{Sym}^d(C)\to\mathrm{Pic}^d(C)$. The image of $\mathrm{Sym}^{g-1}(C)\to\mathrm{Pic}^{g-1}(C)\cong\mathrm{Jac}(C)$ (after a translation) is the theta divisor $\Theta\subset\mathrm{Jac}(C)$, providing the canonical principal polarization.

The theta divisor distinguishes Jacobians among all p.p.a.v.'s.

Torelli theorem (Torelli 1913, Andreotti 1958, Matsusaka 1958). The map $$\{\text{smooth projective curves of genus }g\}\,\big/\,\mathrm{iso}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathcal{A}_g(\mathbb{C}),\quad C\mapsto(\mathrm{Jac}(C),\Theta)$$ is injective. So the polarized Jacobian determines the curve up to isomorphism.

Counting dimensions reveals the Schottky problem. The image — the Jacobian locus $\mathcal{J}_g\subset\mathcal{A}_g$ — has dimension

  • $\dim\mathcal{M}_g = 3g-3$ for $g\ge 2$ (moduli of curves),
  • $\dim\mathcal{A}_g = g(g+1)/2$ (moduli of p.p.a.v.'s).

For $g=2,3$ the two coincide: every p.p.a.v. is a Jacobian. For $g=4$, $3g-3=9$ vs. $g(g+1)/2=10$ — codimension $1$ — and the Schottky equation cuts out the closure of $\mathcal{J}_4$. For $g\ge 5$ the codimension grows quadratically, and characterising $\mathcal{J}_g$ remains an active problem (Riemann's theta-null relations, Fay's identities, the Buser–Sarnak–Krichever programs).

5. Néron models and reduction

Now the arithmetic side. Let $K$ be a discretely-valued field with valuation ring $\mathcal{O}_K$ and residue field $k$. Given an abelian variety $A/K$, can we extend it to a smooth $\mathcal{O}_K$-scheme? The naive idea — clear denominators in defining equations — usually loses smoothness at the closed point.

Néron's theorem (1964). There is a unique smooth separated $\mathcal{O}_K$-group scheme $\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{O}_K$ of finite type — the Néron model of $A$ — with generic fibre $\mathcal{A}_K=A$ and the following Néron mapping property: for every smooth $\mathcal{O}_K$-scheme $T$, every $K$-morphism $T_K\to A$ extends uniquely to a morphism $T\to\mathcal{A}$. Consequently $\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{O}_K)=A(K)$.

The Néron model is rarely projective; it gives up properness in exchange for keeping smoothness. The interesting object is its special fibre.

Decompose $\mathcal{A}_k=\mathcal{A}_k^0\sqcup(\mathcal{A}_k\setminus\mathcal{A}_k^0)$ into the connected component of identity and its complement. By a structure theorem of Chevalley (as refined by Grothendieck and Raynaud):

$$0\;\longrightarrow\;U\;\longrightarrow\;\mathcal{A}_k^0\;\longrightarrow\;G\;\longrightarrow\;0$$

with $U$ unipotent (a power of $\mathbb{G}_a$) and $G$ a semiabelian variety — that is, $G$ fits into

$$0\;\longrightarrow\;T\;\longrightarrow\;G\;\longrightarrow\;B\;\longrightarrow\;0$$

with $T$ a torus and $B$ an abelian variety over $k$. The component group

$$\Phi \;=\; \mathcal{A}_k/\mathcal{A}_k^0$$

is a finite étale group scheme over $k$.

Grothendieck's semistable reduction theorem (SGA 7, 1972). For any $A/K$, there is a finite separable extension $K'/K$ such that $A_{K'}$ has semistable reduction. Equivalently: after extending the base, the unipotent rank vanishes.

The Néron–Ogg–Shafarevich criterion connects reduction to Galois representations: $A/K$ has good reduction at $v$ iff the inertia group $I_v$ acts trivially on $T_\ell A$ for some (equivalently every) $\ell\ne\mathrm{char}\,k(v)$. So the local Galois representation knows the reduction type — and the conductor exponent $f_v$ measures the inertia invariants:

$$f_v \;=\; \dim T_\ell A \;-\; \dim(T_\ell A)^{I_v} \;+\; (\text{wild part}).$$

For elliptic curves the resulting Kodaira–Néron classification (Kodaira 1963, Néron 1964, Tate's algorithm 1975) lists exactly $10$ reduction types: $I_0$ (good), $I_n$ ($n\ge 1$, multiplicative), and seven additive types $II,III,IV,I_0^*,I_n^*,IV^*,III^*,II^*$. For $g\ge 2$ the analogous classification is more elaborate but follows the same pattern via the toric / unipotent / abelian rank triple.

6. Mordell–Weil and Faltings finiteness

The two foundational finiteness theorems of arithmetic on abelian varieties.

Mordell–Weil theorem (Mordell 1922 for elliptic curves over $\mathbb{Q}$; Weil 1929 in general). Let $A$ be an abelian variety over a number field $K$. Then $A(K)$ is a finitely generated abelian group: $$A(K) \;\cong\; \mathbb{Z}^r \;\oplus\; A(K)_{\mathrm{tors}}$$ with $r=\mathrm{rank}\,A(K)$ finite and $A(K)_{\mathrm{tors}}$ finite.

The proof has two halves, both visible already in the elliptic case.

  • Weak Mordell–Weil: $A(K)/nA(K)$ is finite. The Kummer sequence $0\to A[n]\to A\xrightarrow{[n]}A\to 0$ gives an injection $A(K)/nA(K)\hookrightarrow H^1(G_K,A[n])$; selmer-style finite-ramification arguments bound the right-hand side using the unit group and class group of $K(A[n])$.
  • Descent via heights: the Néron–Tate canonical height $\hat h\colon A(\bar K)\to\mathbb{R}$ is a positive-semidefinite quadratic form vanishing exactly on torsion. Coset representatives $Q_1,\dots,Q_m$ for $A(K)/nA(K)$ together with the inequality $\hat h(P')\le\tfrac{1}{n^2}\hat h(P)+C$ (where $P=nP'+Q_i$) iterate every point into a Northcott-finite ball.
Faltings' theorem (1983, Endlichkeitssätze für abelsche Varietäten). Fix a number field $K$, an integer $g\ge 1$, and a finite set $S$ of places of $K$. Then there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of $g$-dimensional principally polarized abelian varieties over $K$ with good reduction outside $S$.

Faltings' proof simultaneously establishes three deep statements that had been open for decades:

  • Tate's isogeny conjecture over number fields: $\mathrm{Hom}(A,B)\otimes\mathbb{Z}_\ell\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{G_K}(T_\ell A, T_\ell B)$.
  • Semisimplicity of $V_\ell A=T_\ell A\otimes\mathbb{Q}_\ell$ as a $G_K$-representation.
  • Shafarevich's conjecture in the form stated above (only finitely many AVs with controlled reduction).
Mordell's conjecture (Faltings 1983). Let $C$ be a smooth projective curve of genus $g\ge 2$ over a number field $K$. Then $C(K)$ is finite.

The bridge from Faltings' Shafarevich-style theorem to Mordell is the Kodaira–Parshin construction: a $K$-rational point $P\in C(K)$ produces an abelian variety $A_P$ (Jacobian of an étale cover of $C$ branched at $P$) of fixed dimension and bounded reduction. Faltings' finiteness for abelian varieties bounds the $A_P$, hence bounds the points $P$.

Beyond Faltings, several sharper finiteness statements remain conjectural. Effective Mordell (the bound on $|C(K)|$ Faltings produces is ineffective; Vojta–Bombieri sharpened the constants but no algorithm enumerates $C(K)$ in general). Uniform Mordell (Mazur conjectured $|C(K)|\le N(g,K)$; Dimitrov–Gao–Habegger 2021 proved $|C(K)|\le c(g)\cdot 7^{r_K+\mathrm{rank}\,\mathrm{Jac}(C)(K)}$). Lang's conjecture: rational points on a variety of general type are not Zariski dense — implies uniform Mordell and is wide open.

Connections

Abelian varieties sit at the intersection of nearly every advanced topic in arithmetic geometry. The open frontiers a beginner most often meets are the Schottky problem (which p.p.a.v.'s are Jacobians, sharp for $g\ge 4$), the BSD conjecture (the analytic rank of $L(A,s)$ controls the Mordell–Weil rank — known only in restricted cases via Iwasawa-theoretic Euler systems), uniform/effective Mordell (the bound on $|C(K)|$ remains qualitatively Faltings-style), and $p$-adic uniformization in characteristic $p$ (Drinfeld and Mumford curves, $p$-divisible groups, the Newton stratification of $\mathcal{A}_g$ in characteristic $p$).

  • Elliptic curves — the dimension-$1$ case; everything here specialises and many statements ($g=1$) become classical.
  • Higher-genus curves — Jacobians live here; the Abel–Jacobi map and Torelli are the bridge to §4.
  • Galois representations — the $\ell$-adic Galois representation on $T_\ell A$ is the Tate module of §3, with Frobenius eigenvalues controlled by Weil.
  • Heights — Néron–Tate canonical height — runs the descent step in Mordell–Weil and produces the regulator that appears in BSD.
  • Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer — the conjectural rank formula generalises from elliptic curves to abelian varieties; Iwasawa theory provides the strongest unconditional partial results.
  • Moduli spaces — $\mathcal{A}_g$ is the moduli space of principally polarized abelian varieties, embedding the Jacobian locus $\mathcal{J}_g$ via Torelli.
  • Theta functions — the global sections of the line bundle giving the polarization; theta-null relations encode the polarization data.
