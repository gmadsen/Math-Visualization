Statistical learning theory

When does minimizing error on the data you have control error on the data you haven't seen? The geometry of a hypothesis class — counted by VC dimension and Rademacher complexity — answers exactly.

1. Empirical risk minimization

A learning problem fixes an unknown distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ and a loss $\ell(\hat y, y)\ge 0$. The thing we actually want to make small is the population risk (or expected risk) of a predictor $h\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}$:

$R(h)=\mathbb{E}_{(X,Y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\ell(h(X),Y)\big].$

We never see $\mathcal{D}$ — only a sample $S=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{m}$ drawn i.i.d. from it. So we minimize a proxy, the empirical risk:

$\widehat R(h)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell(h(x_i),y_i).$

The empirical risk minimizer over a chosen hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$ is $h_S=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\widehat R(h)$. The best any predictor could do — over all measurable functions, ignoring $\mathcal{H}$ — is achieved by the Bayes predictor. For squared loss it is the regression function $h^\star(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]$; for $0$–$1$ loss in binary classification it is the plug-in rule $h^\star(x)=\mathbf{1}[\,\eta(x)\ge\tfrac12\,]$ with $\eta(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid X=x)$, whose risk is the irreducible Bayes risk $R^\star$.

The two sources of error

Write $h^\star_{\mathcal{H}}=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R(h)$ for the best predictor in the class. Then the excess risk of the learned $h_S$ splits cleanly:

$\underbrace{R(h_S)-R^\star}_{\text{excess risk}}=\underbrace{\big(R(h_S)-R(h^\star_{\mathcal{H}})\big)}_{\text{estimation error}}+\underbrace{\big(R(h^\star_{\mathcal{H}})-R^\star\big)}_{\text{approximation error}}.$

Approximation error shrinks as $\mathcal{H}$ grows richer; estimation error grows, because a richer class overfits the finite sample. The quantity we can measure on held-out data tracks the gap between the proxy and the truth — the generalization gap $R(h_S)-\widehat R(h_S)$. ERM is honest only when this gap is small.

Worked mini-example. Take $\mathcal{X}=[0,1]$, label $Y=\mathbf{1}[X\ge\tfrac12]$ deterministically, so $R^\star=0$. With $\mathcal{H}$ = degree-$d$ polynomial thresholds and $m=20$ noisy points, the degree-$1$ class has $\widehat R\approx 0.1$ but small generalization gap; the degree-$15$ class drives $\widehat R\to 0$ by wiggling through every point, yet $R(h_S)$ climbs to $\approx 0.3$. Empirical risk fell while true risk rose: textbook overfitting.

2. PAC learning & sample complexity

How much data do we need? The Probably Approximately Correct framework (Valiant, 1984) makes the question precise. A class $\mathcal{H}$ is PAC-learnable if there is a learner and a function $m_{\mathcal{H}}(\varepsilon,\delta)$ such that for every distribution $\mathcal{D}$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ over a sample of size $m\ge m_{\mathcal{H}}(\varepsilon,\delta)$, the output $h_S$ satisfies $R(h_S)\le \min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R(h)+\varepsilon$. "Approximately" is $\varepsilon$; "probably" is $1-\delta$.

Two regimes:

  • Realizable: some $h\in\mathcal{H}$ has $R(h)=0$ (the target is in the class, noise-free). Here we ask only that $R(h_S)\le\varepsilon$.
  • Agnostic: no such guarantee — we compete with the best-in-class $\min_{h}R(h)$, which may be positive.

Hoeffding's inequality — the engine

If $Z_1,\dots,Z_m$ are i.i.d. in $[0,1]$ with mean $\mu$, then for every $t>0$,

$\mathbb{P}\!\left(\Big|\tfrac1m\textstyle\sum_i Z_i-\mu\Big|>t\right)\le 2e^{-2mt^2}.$

Fix one hypothesis $h$: its losses $\ell(h(x_i),y_i)\in[0,1]$ are i.i.d. with mean $R(h)$, so $\widehat R(h)$ concentrates around $R(h)$ at the sub-Gaussian rate $e^{-2mt^2}$. The deviation scale is $\sqrt{1/m}$.

The finite-class bound

Agnostic, $|\mathcal{H}|<\infty$. Apply Hoeffding to each $h$ at level $t=\varepsilon/2$ and union-bound over the class: $\mathbb{P}(\exists h:\ |\widehat R(h)-R(h)|>\tfrac\varepsilon2)\le 2|\mathcal{H}|e^{-m\varepsilon^2/2}$. Setting this to $\delta$ and solving gives the sample complexity

$m\ \ge\ \dfrac{2}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(\ln|\mathcal{H}|+\ln\tfrac{2}{\delta}\Big)\qquad\text{(agnostic).}$

Realizable the rate is faster — only the consistent hypotheses survive, so a single-sided multiplicative-Chernoff argument gives

$m\ \ge\ \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\ln|\mathcal{H}|+\ln\tfrac{1}{\delta}\Big)\qquad\text{(realizable).}$

The dependence is $1/\varepsilon$ realizable versus $1/\varepsilon^{2}$ agnostic — the price of noise. Both grow only as $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$: doubling the number of bits to describe a hypothesis only adds a constant per bit.

Worked mini-example. A class of $|\mathcal{H}|=2^{20}\approx 10^6$ hypotheses (20-bit descriptions), target accuracy $\varepsilon=0.1$, confidence $\delta=0.05$. Realizable: $m\ge\tfrac1{0.1}(20\ln 2+\ln 20)\approx 10\,(13.86+3.00)=169$ samples. Agnostic: $m\ge\tfrac2{0.01}(13.86+\ln 40)\approx 200\,(13.86+3.69)=3510$ samples. Same class, $\sim20\times$ more data once noise enters.

3. VC dimension & shattering

The finite-class bound is useless for infinite $\mathcal{H}$ — half-planes in $\mathbb{R}^2$ form an uncountable class with $\ln|\mathcal{H}|=\infty$. Yet such classes are learnable. The fix is to count not hypotheses but the distinct behaviors the class can exhibit on a finite sample.

Fix points $x_1,\dots,x_m$. A dichotomy is a labeling $(h(x_1),\dots,h(x_m))\in\{0,1\}^m$ realized by some $h\in\mathcal{H}$. The growth function counts them:

$\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m)=\max_{x_1,\dots,x_m}\big|\{(h(x_1),\dots,h(x_m)):h\in\mathcal{H}\}\big|\ \le\ 2^m.$

A set of $m$ points is shattered if $\mathcal{H}$ realizes all $2^m$ labelings on it. The VC dimension $\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})$ is the size of the largest shattered set (possibly $\infty$).

Sauer–Shelah lemma

The crucial dichotomy: either $\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m)=2^m$ for all $m$ (infinite VC dim), or it is eventually polynomial. If $\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})=d<\infty$ then

$\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m)\ \le\ \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{m}{i}\ \le\ \Big(\frac{em}{d}\Big)^{d}\quad(m\ge d).$

So a finite VC dimension collapses an exponential count to a degree-$d$ polynomial — the discrete miracle that makes infinite classes learnable. The effective "$\ln|\mathcal{H}|$" becomes $\ln\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(m)\approx d\ln(em/d)$.

Worked mini-example (thresholds, VC = 1). One point $\{x_1\}$ is shattered: $h(x_1)=0$ via $a>x_1$ and $h(x_1)=1$ via $a\le x_1$. But two points $x_1 \lt x_2$ cannot get the labeling $(1,0)$ — a threshold that fires on the smaller point necessarily fires on the larger. So no $2$-set is shattered: $\mathrm{VC}=1$. For axis-aligned rectangles, four points in a "diamond" (top/bottom/left/right extremes) are shattered, but no $5$ points can be — one always lies in the convex hull of the bounding box of the others, so it can't be excluded while keeping them.

4. Uniform convergence & the fundamental theorem

Hoeffding controls a single hypothesis. ERM needs more: because $h_S$ is chosen after seeing the data, its empirical risk is biased downward. We need the gap small simultaneously for every $h$. A class has the uniform convergence property if for every $\varepsilon,\delta$ there is $m$ so that with probability $\ge1-\delta$,

$\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\big|R(h)-\widehat R(h)\big|\le\varepsilon.$

Uniform convergence is exactly what makes ERM safe: if every empirical risk is within $\varepsilon$ of its true value, then $R(h_S)\le\widehat R(h_S)+\varepsilon\le\widehat R(h^\star_{\mathcal{H}})+\varepsilon\le R(h^\star_{\mathcal{H}})+2\varepsilon$.

The VC generalization bound

Replacing $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$ by $\ln\Pi_{\mathcal{H}}(2m)\lesssim d\ln(m/d)$ in the union bound (and handling the data-dependence by the symmetrization / ghost-sample trick) yields: with probability $\ge1-\delta$, for all $h\in\mathcal{H}$,

$R(h)\ \le\ \widehat R(h)+O\!\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{d\ln(m/d)+\ln(1/\delta)}{m}}\right).$

The estimation error decays like $\sqrt{d/m}$: more data helps as $1/\sqrt m$, more capacity hurts as $\sqrt d$. Inverting gives the agnostic sample complexity $m=\Theta\!\big((d+\ln(1/\delta))/\varepsilon^{2}\big)$ (and $\Theta((d+\ln(1/\delta))/\varepsilon)$ realizable).

The Fundamental Theorem of Statistical Learning. For a class $\mathcal{H}$ of binary classifiers under $0$–$1$ loss, the following are equivalent:
  • $\mathcal{H}$ has the uniform convergence property;
  • $\mathcal{H}$ is agnostic-PAC-learnable;
  • $\mathcal{H}$ is PAC-learnable (realizable);
  • any ERM rule is a successful learner for $\mathcal{H}$;
  • $\mathcal{H}$ has finite VC dimension.
A single integer $d=\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal{H})$ decides learnability and pins the sample complexity to $\Theta((d+\ln(1/\delta))/\varepsilon^2)$.
Worked mini-example. Linear classifiers in $\mathbb{R}^{10}$ have $d=11$. For $\varepsilon=0.1,\ \delta=0.05$ the theorem promises learnability with $m=\Theta((11+\ln 20)/0.01)\approx \Theta(1400)$ samples — finite, distribution-free, no matter how adversarial $\mathcal{D}$ is. Contrast a class of infinite VC dimension (e.g. all finite unions of intervals): no finite sample suffices uniformly, and ERM can be made to fail.

5. Rademacher complexity & margins

VC dimension is combinatorial and distribution-free — which is also its weakness: it ignores the data distribution and is hard to extend past binary labels. Rademacher complexity is the data-dependent, real-valued upgrade. Let $\sigma=(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m)$ be i.i.d. Rademacher signs ($\pm1$ each with probability $\tfrac12$). The empirical Rademacher complexity of a function class $\mathcal{F}$ on a sample $S=(x_1,\dots,x_m)$ is

$\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{F})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\!\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_i f(x_i)\right].$

It measures how well the class can correlate with pure noise: a rich class fits random signs (large $\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S$), a rigid class cannot. This is precisely capacity.

The Rademacher generalization bound

Via McDiarmid's bounded-differences inequality (changing one sample point moves $\sup_h(R-\widehat R)$ by at most $1/m$) plus symmetrization, with probability $\ge1-\delta$, for all $h\in\mathcal{H}$ (loss in $[0,1]$):

$R(h)\ \le\ \widehat R(h)+2\,\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\ell\circ\mathcal{H})+3\sqrt{\dfrac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2m}}.$

The factor of $2$ in front of $\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S$ is the symmetrization constant. Talagrand's contraction lemma lets us peel the loss off: if $\ell(\cdot,y)$ is $L$-Lipschitz then $\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\ell\circ\mathcal{H})\le L\,\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{H})$, so we only ever need to bound the complexity of the raw class.

Margins

For a linear class with $\|w\|\le B$ on inputs with $\|x\|\le R$, Cauchy–Schwarz and Jensen give $\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{H})\le BR/\sqrt m$ — dimension-free. Combined with a margin loss, this yields the margin bound: a classifier achieving margin $\gamma$ on the training set generalizes at rate $\tilde O(BR/(\gamma\sqrt m))$, independent of the ambient dimension. This is the theory behind support-vector machines and AdaBoost: confident, large-margin fits generalize even when the parameter count is huge.

Worked mini-example. The full class of $\pm1$-valued functions on $m$ fixed points has $\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S=1$: it realizes every sign pattern, so $\sup_f\frac1m\sum\sigma_i f(x_i)=\frac1m\sum|\sigma_i|=1$ for every $\sigma$. A single constant function $f\equiv1$ has $\widehat{\mathfrak{R}}_S=\mathbb{E}_\sigma|\frac1m\sum\sigma_i|=\Theta(1/\sqrt m)\to0$. Capacity is the gap between these.

6. Bias–variance, regularization & SRM

For squared loss there is a second, sharper decomposition. Fix $x$, let the data be $y=f(x)+\text{noise}$ with $\mathrm{Var}(\text{noise})=\sigma^2$, and let $\hat f_S(x)$ be the prediction of a learner trained on a random sample $S$. Averaging over $S$:

$\mathbb{E}_S\big[(\hat f_S(x)-y)^2\big]=\underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}_S[\hat f_S(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}+\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_S\!\big(\hat f_S(x)\big)}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}.$

Bias is systematic error from a class too rigid to represent $f$ (the approximation error of §1); variance is sensitivity to which particular sample you drew (estimation error). Noise $\sigma^2$ is irreducible — the Bayes floor. As complexity rises, bias falls and variance climbs: the total test error traces the classic U-shape, with an optimal complexity at the bottom.

Modern caveat — double descent. The clean U is the under-parameterized story. As models pass the interpolation threshold (enough capacity to fit the data exactly), test error can fall again — the "double descent" curve. The classical bias–variance tradeoff governs the first descent; implicit regularization of overparameterized fits governs the second.

Regularization as constrained ERM

Rather than picking a class size by hand, add a penalty: $\min_h\ \widehat R(h)+\lambda\,\Omega(h)$. By Lagrangian duality this equals constrained ERM, $\min\{\widehat R(h):\Omega(h)\le t\}$. Two staples:

  • Ridge ($\ell_2$): $\Omega(w)=\|w\|_2^2$. Shrinks coefficients smoothly toward $0$ — trades a little bias for a large variance reduction.
  • Lasso ($\ell_1$): $\Omega(w)=\|w\|_1$. The polytope corners of the $\ell_1$ ball force exact zeros — sparse, selective models.

Increasing $\lambda$ moves you left along the complexity axis: more bias, less variance. Regularization is a knob on the same tradeoff.

Structural risk minimization

SRM (Vapnik) formalizes the choice. Nest the hypothesis classes $\mathcal{H}_1\subset\mathcal{H}_2\subset\cdots$ by capacity (e.g. by VC dimension $d_k$), and minimize the guaranteed risk — empirical risk plus the §4 complexity penalty:

$h_{\mathrm{SRM}}=\arg\min_{k}\ \arg\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_k}\ \widehat R(h)+\mathrm{pen}(d_k,m,\delta).$

The penalty makes the method automatically balance fit against capacity — the principled version of the U-curve's minimum.

Worked mini-example. Polynomial regression on $m=30$ points from $f(x)=\sin(2\pi x)+\text{noise}$, $\sigma=0.2$. Degree $1$: high bias ($\approx0.25$), tiny variance — underfits the sine. Degree $9$: near-zero bias, variance $\approx0.4$ — wild between data points. Degree $3$ sits at the U's bottom, total error $\approx\sigma^2+0.03$. Ridge with moderate $\lambda$ lets even degree $9$ behave like an effective degree $\sim3$ by shrinking the high-order coefficients.

Connections

The classical theory above is a complete story for finite-VC classes — and yet it spectacularly fails to explain why hugely overparameterized neural networks generalize. Deep nets have effectively infinite VC dimension and can fit pure noise, so the uniform-convergence bounds are vacuous; the modern frontier (implicit regularization, the double-descent curve, norm- and margin-based bounds, the neural-tangent-kernel regime) is an active attempt to find the right capacity measure. Kernel methods reframe the linear theory of §5 in a feature Hilbert space, and reinforcement learning extends the i.i.d. story to dependent data.

  • High-dimensional geometry — concentration of measure — Talagrand's inequality and McDiarmid bounded differences are the probabilistic engine behind every uniform-convergence and Rademacher bound here.
  • Large deviations — Cramér's theorem — the exponential tail rate that sharpens Hoeffding and quantifies how fast empirical risk concentrates.
  • Probability theory — the law of large numbers — generalization is uniform LLN: empirical risk converges to population risk simultaneously over the whole class.
  • Mathematical statistics — estimators — ERM is M-estimation; bias, variance, and consistency are the statistician's language for the same tradeoffs.
  • Convex optimization — subgradients — convex surrogate losses (hinge, logistic) and $\ell_1/\ell_2$ regularization make ERM a tractable convex program.
  • Information theory — KL divergence — PAC-Bayes bounds and the MDL principle recast capacity control as a KL penalty between posterior and prior over hypotheses.

Forward to come: kernel methods (the RKHS view of large-margin classifiers) and deep-learning theory (why uniform convergence breaks and what replaces it) build directly on these foundations.
