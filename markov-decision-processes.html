Markov decision processes

A controlled Markov chain with rewards: at each state you pick an action, the world transitions stochastically, and a single discounted-reward objective makes “optimal behaviour” a sharply defined fixed-point problem.

1. The MDP model

A Markov decision process formalises sequential decision-making under uncertainty. It is a tuple

$\mathcal M = (\mathcal S,\ \mathcal A,\ P,\ R,\ \gamma)$

where $\mathcal S$ is a (here finite) set of states, $\mathcal A$ a set of actions, $P(s'\mid s,a)$ the transition kernel giving the probability of landing in $s'$ after taking action $a$ in state $s$, $R(s,a)$ the (expected) reward for that choice, and $\gamma\in[0,1)$ a discount factor. The defining Markov property: the next state and reward depend only on the current $(s,a)$, not on the history. An MDP is exactly a Markov chain that you steer — for each fixed action the rows $P(\cdot\mid s,a)$ are a stochastic matrix.

A policy $\pi$ says how to act. A deterministic stationary policy is a map $\pi:\mathcal S\to\mathcal A$; a stochastic policy gives a distribution $\pi(a\mid s)$ over actions. Stationary means it does not depend on time. Fixing a policy collapses the MDP back to a plain Markov chain with one-step kernel $P^\pi(s'\mid s)=\sum_a \pi(a\mid s)\,P(s'\mid s,a)$ and reward $R^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)R(s,a)$. The goal is to maximise the expected discounted return $\;\mathbb E\big[\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t R(S_t,A_t)\big]$.

Worked mini-example. The widget below is the running example for the whole page: three states $s_0$ (Start), $s_1$ (Wait), $s_2$ (Goal, absorbing), each with two actions $a$ (advance) and $b$ (stay/idle). From $s_1$, action $a$ reaches the goal with probability $0.8$ and slips back to $s_0$ with probability $0.2$ — a genuinely stochastic transition — and pays $R(s_1,a)=5$. Idling at $s_1$ costs $R(s_1,b)=-1$. The goal $s_2$ pays $+1$ every step forever, so already $V$ at $s_2$ will be $1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\dots=\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}$. Notice each row of $P(\cdot\mid s,a)$ sums to $1$.

2. Value functions & Bellman expectation

Fix a policy $\pi$. Its state-value function measures how good it is to be in $s$ and follow $\pi$ thereafter:

$\displaystyle V^\pi(s) = \mathbb E_\pi\!\Big[\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t R(S_t,A_t)\,\Big|\, S_0=s\Big].$

Peeling off the first step gives the Bellman expectation equation, a linear fixed-point relation:

$\displaystyle V^\pi(s) = \sum_{a}\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\big[\,R(s,a)+\gamma\,V^\pi(s')\,\big].$

The companion action-value (or $Q$-) function fixes the first action and then follows $\pi$:

$\displaystyle Q^\pi(s,a) = R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^\pi(s'),\qquad V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,Q^\pi(s,a).$

For a finite MDP the expectation equation is a system of $|\mathcal S|$ linear equations $V^\pi = R^\pi + \gamma P^\pi V^\pi$, i.e. $(I-\gamma P^\pi)V^\pi = R^\pi$. Because $\gamma<1$ and $P^\pi$ is stochastic, $I-\gamma P^\pi$ is invertible, so $V^\pi=(I-\gamma P^\pi)^{-1}R^\pi$ is the unique solution. This is policy evaluation.

Worked mini-example. Take the always-advance policy $\pi\equiv a$ at $\gamma=0.9$ on the running MDP. The goal self-loops, so $V^\pi(s_2)=\tfrac{1}{1-0.9}=10$. Then $V^\pi(s_1)=5+0.9(0.8\cdot 10 + 0.2\,V^\pi(s_0))$ and $V^\pi(s_0)=0+0.9\,V^\pi(s_1)$ — two equations in two unknowns whose solution is $V^\pi(s_0)\approx 13.10$, $V^\pi(s_1)\approx 14.56$. Idling instead at $s_1$ (policy $b$ there) leaves you stuck collecting $-1$ forever: $V^\pi(s_1)=\tfrac{-1}{1-0.9}=-10$.

Choose the action $\pi$ takes at $s_0$ and $s_1$ (the goal $s_2$ always self-loops), slide $\gamma$, and read the solved $V^\pi$ and $Q^\pi$.

3. Bellman optimality

Among all policies there is a best achievable value at each state, the optimal value function $V^*(s)=\max_\pi V^\pi(s)$. It satisfies the Bellman optimality equation — the expectation equation with the policy average replaced by a maximum over actions:

$\displaystyle V^*(s) = \max_{a}\Big\{\,R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^*(s')\,\Big\} = \max_a Q^*(s,a),$

with the optimal action-value $Q^*(s,a)=R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)\,V^*(s')$. This is a nonlinear system (the max is the kink), so it is not solved by a matrix inverse — that is what the next two sections are for. The discrete-time Bellman recursion is precisely the dynamic-programming principle of optimality specialised to a stationary, infinite-horizon, discounted problem.

Once $V^*$ (equivalently $Q^*$) is known, an optimal policy is read off greedily:

$\displaystyle \pi^*(s) \in \arg\max_a\ Q^*(s,a) = \arg\max_a\Big\{R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V^*(s')\Big\}.$

A central theorem: every finite discounted MDP has at least one deterministic stationary optimal policy, and it is simultaneously optimal from every start state. You never need to randomise or to remember the clock to act optimally.

Worked mini-example. On the running MDP the goal contributes $V^*(s_2)=\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}$. Advancing dominates idling everywhere: at $s_1$, $Q^*(s_1,a)=5+\gamma(0.8\,V^*(s_2)+0.2\,V^*(s_0))$ beats $Q^*(s_1,b)=-1+\gamma V^*(s_1)$, and at $s_0$, $Q^*(s_0,a)=\gamma V^*(s_1)$ beats $Q^*(s_0,b)=\gamma V^*(s_0)$. So $\pi^*\equiv a$ for every $\gamma\in[0,1)$; at $\gamma=0.9$ the optimal values are $V^*\approx(13.10,\,14.56,\,10)$.

4. Value iteration & contraction

Define the Bellman optimality operator $T$ acting on value functions by

$\displaystyle (TV)(s) = \max_a\Big\{R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V(s')\Big\}.$

Then $V^*$ is exactly the fixed point $TV^*=V^*$. The key analytic fact: $T$ is a $\gamma$-contraction in the sup-norm $\lVert V\rVert_\infty=\max_s|V(s)|$. For any two value functions $U,V$,

$\lVert TU - TV\rVert_\infty \le \gamma\,\lVert U-V\rVert_\infty,$

because the inner expectation is an average (a convex combination, non-expansive) scaled by $\gamma$, and $\max_a$ is itself $1$-Lipschitz in the sup-norm. By the Banach fixed-point theorem, on the complete metric space $(\mathbb R^{|\mathcal S|},\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)$ a contraction has a unique fixed point and the iteration converges to it from any seed. That iteration is value iteration: $V_{k+1}=TV_k$, and

$\lVert V_k - V^*\rVert_\infty \le \gamma^k\,\lVert V_0 - V^*\rVert_\infty.$

So the error shrinks geometrically at rate $\gamma$ — a clean, dimension-free guarantee. Smaller $\gamma$ converges faster; $\gamma$ near $1$ converges slowly (the price of caring about the far future).

Worked mini-example. Start from $V_0\equiv 0$ at $\gamma=0.9$. The optimal values are $V^*=(13.10,\,14.56,\,10)$, so the initial sup-error is $\lVert V_0-V^*\rVert_\infty=14.56$ — carried by the start states $s_0,s_1$, whose value has to propagate back from the goal. The absorbing goal $s_2$ is the easy case: $V_k(s_2)=\tfrac{1-\gamma^k}{1-\gamma}$ gives $|V_k(s_2)-V^*(s_2)|=\gamma^k\cdot 10$, contracting at exactly $\gamma$ — but that is the smallest gap, not the largest. The sup-norm error obeys the guarantee $\lVert V_k-V^*\rVert_\infty\le\gamma^k\cdot 14.56$ and runs ahead of it: the readout sequence is $14.56,\,13.10,\,8.84,\dots$, with the per-step ratio $\le\gamma$ (and well below it once value reaches the start states).

5. Policy iteration

Policy iteration alternates two exact steps until the policy stops changing:

  1. Policy evaluation. Solve $V^{\pi_k}=(I-\gamma P^{\pi_k})^{-1}R^{\pi_k}$ for the current policy (the linear system of §2).
  2. Policy improvement. Set $\pi_{k+1}(s)\in\arg\max_a\big\{R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V^{\pi_k}(s')\big\}$, the greedy policy with respect to $V^{\pi_k}$.

The policy improvement theorem guarantees $V^{\pi_{k+1}}\ge V^{\pi_k}$ pointwise, with strict improvement at some state unless $\pi_k$ is already greedy w.r.t. its own value — in which case $V^{\pi_k}$ satisfies the Bellman optimality equation and $\pi_k=\pi^*$. Because there are only finitely many deterministic policies ($|\mathcal A|^{|\mathcal S|}$ of them) and each round strictly improves until stable, policy iteration terminates in finitely many iterations at an exactly optimal policy.

Contrast with value iteration: value iteration does cheap inexact backups and converges only in the limit (geometrically); policy iteration does an expensive exact solve each round but converges in a small finite number of rounds — typically very few. They are two ends of the generalised policy iteration spectrum, and modified policy iteration interpolates by doing a few evaluation sweeps instead of a full solve.

Worked mini-example. Seed with the lazy policy $\pi_0:s_0\to b,\,s_1\to b$ (idle everywhere but the goal) at $\gamma=0.9$. Evaluation gives $V^{\pi_0}=(0,-10,10)$ — idling at $s_1$ is a $-10$ trap. Greedy improvement repairs $s_1$ first (advancing from $s_0$ into a $-10$ state still looks bad), giving $\pi_1:s_0\to b,\,s_1\to a$. Re-evaluating yields $V^{\pi_1}=(0,12.2,10)$; now advancing from $s_0$ pays off, so greedy flips $s_0$ too, giving $\pi_2\equiv a$. A final evaluation $V^{\pi_2}\approx(13.10,14.56,10)$ is greedy-stable. So the optimal policy is reached after two improvement steps (and one more round confirms stability).

6. Discounting & horizons

Why discount at all? The factor $\gamma\in[0,1)$ keeps the infinite sum $\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t R_t$ finite (when rewards are bounded), encodes a preference for sooner rewards, and — crucially — is what makes $T$ a contraction. The geometric weights $\gamma^t$ decay, and the scale on which they matter is the effective horizon

$\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx \frac{1}{1-\gamma}.$

The weight placed on the reward at $t=H_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is $\gamma^{1/(1-\gamma)}\to e^{-1}\approx 0.368$ as $\gamma\to 1$, so $1/(1-\gamma)$ is the "$1/e$ time-constant" of the discount: rewards much beyond it are nearly invisible. $\gamma=0.9$ looks roughly $10$ steps ahead; $\gamma=0.99$, about $100$. As $\gamma\to 1$ the agent becomes far-sighted but the contraction rate degrades to $1$ and value iteration slows to a crawl.

Three standard objectives sit on a spectrum:

  • Discounted ($\gamma<1$): maximise $\mathbb E[\sum_t\gamma^t R_t]$. A stationary deterministic optimal policy always exists — the case this whole page treats.
  • Finite-horizon ($N$ steps): maximise $\mathbb E[\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}R_t]$, solved by backward induction $V_n(s)=\max_a\{R(s,a)+\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V_{n+1}(s')\}$ from $V_N\equiv 0$. The optimal policy is generally time-dependent: what is optimal with $10$ steps left differs from $1$ step left.
  • Average-reward (the $\gamma\to 1$ limit): maximise the per-step gain $\lim_{N\to\infty}\tfrac1N\mathbb E[\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}R_t]$, used when there is no natural discounting and the process runs indefinitely; it needs its own (Bellman) gain–bias machinery since the undiscounted sum diverges.
Worked mini-example. At the goal $s_2$ the value is the discounted geometric series $V^*(s_2)=\sum_{t\ge 0}\gamma^t\cdot 1=\tfrac{1}{1-\gamma}$, which is literally the effective horizon. So $\gamma=0.9\Rightarrow 10$, $\gamma=0.95\Rightarrow 20$, $\gamma=0.99\Rightarrow 100$ — moving $\gamma$ from $0.9$ to $0.99$ multiplies the goal's value tenfold while making value iteration roughly ten times slower.

Connections

The MDP is the discrete, stochastic backbone of sequential decision-making. The frontier is what happens when its clean assumptions break: when the state is only partially observed (POMDPs, where the belief state restores Markovianness but explodes the state space), when $P$ and $R$ are unknown and must be learned from samples (reinforcement learning), and when $\mathcal S$ is too large to enumerate (function approximation, where the contraction guarantees can fail). Even the basics — average-reward optimality, robust MDPs against an adversarial kernel — remain active research.

  • Probability theory — Markov chains — fixing a policy collapses an MDP to the chain $P^\pi$; stationary distributions and mixing govern average-reward MDPs.
  • Optimal control & dynamic programming — discrete-time Bellman equation — the continuous HJB / Pontryagin cousin; the discounted MDP is its stationary, stochastic specialisation.
  • Fixed-point theorems — Banach contraction — the abstract engine behind value iteration's existence, uniqueness, and geometric convergence.
  • Convex optimization — linear programming — a finite discounted MDP is exactly solvable as an LP in the value variables, the third classical algorithm alongside value and policy iteration.
  • Optimal control — stochastic control — adds noise and continuous state to the same Bellman backbone, the bridge toward stochastic dynamic programming.
