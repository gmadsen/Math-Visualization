Mathematical finance & stochastic control

From Itô's lemma to Black–Scholes to Merton's portfolio problem: how the SDE skeleton built in stochastic calculus prices contingent claims by no-arbitrage replication, and how dynamic-programming over a controlled diffusion solves the optimal-investment problem via the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation.

1. The Black–Scholes model

The 1973 model of Black, Scholes, and Merton was the first arbitrage-free pricing theory rigorous enough to be calibrated against quoted market prices. It rests on three structural choices: (i) the asset $S_t$ follows geometric Brownian motion with constant drift $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$, (ii) trading is frictionless and continuous in time, (iii) there is a riskless bond earning rate $r$, and (iv) the asset pays no dividends — with a continuous dividend yield $q$ the formula becomes $C = Se^{-qT}\Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2)$ with $r$ replaced by $r-q$ in $d_{1,2}$.

The driving SDE is $$dS_t \;=\; \mu\,S_t\,dt + \sigma\,S_t\,dB_t, \qquad S_0=s.$$ Applying Itô to $\log S_t$ (see §Itô's formula) gives the closed form $$S_t = s\exp\!\bigl((\mu-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t+\sigma B_t\bigr),$$ so $\log S_t$ is Gaussian, $S_t$ stays positive almost surely, and the mean grows like $s e^{\mu t}$ while the median grows like $s e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)t}$ — the lognormal mean–median gap is the "volatility drag."

No-arbitrage pricing of a European call. A European call on $S$ with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ pays $(S_T-K)^+$. The Black–Scholes formula prices it at time $t<T$ as $$C(S,t) \;=\; S\,\Phi(d_1) \;-\; Ke^{-r(T-t)}\Phi(d_2),$$ with $$d_1 \;=\; \frac{\log(S/K)+(r+\sigma^2/2)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}, \qquad d_2 \;=\; d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T-t},$$ and $\Phi$ the standard-normal CDF. The formula does not depend on $\mu$ — the empirically untestable real-world drift drops out, because the price equals the cost of a self-financing hedging portfolio, which only uses $r$ and $\sigma$.

The thumb rule. For at-the-money ($S=K$) and zero rate, $d_1=\sigma\sqrt{T}/2$, $d_2=-d_1$, so $C\approx S\cdot 2\Phi'(0)\cdot d_1 \approx 0.4\,S\,\sigma\sqrt{T}$. A 1-year ATM option on a 20%-vol stock is worth roughly 8% of the underlying — useful for sanity-checking quotes in your head.

2. The Black–Scholes PDE

Where does the formula come from? Two parallel derivations meet at the same equation. The first is delta-hedging. Hold one call $V(S,t)$ and short $\Delta_t=\partial_S V$ shares; the portfolio value $\Pi_t = V_t - \Delta_t S_t$ has differential (Itô): $$d\Pi_t \;=\; \Bigl(\partial_t V + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}V\Bigr)dt + \bigl(\partial_S V - \Delta_t\bigr)dS_t.$$ Choosing $\Delta_t=\partial_S V$ kills the stochastic term. The remaining $d\Pi_t$ is deterministic, so by no-arbitrage it must grow at the risk-free rate: $d\Pi_t = r\Pi_t\,dt$. Equating coefficients, $$\boxed{\;\partial_t V + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}V + rS\,\partial_S V - rV \;=\; 0\;}$$ with terminal condition $V(T,S)=(S-K)^+$ for a call. The second derivation is via Feynman–Kac applied to the risk-neutral SDE $dS_t=rS_t\,dt+\sigma S_t\,d\tilde B_t$: the same PDE pops out as the generator equation for $V(t,S)=e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[g(S_T)\mid S_t=S]$. The two pictures — hedging and expectation under the right measure — are different aspects of the same operator.

Reduction to the heat equation. Substituting $S=Ke^x$, $\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2(T-t)$, $V=Ke^{-r(T-t)}e^{\alpha x+\beta\tau}u(x,\tau)$ for appropriate $\alpha,\beta$ collapses the PDE to $\partial_\tau u = \partial_{xx}u$. Black–Scholes is therefore the heat equation in disguise — and the closed form is the Gaussian convolution of the payoff.

3. Risk-neutral pricing and martingale measures

Girsanov's theorem provides a more conceptual restatement: drifts are a feature of the measure, not of the process. Under the historical measure $\mathbb{P}$ the asset has the real-world drift $\mu$, but a change of measure $$\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}} \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl(-\theta B_T - \tfrac{1}{2}\theta^2 T\Bigr), \qquad \theta \;=\; \frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}\quad\text{(market price of risk)}$$ turns $\tilde B_t = B_t + \theta t$ into a $\mathbb{Q}$-Brownian motion. Under $\mathbb{Q}$, $$dS_t \;=\; r S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,d\tilde B_t,$$ so the discounted price $\tilde S_t = e^{-rt}S_t$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$-martingale. Risk-neutral pricing: $$V_0 \;=\; e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\bigl[g(S_T)\bigr]$$ for any (suitably integrable) payoff $g$. The discount accounts for time-value; the change of measure encodes no-arbitrage.

This is the operating principle behind almost every modern derivatives desk: build a model in which the discounted underlying is a $\mathbb{Q}$-martingale, then price the derivative as a (discounted) $\mathbb{Q}$-expectation, computed by Monte Carlo or by solving the corresponding PDE.

Fundamental theorems of asset pricing. FTAP I: no arbitrage $\iff$ there exists an equivalent martingale measure (EMM) under which discounted prices are martingales. FTAP II: the market is complete (every payoff is replicable) $\iff$ the EMM is unique. In Black–Scholes, $\mathbb{Q}$ is unique and the market is complete. As soon as we add a second source of randomness — stochastic volatility, jumps, multi-factor rates — uniqueness fails and pricing acquires a degree of freedom that must be fixed by calibration.

4. Optimal portfolio selection (Merton)

Pricing solves "what is this payoff worth?" The complementary question is "how should I invest in the first place?" Merton (1969) framed it as a dynamic optimization. Let $W_t$ denote wealth, and let an investor allocate fraction $\pi_t$ of wealth to the risky asset $S_t$ and the rest to the bond, consuming at rate $c_t\ge 0$. Wealth evolves as $$dW_t \;=\; \bigl[(r + \pi_t(\mu - r))W_t - c_t\bigr]dt \;+\; \pi_t \sigma W_t\,dB_t.$$ The investor seeks to maximize $$\mathbb{E}\!\int_0^T e^{-\rho t}\,u(c_t)\,dt \;+\; e^{-\rho T}\,U(W_T)$$ over admissible feedback controls $(c_t,\pi_t)$, for a utility $u$ — usually constant relative risk aversion: $u(c)=c^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$, with $\gamma>0$, $\gamma\ne 1$.

The closed form. Because CRRA utility is homogeneous of degree $1-\gamma$, the value function separates: $V(t,w) = \phi(t)\,w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ for some deterministic $\phi$. Plugging into the HJB equation (next section), the optimal portfolio fraction is $$\boxed{\;\pi^* \;=\; \frac{\mu - r}{\gamma\,\sigma^2}\;}$$ — the Merton fraction. It's the Sharpe ratio $(\mu-r)/\sigma$ divided by $\gamma\sigma$, equivalently the excess return divided by risk-aversion-weighted variance. Crucially, $\pi^*$ does not depend on wealth $w$ or on the horizon $T$. Higher Sharpe ratios mean larger positions; higher risk aversion ($\gamma\uparrow$) or higher volatility ($\sigma\uparrow$) shrink the position toward zero (and can flip its sign when $\mu<r$).

5. The Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation

The Merton result generalizes. Let $X_t\in\mathbb{R}^d$ be a controlled diffusion $$dX_t \;=\; b(t,X_t,u_t)\,dt \;+\; \sigma(t,X_t,u_t)\,dB_t,$$ where the control $u_t\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^k$ is chosen by an agent observing $X_t$. Given a running reward $f(t,x,u)$ and a terminal reward $g(x)$, the value function is $$V(t,x) \;=\; \sup_{u}\,\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\!\left[\int_t^T f(s,X_s,u_s)\,ds \;+\; g(X_T)\right].$$ Dynamic programming gives the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation: $$\partial_t V \;+\; \sup_{u\in U}\!\Bigl\{\mathcal{L}^u V(t,x) + f(t,x,u)\Bigr\} \;=\; 0, \qquad V(T,x)=g(x),$$ with $\mathcal{L}^u V = b\cdot\nabla V + \tfrac{1}{2}\tr\bigl(\sigma\sigma^\top \nabla^2 V\bigr)$ the generator of $X$ under control $u$.

The supremum makes HJB nonlinear, in contrast to Feynman–Kac which is linear (the controlled process is fixed, only the expectation is computed). The argmax inside is an optimal feedback control $u^*(t,x)$. A verification theorem establishes that any smooth solution $V$ of the HJB equation satisfying suitable growth/terminal conditions equals the value function and the candidate $u^*$ is optimal — sufficient conditions for what dynamic programming derives heuristically.

Merton revisited. For Merton's problem with CRRA utility and no consumption, take state $X_t=W_t$, control $\pi$, $b = (r+\pi(\mu-r))w$, $\sigma = \pi\sigma w$. Substituting the ansatz $V(t,w)=\phi(t)w^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)$ and computing $\sup_\pi$ analytically reproduces $\pi^*=(\mu-r)/(\gamma\sigma^2)$, with $\phi$ an ODE in $t$. The closed-form Merton result is the canonical example of a solvable HJB.

6. Itô diffusions in finance

Three Itô-calculus facts do most of the work in derivatives pricing:

  • Quadratic variation. For an Itô process $dX_t = b\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t$, $d\langle X\rangle_t = \sigma^2\,dt$. Hedging error variance is a quadratic-variation integral of the mis-hedged piece.
  • Itô's formula. $df(X_t) = f'(X_t)\,dX_t + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(X_t)\,d\langle X\rangle_t$. The second term is the source of the $\partial_{SS}$ in the Black–Scholes PDE.
  • Itô isometry. $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(\int_0^T\theta_t\,dB_t\bigr)^2\bigr] = \mathbb{E}\int_0^T\theta_t^2\,dt$. Quantifies hedging-error variance as a single $L^2$ integral.

Self-financing portfolios. A portfolio holding $\Delta_t$ shares and $\beta_t$ in the riskless bond, with total wealth $W_t=\Delta_t S_t+\beta_t e^{rt}$, is self-financing if no external cash is added or removed: $dW_t = \Delta_t\,dS_t + r\beta_t e^{rt}\,dt$. The hedging derivation builds a self-financing portfolio that replicates the option payoff at maturity — and by no-arbitrage, its current cost must equal the option price.

7. Beyond Black–Scholes

The Black–Scholes formula is calibrated to one parameter per option: implied volatility $\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}$, the $\sigma$ that makes the formula return the observed price. If Black–Scholes were correct, $\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(K,T)$ would be flat in $K$. Empirically, it isn't: in equity options the implied surface shows a volatility smile (U-shaped in $K$ for short maturities) and a skew (lower strikes carry higher vol — out-of-the-money puts price in crash risk). This is the "working empirical object" that any post-Black–Scholes model must fit.

Three families of extensions dominate practice:

  • Stochastic volatility (Heston 1993). Volatility itself follows an SDE: $$dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sqrt{v_t}\,S_t\,dB_t^{(1)}, \qquad dv_t = \kappa(\theta-v_t)\,dt+\xi\sqrt{v_t}\,dB_t^{(2)},$$ with $B^{(1)},B^{(2)}$ correlated. Two Brownian drivers, one tradable: the market is incomplete and the volatility-risk premium is a free parameter calibrated to market prices.
  • Jump-diffusion (Merton 1976). Add a compound Poisson process: $dS_t/S_{t-} = \mu\,dt+\sigma\,dB_t + (J-1)\,dN_t$, where $N$ is Poisson with rate $\lambda$ and $J$ is the random jump multiplier. Captures fat tails and crash risk in a tractable way — the resulting PDE has an integral term but pricing remains closed-form for European options.
  • Local volatility (Dupire 1994). Take $\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}(t,S)$ as a deterministic function of state, chosen to exactly fit the entire implied surface via the Dupire formula $$\sigma_{\mathrm{loc}}^2(K,T) \;=\; \frac{2\,\partial_T C(K,T)}{K^2\,\partial_{KK}C(K,T)}$$ (stated here under $r=q=0$; the general form has $\partial_T C + (r-q)K\,\partial_K C + qC$ in the numerator). No new randomness, but the surface determines the local dynamics — and the model is famously bad at predicting how the surface itself evolves.

8. Connections

Mathematical finance is the most operational application of stochastic calculus: every line of the derivation has a counterpart on a trader's screen, and conversely every market irregularity tells you something about the modelling assumption that broke. The flow of ideas outward:

  • Stochastic calculus — Itô's formula — the technical engine for delta-hedging and PDE derivation; without $(dB)^2 = dt$ no Black–Scholes correction term appears.
  • Stochastic calculus — Girsanov's theorem — the change-of-measure that turns the historical asset into a discounted martingale, i.e. the foundation of risk-neutral pricing.
  • Partial differential equations — the heat equation — Black–Scholes is the heat equation in log-price coordinates; numerical PDE solvers (finite difference, finite element) are an industrial-strength alternative to Monte Carlo.
  • Stochastic processes and martingales — the martingale framework provides the language (filtrations, stopping times, optional sampling) in which American-option pricing, fundamental theorems of asset pricing, and martingale-representation-driven hedging are all stated.
  • Dynamical systems — verification theorems for HJB connect optimal-control PDEs to viscosity solutions, the standard framework when value functions are merely continuous; the same framework appears in front-propagation, image processing, and reinforcement learning.

Open frontiers: rough-volatility models (Bayer–Friz–Gatheral, fractional Brownian drivers with $H<1/2$ — empirically a better fit to the term-structure of implied vol than Heston); machine-learning calibration of model-free option pricing; market-microstructure-aware optimal execution (Almgren–Chriss, Cartea–Jaimungal); equilibrium models that endogenize $\mu$ rather than treating it as a model input.
