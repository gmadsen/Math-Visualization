Quantum information

Computing with vectors in $\mathbb{C}^2$: qubits and the Bloch sphere, unitary gates, entanglement, the Born rule and no-cloning, Grover-style interference, and error correction by stabilizers.

1. Qubits and the Bloch sphere

A classical bit is $0$ or $1$. A qubit is a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$,

$$|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle,\qquad |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1,\quad \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}.$$

A global phase $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$ produces identical measurement statistics, so it is physically meaningless. Quotienting it out collapses the unit sphere $S^3\subset\mathbb{C}^2$ to $\mathbb{CP}^1$, which is geometrically a $2$-sphere — the Bloch sphere. Writing $|\psi\rangle=\cos\tfrac\theta2|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2|1\rangle$, the state sits at $(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)$: $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ at the poles, equal superpositions on the equator.

2. Quantum gates and circuits

Because evolution of a closed quantum system is unitary, every gate is a unitary matrix $U$ (so $U^\dagger U=I$): it preserves the norm, conserves total probability, and is reversible. The single-qubit workhorses are the Paulis $X,Y,Z$, the Hadamard $H=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ that builds superposition, and the phase gates $S,T$. On the Bloch sphere each is a rotation.

Add one entangling two-qubit gate (CNOT) and a small set such as $\{H,T,\text{CNOT}\}$ becomes universal: any unitary is approximated to arbitrary accuracy (Solovay–Kitaev). Build a circuit below and watch the amplitudes move.

3. Entanglement and Bell states

A two-qubit state is entangled when it cannot be written as a product $|\psi\rangle_A\otimes|\phi\rangle_B$. The family $\cos\theta\,|00\rangle+\sin\theta\,|11\rangle$ runs from a product state at $\theta=0$ to the maximally entangled Bell state $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ at $\theta=\tfrac\pi4$.

Entanglement is measured by the entanglement entropy $S(\rho_A)=-\operatorname{tr}\rho_A\log_2\rho_A$ of the reduced state $\rho_A=\operatorname{tr}_B|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=\operatorname{diag}(\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta)$. At the Bell point $\rho_A=\tfrac12 I$ and $S=1$ bit: each qubit alone looks completely random, yet the pair is in a definite pure state. This nonlocal correlation is the resource behind teleportation, superdense coding, and Bell-inequality violations.

4. Measurement, the Born rule, and no-cloning

Measuring $|\psi\rangle$ in an orthonormal basis $\{|e_k\rangle\}$ returns outcome $k$ with probability $|\langle e_k|\psi\rangle|^2$ — the Born rule — and collapses the state onto $|e_k\rangle$. Measurement is irreversible and basis-dependent: the equal superposition $|{+}\rangle$ is random in the $Z$ basis but certain in the $X$ basis.

Linearity has a sharp consequence — the no-cloning theorem: no unitary can map $|\psi\rangle|0\rangle\mapsto|\psi\rangle|\psi\rangle$ for every $|\psi\rangle$. A copier that works on $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ produces the Bell state on $|{+}\rangle$, not $|{+}\rangle|{+}\rangle$. Unknown quantum states cannot be copied — the bedrock of quantum key distribution.

5. Quantum algorithms: interference and Grover

The popular picture — "a quantum computer tries all inputs at once" — is misleading. Superposition does evaluate $f$ on every input, but a measurement returns only one random outcome. Speedups come from arranging interference so that amplitudes for wrong answers cancel and right answers reinforce before you measure.

Grover’s search finds a marked item among $N$ by repeatedly reflecting the state, rotating it toward the target by an angle $\theta$ with $\sin\theta=1/\sqrt N$ each step. After $k$ iterations the success probability is $\sin^2\!\big((2k+1)\theta\big)$, peaking near $k\approx\tfrac\pi4\sqrt N$ — a quadratic speedup over the classical $\Theta(N)$, and provably optimal. Shor’s algorithm goes further, factoring integers in polynomial time via quantum period-finding.

Over-rotation is real: past the optimal $k$ the success probability falls again (the state rotates past the target). For $N=16$ the peak is at $k=3$.

6. Quantum error correction and stabilizers

Quantum information is fragile, and the obstacles are exactly the features above: you cannot clone a state to make backups, and you cannot measure it to check for errors without collapsing it. The escape is to encode one logical qubit in several physical ones and measure only joint parities — syndromes — that reveal an error without revealing the data.

The 3-qubit bit-flip code stores $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ as $\alpha|000\rangle+\beta|111\rangle$ and uses the stabilizers $Z_0Z_1$ and $Z_1Z_2$. Both logical words have even parity, so a syndrome measurement returns $(+1,+1)$ with no error and a distinct pattern for each single bit-flip — locating it without touching $\alpha,\beta$. Shor’s 9-qubit code extends this to any single-qubit error, and below a threshold error rate, fault-tolerant computation becomes possible.

Connections

Quantum information sits where linear algebra, probability, complexity, and physics meet. The open frontiers are practical: building enough physical qubits to get one reliable logical qubit (the error-correction threshold), finding algorithms with provable speedups beyond the known families, and pinning down where the quantum/classical boundary really lies for computation.

  • Functional analysis — Hilbert spaces — state vectors, inner products, and unitary/self-adjoint operators are the entire arena.
  • Complexity theory — P and NP — the class BQP and the question of where quantum speedups can and cannot exist.
  • Coding theory — linear codes — stabilizer codes are the quantum analogue of classical linear codes, with parity checks becoming syndromes.
  • Information theory — Shannon entropy — von Neumann entropy $S(\rho)$ generalizes it and quantifies entanglement.
  • Representation theory — group representations — single-qubit gates are the group $U(2)$ acting on $\mathbb{C}^2$.
