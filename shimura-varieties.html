Shimura varieties

Higher-dimensional generalizations of modular curves: locally symmetric quasi-projective varieties whose points parametrize Hodge structures, whose canonical models live over arithmetic number fields, and whose étale cohomology realizes the automorphic-to-Galois bridge of the Langlands program.

1. The Shimura datum

A Shimura variety is built not from a Lie group alone but from a Shimura datum $(G, X)$: a connected reductive algebraic group $G$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ together with a $G(\mathbb{R})$-conjugacy class $X$ of homomorphisms $$h \colon \mathbb{S} \;\longrightarrow\; G_\mathbb{R},$$ where $\mathbb{S} = \mathrm{Res}_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{G}_m$ is the Deligne torus — the algebraic group whose real points are $\mathbb{C}^\times$. Each $h$ encodes a real Hodge structure: composing with the adjoint representation $G_\mathbb{R} \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g}_\mathbb{R})$ gives a Hodge decomposition of the Lie algebra.

Deligne's three axioms (SV1), (SV2), (SV3) single out the geometrically meaningful data:

  • (SV1) The Hodge structure on $\mathfrak{g}$ defined by $\mathrm{Ad}\circ h$ has type $\{(-1,1), (0,0), (1,-1)\}$. Equivalently, $\mathfrak{g}_\mathbb{C} = \mathfrak{g}^{-1,1} \oplus \mathfrak{g}^{0,0} \oplus \mathfrak{g}^{1,-1}$.
  • (SV2) $\mathrm{ad}(h(i))$ is a Cartan involution on $G^{\mathrm{ad}}_\mathbb{R}$ (so the centralizer $K_h \subset G(\mathbb{R})$ is compact modulo center). This forces $X$ to be a Hermitian symmetric domain.
  • (SV3) $G^{\mathrm{ad}}$ has no $\mathbb{Q}$-factor on which $h$ becomes trivial — there are no $\mathbb{Q}$-rational redundancies.

Under (SV1)–(SV3), the conjugacy class $X = G(\mathbb{R})/K_h$ is a finite disjoint union of Hermitian symmetric domains; the connected component $X^+$ is one of the bounded symmetric domains classified by Cartan. The two foundational examples make the abstract data concrete:

Concretely, the Hodge cocharacter $\mu_h$ controls the tangent space at a point of $X$: the $(0,1)$-part of the Hodge decomposition gives the antiholomorphic directions. The conjugacy class $\{\mu_h\}$ over $\mathbb{C}$ is in general defined over a number field — the reflex field $E = E(G,X)$ — which will be the field of definition of the canonical model. For $\mathrm{GL}_2$ and $\mathrm{GSp}_{2g}$ one has $E = \mathbb{Q}$; for unitary groups $\mathrm{GU}(p,q)$ attached to a CM field $K$ the reflex is $K$ or its reflex CM field; for orthogonal groups the reflex is $\mathbb{Q}$ but the canonical model still encodes deep arithmetic.

2. Adelic double quotient and canonical models

Once the datum $(G,X)$ is fixed, choose a compact open subgroup $K \subset G(\mathbb{A}_f)$ of the finite adèles. The associated Shimura variety at level $K$ is the double quotient $$\mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X) \;=\; G(\mathbb{Q}) \,\backslash\, \bigl( X \times G(\mathbb{A}_f) / K \bigr).$$ Strong approximation gives a finite decomposition $G(\mathbb{A}_f) = \bigsqcup_i G(\mathbb{Q})^+ g_i K$, so concretely $$\mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X)(\mathbb{C}) \;=\; \bigsqcup_i \Gamma_i \backslash X^+,$$ a finite disjoint union of arithmetic quotients of the connected Hermitian domain $X^+$, indexed by the double coset $G(\mathbb{Q})^+ \backslash G(\mathbb{A}_f) / K$. Here $\Gamma_i = g_i K g_i^{-1} \cap G(\mathbb{Q})^+$ is an arithmetic congruence subgroup of $G(\mathbb{Q})^+$.

For $G = \mathrm{GL}_2$ and $K = K_0(N) = \prod_p K_{0,p}$ (the standard congruence-$\Gamma_0(N)$ adèlic level), the double quotient gives back the modular curve $Y_0(N)$ studied in §modular curves. The whole formalism is engineered so that varying $K$ traces out a tower $\{\mathrm{Sh}_K\}_K$ with $G(\mathbb{A}_f)$ acting on the inverse limit through Hecke correspondences.

Theorem (Baily–Borel). For neat $K$, $\mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X)$ is a smooth quasi-projective complex algebraic variety. Adding the Baily–Borel boundary compactifies it to a normal projective variety; smoother toroidal compactifications of Ash–Mumford–Rapoport–Tai refine this with explicit local fan data.

The deeper miracle is that this complex variety descends to a number field. Shimura's reciprocity law / Deligne's canonical model theorem asserts: there is a canonical $E$-model $\mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X)_E$ over the reflex field $E = E(G,X)$, characterized by how it interacts with special points — points where the homomorphism $h$ factors through a maximal $\mathbb{Q}$-torus. Special points correspond to CM Hodge structures, and on them the Galois action of $\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{E}/E)$ is described explicitly by the reflex norm and class field theory of $E$.

The adelic packaging is not cosmetic. Hecke correspondences come from right multiplication by $g \in G(\mathbb{A}_f)$ on the second factor; the action of an unramified Hecke algebra at $p \nmid N$ becomes the obvious commuting algebra on the inverse system $\{\mathrm{Sh}_K\}$. This is what makes $H^*_{\mathrm{ét}}(\mathrm{Sh})$ a module for both $\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{E}/E)$ and the (adelic) Hecke algebra simultaneously — the seed of §5.

Shimura varieties · Siegel modular varieties $\mathcal{A}_g$

3. Siegel modular varieties

The most important infinite family is $\mathcal{A}_g = \mathrm{Sh}_{K}(\mathrm{GSp}_{2g}, \mathfrak{H}_g)$, the Siegel modular variety of genus $g$. Its complex points parametrize principally polarized abelian varieties of dimension $g$ (with level structure determined by $K$).

The Siegel upper half-space is $$\mathfrak{H}_g \;=\; \{\, \tau \in M_g(\mathbb{C}) \,:\, \tau = \tau^\top,\ \mathrm{Im}\,\tau > 0 \,\},$$ a complex Hermitian symmetric domain of dimension $\binom{g+1}{2}$. The symplectic group $\mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{R})$ acts by generalized fractional-linear transformations: $$\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} \cdot \tau \;=\; (A\tau + B)(C\tau + D)^{-1}.$$ For the principal congruence subgroup $\Gamma(N) \subset \mathrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})$, the quotient $$\mathcal{A}_g(N) \;=\; \Gamma(N) \backslash \mathfrak{H}_g$$ is the moduli space of principally polarized abelian $g$-folds with full level-$N$ structure.

Why polarization matters. A complex torus $V/\Lambda$ comes from an abelian variety iff $\Lambda$ admits a positive Riemann form — this is the Hodge–Riemann condition that promotes a torus to projective. A principal polarization is the simplest such datum (alternating, $\det = 1$); $\mathfrak{H}_g$ parametrizes pairs $(A, \lambda)$ with $\lambda$ principal. See abelian varieties §2.

For $g = 2$ the Siegel space $\mathfrak{H}_2 \subset \mathbb{C}^3$ has complex dimension $3$, and a point $\tau = \begin{pmatrix} \tau_1 & z \\ z & \tau_2 \end{pmatrix}$ is determined by three complex parameters with constraints $\mathrm{Im}\,\tau_1 > 0$, $\mathrm{Im}\,\tau_2 > 0$, $\mathrm{Im}(\tau_1 \tau_2 - z^2) > 0$. The Siegel variety carries the Hodge bundle $\omega = \det(\mathrm{Lie}\,\mathcal{A})^\vee$ where $\mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{A}_g$ is the universal abelian scheme; sections of $\omega^{\otimes k}$ are Siegel modular forms of weight $k$. The compactification theory due to Mumford and Faltings–Chai gives toroidal compactifications $\overline{\mathcal{A}_g}$ whose boundary parametrizes degenerations $A \rightsquigarrow \text{(semi-abelian)}$; the resulting minimal compactification $\mathcal{A}_g^*$ (Baily–Borel) glues in lower-dimensional Siegel varieties $\mathcal{A}_{g'}$ for $g' < g$ along the boundary — a recursive arithmetic structure.

Concretely, $\mathcal{A}_2$ has dimension $3$ over $\mathbb{Q}$; its $\mathbb{Q}$-points classify principally polarized abelian surfaces over $\mathbb{Q}$, which (by Torelli) come either from genus-$2$ curves or are products of elliptic curves. Vector-valued Siegel modular forms on $\mathcal{A}_2$ feed into the conjectural automorphic spectrum for $\mathrm{GSp}_4$ and contribute to (e.g.) Yoshida's lifts and the Saito–Kurokawa correspondence.

4. Hilbert and quaternionic Shimura varieties

Replacing $\mathrm{GL}_2/\mathbb{Q}$ by a Weil restriction or an inner form generates two further familiar families. Both extend the modular-curve story to a totally real or quaternionic base.

Hilbert modular varieties

Let $F$ be a totally real number field with $[F:\mathbb{Q}] = d$ and ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_F$. The group $$G \;=\; \mathrm{Res}_{F/\mathbb{Q}}\,\mathrm{GL}_2$$ satisfies $G(\mathbb{Q}) = \mathrm{GL}_2(F)$ and $G(\mathbb{R}) = \prod_{\sigma \colon F \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}} \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$. Its Hermitian symmetric space is the product $$X^+ \;=\; \mathbb{H}^d,$$ one upper half-plane per real embedding of $F$. The resulting Shimura variety $\mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X)$ is the Hilbert modular variety $Y_F(K)$, a $d$-dimensional algebraic variety over $\mathbb{Q}$. Its $\mathbb{C}$-points parametrize abelian varieties of dimension $d$ with real multiplication by $\mathcal{O}_F$ (an injection $\mathcal{O}_F \hookrightarrow \mathrm{End}(A)$ such that $\mathrm{Lie}(A)$ becomes a free $F \otimes_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{R}$-module of rank $1$).

For $F = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$ ($d = 2$) one gets a Hilbert modular surface; its Hirzebruch–Zagier divisors come from embedded Shimura subvarieties of "smaller signature" — a structure that produces explicit relations among $L$-values.

Quaternionic Shimura varieties (Shimura curves)

Let $B$ be a quaternion algebra over a totally real field $F$, split at exactly one infinite place (so $B \otimes_F \mathbb{R} \cong M_2(\mathbb{R}) \times \mathbb{H}^{d-1}$). The reductive group $G$ obtained from $B^\times$ has Hermitian symmetric space $\mathbb{H}$ (the single split place); the resulting Shimura variety is a curve — a Shimura curve $X^B(K)$. When $B = M_2(F)$ this collapses to a Hilbert modular curve; for non-split $B$ the curve has no cusps (compact!) and its moduli interpretation is in terms of fake elliptic curves: abelian surfaces with quaternionic multiplication by an order $\mathcal{O}_B \subset B$.

Compact vs non-compact. $X^B$ is compact iff $B$ is non-split. This subtle change makes Shimura curves attractive: no cusps means cleaner trace formulas, no Eisenstein contribution, and the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence relates $H^1$ of $X^B$ to certain newforms on the modular curve $X_0(N_B)$ where $N_B$ is the discriminant of $B$.

The arithmetic payoff: any classical newform $f \in S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ such that $N$ has an even number of prime divisors with $\mathrm{ord}_p(N) = 1$ has, by Jacquet–Langlands, a quaternionic counterpart $f^B$ on a Shimura curve $X^B$; the Galois representation of $f$ is realized in $H^1(X^B \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \overline{\mathbb{Q}}, \overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell)$ instead of $H^1(X_0(N))$. This is essential to level-lowering arguments in the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem — see Ribet's theorem.

5. Étale cohomology and Galois representations

Now the arithmetic harvest. Fix a prime $\ell$ different from any residue characteristic that will appear, choose a level $K$, and consider the $\ell$-adic étale cohomology $$H^i_{\mathrm{ét}}\bigl( \mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X) \times_E \overline{E},\ \overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell \bigr).$$ This finite-dimensional $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell$-vector space carries two commuting actions:

  • A continuous action of $\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{E}/E)$, by functoriality of étale cohomology.
  • An action of the (adelic) Hecke algebra $\mathcal{H}(G(\mathbb{A}_f) /\!/ K)$, induced by the Hecke correspondences on the Shimura variety.

Because the Hecke algebra is commutative on its spherical part (away from $\mathrm{ramif}(K)$), the étale cohomology decomposes as a direct sum over Hecke eigensystems. For each automorphic representation $\pi$ of $G(\mathbb{A})$ contributing to $H^i_{\mathrm{ét}}$, the $\pi$-isotypic component is a Galois representation: $$H^i_{\mathrm{ét}}\bigl( \mathrm{Sh}_K(G,X) \times \overline{E},\ \overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell \bigr)_\pi \;=\; \pi_f^K \otimes \rho_{\pi,\ell}.$$ This is the canonical mechanism by which automorphic forms produce Galois representations.

Eichler–Shimura ($\mathrm{GL}_2/\mathbb{Q}$). For a weight-$2$ newform $f \in S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ with $q$-expansion $\sum a_n q^n$, the eigensystem $T_p \mapsto a_p$ singles out a $2$-dimensional Galois representation $\rho_{f,\ell} \colon \mathrm{Gal}(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q}) \to \mathrm{GL}_2(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell)$ inside $H^1_{\mathrm{ét}}(X_0(N) \times \overline{\mathbb{Q}}, \overline{\mathbb{Q}}_\ell)$, with $\mathrm{tr}\,\rho_{f,\ell}(\mathrm{Frob}_p) = a_p$ for $p \nmid N\ell$.

For higher rank and weight, this picture was generalized by Deligne (weight $\ge 2$ holomorphic), Carayol (Hilbert modular forms via Shimura curves, level-by-level), Brylinski–Labesse (totally real fields), Kottwitz (Siegel and unitary cases via the "stable trace formula on Shimura varieties"), and many others — culminating in Shin–Caraiani–Scholze for unitary groups and the modern derived-category approach of Fargues–Scholze on the Fargues–Fontaine curve.

6. The Langlands–Kottwitz program

The deepest application of Shimura varieties to arithmetic is the explicit count of $\mathbb{F}_q$-points of their integral models — the Langlands–Kottwitz formula. Once integral models exist (e.g. Mumford's moduli interpretation for Siegel varieties, Rapoport–Zink for unitary groups, Kisin in the abelian-type case), one can ask: how many $\mathbb{F}_q$-points does $\mathrm{Sh}_K \otimes \mathbb{F}_p$ have?

For Siegel-type varieties, this question is governed by Honda–Tate theory: isogeny classes of abelian varieties over $\mathbb{F}_q$ are parametrized by Weil $q$-numbers (algebraic integers $\pi$ all of whose archimedean conjugates have absolute value $\sqrt{q}$), and each class contributes its size weighted by automorphism. Summing over isogeny classes and grouping yields the Langlands–Kottwitz formula: $$\bigl| \mathrm{Sh}_K(\mathbb{F}_q) \bigr| \;=\; \sum_{(\gamma_0;\gamma,\delta)} c(\gamma_0;\gamma,\delta) \cdot \mathrm{O}_\gamma(\mathbf{1}_K^p) \cdot \mathrm{TO}_\delta(\phi_p),$$ where the sum is over Kottwitz triples $(\gamma_0; \gamma, \delta)$, $\mathrm{O}_\gamma$ is an orbital integral on the prime-to-$p$ Hecke side, $\mathrm{TO}_\delta$ is a $\sigma$-twisted orbital integral on the local-at-$p$ side, and $\phi_p$ is a specific function in the local Hecke algebra at $p$.

Why this matters. Comparing the formula above with the Arthur–Selberg trace formula on the automorphic side (after stabilization) gives the Galois ↔ automorphic matching at every prime $p$ where the moduli problem has good reduction. This proves local-global compatibility for the Galois representations $\rho_\pi$ of §5, identifying the local Galois rep at $p$ with the local Langlands parameter of $\pi_p$.

Inside this formula sit several monumental theorems:

  • Faltings' isogeny theorem — abelian varieties over a number field with isomorphic Tate modules at one prime are isogenous; the Honda–Tate side of Langlands–Kottwitz uses this to identify $\mathbb{F}_q$-isogeny classes with their Frobenius eigenvalues.
  • Kottwitz's stabilization — the $c(\gamma_0;\gamma,\delta)$ coefficient is a product of Kottwitz signs $\alpha(\gamma_0)$ that absorb the failure of the Hasse principle; only after stabilization does the geometric side match the spectral side.
  • Local-global compatibility for $\mathrm{GL}_n$ — proved by Caraiani (semisimple-local-Galois) using Shin's stable trace formula identity for unitary Shimura varieties + Scholze's perfectoid method at the boundary.

Beyond these specific cases, the Langlands–Kottwitz program is the technical backbone of every modern proof of automorphy lifting and modularity. Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem uses it implicitly via Eichler–Shimura on $X_0(N)$; Taylor–Harris's proof of the local Langlands correspondence for $\mathrm{GL}_n/\mathbb{Q}_p$ via global trace identities on unitary Shimura varieties uses it explicitly; the recent Sato–Tate conjecture over totally real fields (Clozel–Harris–Shepherd-Barron–Taylor) plays the formula off symmetric powers on Hilbert modular varieties.

Connections

Shimura varieties sit at the intersection of every major thread in modern arithmetic geometry: they are the geometric vehicle for the Langlands correspondence, the source of (almost) all known constructions of Galois representations, and an active research frontier — integral models in the abelian-type case were only completed by Kisin–Pappas around 2015, while the structure of Shimura varieties at primes of bad reduction (local models, Rapoport–Zink towers, Newton stratification) is a vibrant subject driven by recent work of Caraiani, Scholze, Hansen, Fargues, and Zhu.

  • Modular curves — $X_0(N)$ — the rank-$1$ case $(GL_2, \mathbb{H}^\pm)$; Shimura varieties are higher-rank generalizations to reductive groups beyond $\mathrm{GL}_2$.
  • Automorphic forms (adelic) — definition — the spectral side of $H^*_{\mathrm{ét}}$; Shimura varieties realize automorphic representations in cohomology.
  • Langlands program — global $\mathrm{GL}_n$ — the conjectural correspondence whose Galois half is realized by $\rho_{\pi,\ell}$ above.
  • Complex multiplication — CM abelian varieties — special points on Shimura varieties; the Shimura–Taniyama formula governs their reduction.
  • Abelian varieties — polarizations — moduli interpretation of Siegel Shimura varieties; the Hodge bundle is the obstruction-theoretic engine.
  • Étale cohomology — $\ell$-adic — the cohomology theory carrying the Galois action; Lefschetz fixed-point theorem connects to Langlands–Kottwitz.
  • Galois representations — the output of §5; Shimura varieties give the constructive route from automorphic forms to representations.
  • Modularity & FLT — level lowering — Ribet's theorem rewires automorphic representations across modular and quaternionic Shimura varieties; the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence is essential.

Open frontiers: integral models of Shimura varieties of abelian type at parahoric level (Kisin–Pappas–Zhu); local Shimura varieties à la Rapoport–Viehmann and the Fargues–Scholze categorical local Langlands; the Beilinson–Bloch–Kato conjecture for motives realized in $H^*(\mathrm{Sh})$; automorphy of Galois representations not coming from Shimura varieties (Maass forms, exceptional groups) — the bottleneck for Sato–Tate beyond classical groups.
