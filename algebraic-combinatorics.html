Algebraic combinatorics

A single picture — a box-stack called a Young diagram — quietly indexes the irreducible representations of the symmetric group, a basis for symmetric functions, and a bijection that turns permutations into pairs of tableaux: this is where counting and algebra become the same subject.

1. Partitions, Young diagrams & tableaux

An integer partition of $n$ is a weakly decreasing sequence $\lambda = (\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_k \ge 1)$ of positive integers with $|\lambda| = \lambda_1 + \cdots + \lambda_k = n$; we write $\lambda \vdash n$. Its Young diagram is the left-justified array of $n$ cells with $\lambda_i$ cells in row $i$. The diagram of $\lambda = (3,2)$ is two rows, of lengths $3$ and $2$.

Reflecting the diagram across its main diagonal — turning rows into columns — gives the conjugate partition $\lambda'$, whose $j$-th part counts the cells in column $j$. So $(3,2)' = (2,2,1)$, and conjugation is an involution: $(\lambda')' = \lambda$.

Standard and semistandard tableaux

A Young tableau is a filling of the cells of a diagram with numbers. Two filling rules dominate the subject:

  • A standard Young tableau (SYT) of shape $\lambda \vdash n$ uses each of $1, 2, \ldots, n$ exactly once, with entries strictly increasing left to right along every row and strictly increasing top to bottom down every column.
  • A semistandard Young tableau (SSYT) draws entries from a fixed alphabet (say $\{1, \ldots, N\}$, with repeats allowed), and asks rows to weakly increase left to right while columns strictly increase top to bottom.

The row-weak / column-strict asymmetry is the whole engineering of the subject: it is exactly what makes the count of SSYT a polynomial (the Schur function of §4) and the count of SYT a representation dimension (§6). The number of SYT of shape $\lambda$ is written $f^\lambda$.

Worked mini-example. Shape $\lambda = (2,2) \vdash 4$ has exactly two standard tableaux, since the top-left cell must be $1$, the bottom-right must be $4$, and the only choice is whether $2$ goes right or down: the fillings $\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{smallmatrix}$ and $\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}$. So $f^{(2,2)} = 2$ — a number we will recompute three different ways across this page.

2. The hook length formula

How many standard tableaux does a shape have? A direct enumeration explodes, but there is a clean closed form. The hook of a cell $c$ in a Young diagram consists of $c$ itself, the cells strictly to its right in the same row (the arm), and the cells strictly below it in the same column (the leg). The hook length is

$\mathrm{hook}(c) = \mathrm{arm}(c) + \mathrm{leg}(c) + 1.$

Frame–Robinson–Thrall hook length formula (1954). For $\lambda \vdash n$,

$\displaystyle f^\lambda \;=\; \frac{n!}{\displaystyle\prod_{c \in \lambda} \mathrm{hook}(c)}.$

That a product of small integers always divides $n!$ to an integer is itself a small miracle; the formula has elegant probabilistic proofs (the Greene–Nijenhuis–Wilf hook walk) and a determinantal one.

Worked mini-example. For $\lambda = (2,2)$ the four hook lengths are $3, 2, 2, 1$ (the top-left cell sees one arm cell and one leg cell, so its hook is $1+1+1 = 3$). Then $f^{(2,2)} = 4! / (3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1) = 24/12 = 2$, matching the two tableaux we wrote by hand in §1. For the hook $\lambda = (3,1)$ the hooks are $4, 2, 1, 1$ and $f^{(3,1)} = 24/8 = 3$.

3. Symmetric functions

A polynomial in $x_1, \ldots, x_N$ is symmetric if it is unchanged by every permutation of the variables. Stabilizing in the number of variables and grading by degree gives the ring of symmetric functions $\Lambda = \bigoplus_d \Lambda^d$. Each graded piece $\Lambda^d$ has finite dimension equal to the number of partitions of $d$, and there are four classical bases, all indexed by partitions:

  • monomial $m_\lambda$ — the sum of all distinct monomials whose exponent multiset is $\lambda$ (e.g. $m_{(2,1)} = \sum x_i^2 x_j$ over $i \ne j$);
  • elementary $e_\lambda = e_{\lambda_1} e_{\lambda_2}\cdots$, where $e_k = \sum_{i_1 \, <\, \cdots \, <\, i_k} x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k}$ is the sum of squarefree degree-$k$ monomials;
  • complete homogeneous $h_\lambda = h_{\lambda_1} h_{\lambda_2}\cdots$, where $h_k = \sum_{i_1 \, \le\, \cdots \, \le\, i_k} x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_k}$ is the sum of all degree-$k$ monomials;
  • power sum $p_\lambda = p_{\lambda_1} p_{\lambda_2}\cdots$, where $p_k = \sum_i x_i^k$.

The $e_k$ and $h_k$ differ only by whether repeated indices are allowed; in two variables $e_2 = x_1 x_2$ but $h_2 = x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$.

Newton's identities

The power sums tie the $e$ and $h$ families together by a recursion. Newton's identities read

$\displaystyle p_k - e_1 p_{k-1} + e_2 p_{k-2} - \cdots + (-1)^{k-1} e_{k-1} p_1 + (-1)^k k\, e_k = 0,$

so $p_1 = e_1$, then $p_2 = e_1 p_1 - 2e_2$, and so on — each $p_k$ is a polynomial in the $e$'s and conversely. Over $\mathbb{Q}$ the power sums are themselves a basis, which is why $\Lambda$ is a polynomial ring on either the $e_k$ or the $p_k$.

Worked mini-example ($N = 2$). With variables $x_1, x_2$: $e_1 = x_1 + x_2 = p_1$, $e_2 = x_1 x_2$, $p_2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$, and $h_2 = x_1^2 + x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$. Newton's second identity $p_2 = e_1 p_1 - 2e_2 = (x_1+x_2)^2 - 2x_1 x_2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$ checks out, and $h_2 = e_1^2 - e_2 = p_2/1 + e_2$ relates the families.

4. Schur functions

The fifth — and most important — basis of $\Lambda$ is combinatorial. For $\lambda \vdash n$, the Schur function is the generating function over semistandard tableaux:

$\displaystyle s_\lambda(x_1, x_2, \ldots) \;=\; \sum_{T \,\in\, \mathrm{SSYT}(\lambda)} x^{\mathrm{content}(T)}, \qquad x^{\mathrm{content}(T)} = \prod_i x_i^{(\#\, i\text{'s in } T)}.$

That this is symmetric — invariant under permuting the variables — is the Bender–Knuth involution, and is not obvious from the definition. The coefficient of the monomial $x^\mu$ in $s_\lambda$ is the Kostka number $K_{\lambda\mu}$, the number of SSYT of shape $\lambda$ and content $\mu$.

The Jacobi–Trudi determinant

Schur functions also have a purely algebraic face. The Jacobi–Trudi formula writes them as a determinant in the complete homogeneous functions:

$\displaystyle s_\lambda \;=\; \det\bigl(h_{\lambda_i - i + j}\bigr)_{1 \le i, j \le \ell}, \qquad h_0 = 1,\ h_{ <\, 0} = 0.$

For $\lambda = (2,1)$ this is the $2 \times 2$ determinant $\det\!\begin{pmatrix} h_2 & h_3 \\ h_0 & h_1\end{pmatrix} = h_2 h_1 - h_3$. The $\{s_\lambda\}$ form an orthonormal basis of $\Lambda$ under the Hall inner product $\langle s_\lambda, s_\mu\rangle = \delta_{\lambda\mu}$ — the property that makes them the right basis for representation theory in §6.

Worked mini-example. In three variables, $s_{(2,1)}(x_1, x_2, x_3)$ sums over the $8$ semistandard tableaux of shape $(2,1)$ with entries in $\{1,2,3\}$, giving

$s_{(2,1)} = x_1^2 x_2 + x_1^2 x_3 + x_1 x_2^2 + x_1 x_3^2 + x_2^2 x_3 + x_2 x_3^2 + 2\,x_1 x_2 x_3.$

The coefficient $2$ on $x_1 x_2 x_3$ is the Kostka number $K_{(2,1),(1,1,1)} = 2$ — the two SSYT $\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\3\end{smallmatrix}$ and $\begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\2\end{smallmatrix}$ of content $(1,1,1)$. Setting all $x_i = 1$ gives $8$, agreeing with both the SSYT count and the Jacobi–Trudi value $h_2 h_1 - h_3 = 6\cdot 3 - 10 = 8$.

5. The RSK correspondence

The deepest single fact in this circle of ideas is a bijection. The Robinson–Schensted–Knuth correspondence matches each permutation $w$ of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$ with an ordered pair $(P, Q)$ of standard Young tableaux of the same shape $\lambda \vdash n$. (Knuth's generalization replaces permutations by words or nonnegative-integer matrices and lands in pairs of semistandard tableaux.)

Schensted row insertion

The pair is built one entry at a time by row insertion ("bumping"). To insert a value $x$ into a tableau $P$: scan the first row for the leftmost entry strictly greater than $x$. If none exists, append $x$ to the end of the row and stop. Otherwise replace that entry by $x$ — the displaced entry is bumped down and inserted into the next row by the same rule. Each insertion adds exactly one cell; the recording tableau $Q$ writes the step number $i$ into whichever cell was newly created at step $i$, so $P$ and $Q$ grow in lockstep and share a shape.

Counting both sides of the bijection — permutations on the left, same-shape tableau pairs on the right — gives the headline identity

$\displaystyle \sum_{\lambda \vdash n} \bigl(f^\lambda\bigr)^2 \;=\; n!.$

And by Schensted's theorem, the length of the first row of the common shape equals the length of the longest increasing subsequence of $w$ (the first column gives the longest decreasing one).

Worked mini-example. Insert $3, 1, 4, 2$ in turn. Start $P = \fbox{3}$. Inserting $1$ bumps $3$ to a new row: $\begin{smallmatrix}1\\3\end{smallmatrix}$. Inserting $4$ appends to row $1$: $\begin{smallmatrix}1&4\\3\end{smallmatrix}$. Inserting $2$ bumps $4$ down to row $2$: $P = \begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{smallmatrix}$, with $Q = \begin{smallmatrix}1&3\\2&4\end{smallmatrix}$, both of shape $(2,2)$. The first row has length $2$, and indeed the longest increasing subsequence of $3142$ has length $2$ (e.g. $1,2$ or $3,4$).

6. Representation-theoretic meaning

Everything on this page is shadowed by representation theory; the tableaux were never just combinatorics.

Irreducibles of the symmetric group

The irreducible complex representations of the symmetric group $S_n$ — the Specht modules $S^\lambda$ — are indexed by partitions $\lambda \vdash n$, and

$\dim S^\lambda = f^\lambda = \#\,\mathrm{SYT}(\lambda).$

The general identity $\sum_{\text{irreps}} (\dim)^2 = |G|$ becomes, for $G = S_n$, exactly $\sum_{\lambda \vdash n} (f^\lambda)^2 = n!$ — the very identity RSK proved bijectively in §5. So RSK is a combinatorial proof of the class equation for $S_n$.

Schur–Weyl duality in one line

On the general linear side, the Schur function $s_\lambda$ is the character of the irreducible polynomial representation of $GL_N(\mathbb{C})$ with highest weight $\lambda$: $s_\lambda(x_1, \ldots, x_N)$ is the trace of a diagonal matrix $\mathrm{diag}(x_1, \ldots, x_N)$ acting on that representation. Schur–Weyl duality packages this: the actions of $S_n$ and $GL_N$ on $(\mathbb{C}^N)^{\otimes n}$ commute and jointly decompose it as $\bigoplus_{\lambda} S^\lambda \otimes V_\lambda^{GL}$, the same $\lambda$ indexing both factors.

The Littlewood–Richardson rule

One combinatorial rule governs all the structure constants. The Littlewood–Richardson coefficients $c^\lambda_{\mu\nu}$ are defined by the Schur product

$\displaystyle s_\mu \, s_\nu \;=\; \sum_\lambda c^\lambda_{\mu\nu}\, s_\lambda,$

and by the characteristic map they simultaneously count tensor-product multiplicities of $GL_N$ irreducibles and the multiplicity of $S^\lambda$ in the induction $\mathrm{Ind}_{S_{|\mu|} \times S_{|\nu|}}^{S_n}(S^\mu \boxtimes S^\nu)$. They are computed by counting Littlewood–Richardson skew tableaux (the special case $\nu = (1)$ is Pieri's rule: $s_\mu s_{(1)} = \sum s_\lambda$ over all $\lambda$ obtained by adding one box).

Worked mini-example. For $S_3$ the three partitions $(3), (2,1), (1,1,1)$ give dimensions $f^{(3)} = 1$ (trivial), $f^{(2,1)} = 2$ (standard), $f^{(1,1,1)} = 1$ (sign), and $1^2 + 2^2 + 1^2 = 6 = 3!$. On the Schur side, $s_{(1)} s_{(1)} = s_{(2)} + s_{(1,1)}$ realizes $c^{(2)}_{(1)(1)} = c^{(1,1)}_{(1)(1)} = 1$ — the decomposition of $\mathbb{C}^N \otimes \mathbb{C}^N$ into its symmetric and antisymmetric squares.

Connections

Algebraic combinatorics is the dictionary between counting and algebra, and the dictionary keeps growing. Macdonald polynomials deform the Schur functions with two parameters $(q,t)$ and connect to Hall–Littlewood and Jack polynomials, affine Lie algebras, and the geometry of Hilbert schemes; the combinatorics of crystals gives a uniform model for tableaux across all Lie types; and even the innocent-looking Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are only conjecturally understood from the standpoint of computational complexity (whether $c^\lambda_{\mu\nu} > 0$ is decidable in polynomial time, and the Kronecker coefficients of $S_n$ remain genuinely mysterious).

  • Enumerative combinatorics — integer partitions — the underlying objects $\lambda \vdash n$, their generating functions, and Euler's product.
  • Enumerative combinatorics — permutation statistics — the source data RSK transforms, and where descents and the longest increasing subsequence live.
  • Representation theory — Young tableaux & irreps of $S_n$ — the Specht modules whose dimensions are the $f^\lambda$ of §2 and §6.
  • Representation theory — characters & orthogonality — Schur functions are characters; the Hall inner product mirrors character orthogonality.
  • Order theory and lattices — posets & Hasse diagrams — Young's lattice orders all partitions by containment of diagrams, and its saturated chains are the standard tableaux.
