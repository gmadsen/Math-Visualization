Dirac equation

The first-order relativistic wave equation Dirac wrote in 1928 to fix Klein-Gordon's probability problem — and which gave physics spin-½, the prediction of antimatter, and the gyromagnetic ratio $g=2$ as free byproducts of its algebraic structure.

1. From $E^2=p^2+m^2$ to a first-order operator

Klein-Gordon's $j^0 = i(\phi^*\partial_t\phi - \phi\,\partial_t\phi^*)$ failed as a probability density because it could go negative on a superposition of positive- and negative-energy modes (see the §5 widget on the Klein-Gordon page). Dirac's 1928 strategy was to insist on a first-order equation in time, $i\partial_t\psi = H\psi$, so that the conserved time-component would be $\psi^\dagger\psi \ge 0$ — exactly Schrödinger's positive density, but for a relativistic particle.

To stay relativistic, $H$ must square to the Klein-Gordon operator $\vec p^{\,2} + m^2$, so that energy eigenvalues live on the relativistic mass shell $E^2 = p^2 + m^2$. Dirac wrote

$$H \;=\; \vec\alpha\cdot\vec p + \beta\,m \;=\; \alpha^1 p_1 + \alpha^2 p_2 + \alpha^3 p_3 + \beta\,m,$$

where $\alpha^i$ and $\beta$ are objects to be determined. Squaring (carefully — these are operators, ordering matters):

$$H^2 \;=\; \alpha^i\alpha^j\,p_ip_j \;+\; (\alpha^i\beta + \beta\alpha^i)\,p_i m \;+\; \beta^2\,m^2.$$

Demanding $H^2 = \vec p^{\,2} + m^2$ gives three constraints. The first term must yield $p_ip_i$, but $p_ip_j$ is symmetric in $(i,j)$, so only the symmetric part of $\alpha^i\alpha^j$ contributes — that's the anticommutator $\{\alpha^i,\alpha^j\} = \alpha^i\alpha^j + \alpha^j\alpha^i$. The middle term must vanish (else $H^2$ has linear-in-$p$ pieces). The last gives $\beta^2 = 1$. Together:

$$\boxed{\;\{\alpha^i,\alpha^j\} \;=\; 2\delta^{ij},\qquad \{\alpha^i,\beta\} \;=\; 0,\qquad \beta^2 \;=\; 1.\;}$$

This is a Clifford algebra — three mutually anticommuting matrices that all square to $1$, plus a fourth that anticommutes with all three and also squares to $1$. The Pauli matrices $\sigma^i$ already satisfy $\{\sigma^i,\sigma^j\} = 2\delta^{ij}$ in $2\times 2$, but in dimension $2$ there is no fourth matrix anticommuting with all three (the basis $\{1,\sigma^1,\sigma^2,\sigma^3\}$ is exhausted, and any candidate $\beta = a\,1 + b_i\sigma^i$ that anticommutes with every $\sigma^j$ forces $a = b_i = 0$). The minimum dimension where four mutually-anticommuting Hermitian matrices fit is 4, so $\psi$ must be a 4-component spinor:

$$\psi \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}\psi_1\\\psi_2\\\psi_3\\\psi_4\end{pmatrix},\qquad \alpha^i,\,\beta \in M_4(\mathbb{C}).$$

Dirac's wavefunction is no longer a single complex amplitude (Schrödinger) or a single Lorentz scalar (Klein-Gordon) but carries internal structure — four amplitudes per spacetime point. We will shortly discover that two of those amplitudes encode spin orientation, and the other two the antiparticle channel.

2. The Dirac equation in covariant form

Multiply $i\partial_t\psi = (\vec\alpha\cdot\vec p + \beta m)\psi$ on the left by $\beta$:

$$i\beta\,\partial_t\psi \;=\; \beta\vec\alpha\cdot\vec p\,\psi + \beta^2 m\psi \;=\; \beta\vec\alpha\cdot\vec p\,\psi + m\psi.$$

Define the four gamma matrices $\gamma^0 = \beta$ and $\gamma^i = \beta\alpha^i$. Substituting $\vec p = -i\vec\nabla$ and rearranging:

$$\boxed{\;(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\,\psi(x) \;=\; 0.\;}$$

This is the Dirac equation in manifestly Lorentz-covariant form. The Clifford algebra of the $\alpha^i,\beta$ translates to $\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}\,1_{4\times 4}$ with $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$. The Dirac matrices $\sigma^{\mu\nu} = \tfrac{i}{2}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]$ generate the spinor representation of the Lorentz group: $\psi \to \exp\bigl(-\tfrac{i}{4}\omega_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\bigr)\psi$. The fifth gamma matrix $\gamma^5 = i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ anticommutes with every $\gamma^\mu$ and squares to $1$; the projectors $P_{L,R} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1\mp\gamma^5)$ split $\psi$ into chiral components — the building blocks of the Standard Model's left-handed weak-interacting spinors.

The standard ("Dirac") representation in $2\times 2$ block form is

$$\gamma^0 \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \gamma^i \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^i\\-\sigma^i&0\end{pmatrix},$$

where $\sigma^i$ are the $2\times 2$ Pauli matrices. The Dirac adjoint $\bar\psi = \psi^\dagger\gamma^0$ is engineered so that $\bar\psi\psi$ is a Lorentz scalar and $\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$ a Lorentz vector. This single first-order equation is the relativistic equation of motion for every spin-½ field in the Standard Model — electrons, muons, taus, quarks (after gauge-coupling), and neutrinos (where the Majorana variant identifies $\psi$ with its charge conjugate).

3. Spin-½ from the Dirac equation

One of Dirac's first checks was: does this equation describe the electron, including its spin? Spin is not an input — it must emerge from the algebra. Compute the commutator of the orbital angular momentum $\vec L = \vec x \times \vec p$ with the Dirac Hamiltonian $H = \vec\alpha\cdot\vec p + \beta m$:

$$[L^i, H] \;=\; [\epsilon^{ijk} x^j p^k,\, \alpha^\ell p^\ell + \beta m] \;=\; i\epsilon^{ijk}\alpha^j p^k \;\ne\; 0.$$

Orbital angular momentum is not separately conserved. To find the missing piece, define the $4\times 4$ spin matrix

$$\Sigma^i \;=\; \begin{pmatrix}\sigma^i & 0\\ 0 & \sigma^i\end{pmatrix} \;=\; -\tfrac{i}{2}\epsilon^{ijk}\alpha^j\alpha^k.$$

A direct computation gives $[\Sigma^i/2,\, H] = -i\epsilon^{ijk}\alpha^j p^k$, exactly the negative of $[L^i, H]$. Therefore

$$\boxed{\;\vec J \;=\; \vec L + \tfrac{1}{2}\vec\Sigma\quad\text{commutes with } H,\;}$$

and is the conserved angular momentum on a Dirac spinor. The eigenvalues of $\Sigma^z$ on a rest-frame spinor $(1,0,0,0)^T$ or $(0,1,0,0)^T$ are $\pm 1$, so the spin operator $S^z = \Sigma^z/2$ has eigenvalues $\pm 1/2$ — a spin-½ particle. Spin-½ falls out of the algebraic structure of $H$, with no input beyond $H^2 = \vec p^{\,2} + m^2$.

The spinor representation of the Lorentz group $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ does not exist — the group is not simply connected. What does exist is the double cover $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3) \cong \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, the same group that acts on Weyl spinors via $2\times 2$ complex matrices of unit determinant. A $2\pi$ rotation in $\mathrm{SO}(3) \subset \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ lifts to $-1$ in $\mathrm{Spin}$; a Dirac spinor picks up a sign under a $2\pi$ rotation and only returns to itself under $4\pi$. This $4\pi$ periodicity has been measured directly in neutron-interferometry experiments (Werner, Rauch et al., 1975). Under a boost of rapidity $\xi$, the Lorentz transformation rotates the spinor's lower component into its upper with a rapidity-dependent angle — what the widget below visualises.

4. Positive-definite probability current

Now the payoff. The conserved current of the Dirac equation is

$$j^\mu \;=\; \bar\psi\,\gamma^\mu\,\psi,\qquad \bar\psi \;=\; \psi^\dagger\,\gamma^0.$$

Conservation $\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$ follows directly from the equation: multiply $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$ by $\bar\psi$ on the left, take the conjugate $\bar\psi(i\overleftarrow\partial_\mu\gamma^\mu + m) = 0$ and multiply by $\psi$ on the right, then add. The mass terms cancel and what remains is $\partial_\mu(\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi) = 0$.

The time component is

$$j^0 \;=\; \bar\psi\,\gamma^0\,\psi \;=\; \psi^\dagger\,\gamma^0\,\gamma^0\,\psi \;=\; \psi^\dagger\,\psi \;=\; |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + |\psi_3|^2 + |\psi_4|^2 \;\ge\; 0,$$

using $(\gamma^0)^2 = 1$. Pointwise positive — exactly the property Klein-Gordon's $j^0$ failed to have. Integrating gives the total probability $\int j^0\,d^3 x = \int \psi^\dagger\psi\,d^3 x$, conserved by $\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0$ and bounded below by $0$. The Born rule applies cleanly.

The widget below evaluates $\psi^\dagger\psi$ literally on a superposition $\psi = a\,u(p_+,\uparrow)e^{ip_+x} + b\,e^{i\varphi}\,v(p_-,\uparrow)e^{-ip_-x}$ of explicit 4-component $u, v$ spinors and shows it never dips below zero — while the Klein-Gordon $j^0$ on the same superposition does.

This was the original motivation for the Dirac equation. Klein-Gordon's $j^0$ involves $\partial_t\phi$, independent Cauchy data on a second-order PDE that can flip sign on a positive-and-negative-energy superposition. Dirac's first-order structure has only $\psi$ at fixed time, so $j^0$ is a positive-definite quadratic form. The 4-current $j^\mu$ is also gauge-invariant under global $U(1)$ phase rotation $\psi \to e^{i\alpha}\psi$ and transforms as a Lorentz vector. Promoting the global $U(1)$ to a local symmetry forces minimal coupling $\partial_\mu \to D_\mu = \partial_\mu - ieA_\mu$, producing QED.

5. Negative-energy sea and the prediction of antimatter

The Dirac equation does not solve every Klein-Gordon problem by fiat. Substituting a plane-wave ansatz $\psi = u(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}$ into $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$ gives the algebraic equation $(\gamma^\mu p_\mu - m)u(p) = 0$. Multiplying by $(\gamma^\nu p_\nu + m)$ and using the Clifford algebra reduces this to $(p^2 - m^2)u(p) = 0$ — the same mass-shell condition as Klein-Gordon. The energy spectrum is

$$E \;=\; \pm\sqrt{|\vec p|^2 + m^2},$$

with each branch 2-fold degenerate (spin $\pm 1/2$): two positive-energy spinors $u_+(p,s)$ and two negative-energy spinors $v(p,s)$ per momentum $\vec p$. The first-order structure does not eliminate the negative branch; it just turns the Cauchy problem into a single $\psi$-condition.

For a single Dirac particle this is fatal: an electron in a positive-energy state could emit photons and cascade down through the negative-energy continuum forever. Dirac's 1928 resolution — hole theory — relied on the Pauli exclusion principle: imagine all negative-energy states already filled with electrons (the Dirac sea). Pauli blocking prevents any positive-energy electron from radiating into a negative-energy state. The vacuum is not empty but is the filled sea.

Promote a sea electron to a positive-energy state: the result is a positive-energy electron PLUS a hole in the sea. The hole — a missing negative-charge negative-energy electron — looks like a positive-energy positive-charge particle. Dirac initially identified this with the proton (wrong: mass mismatch), then concluded by 1931 it was a new species: the positron. Carl Anderson found it in cosmic-ray cloud-chamber tracks in 1932. Antimatter was a mathematical prediction, confirmed within four years.

Modern quantum field theory drops the sea metaphor: $\psi$ is reinterpreted as an operator-valued field with creation operators $a_p^\dagger$ for electrons and $b_p^\dagger$ for positrons:

$$\psi(x) \;=\; \sum_s \int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\bigl[\,a_{p,s}\,u_s(p)\,e^{-ip\cdot x} + b_{p,s}^\dagger\,v_s(p)\,e^{+ip\cdot x}\,\bigr].$$

The "negative-energy sea" disappears as a literal picture, but the antiparticle prediction stands. (For bosons, where Pauli does not apply, the Stückelberg-Feynman reinterpretation we saw on the Klein-Gordon page plays the analogous role.)

6. Non-relativistic limit and Pauli equation

How does the Dirac equation reduce to non-relativistic quantum mechanics? Write the 4-spinor in $2\times 2$ block form $\psi = (\varphi, \chi)^T$ where $\varphi$ and $\chi$ are 2-spinors. In the standard Dirac representation, the Hamiltonian-form equation $i\partial_t\psi = (\vec\alpha\cdot\vec\pi + \beta m)\psi$ — with minimal-coupled momentum $\vec\pi = \vec p - e\vec A$ and added scalar potential $V = e\Phi$ — separates into

$$i\partial_t\varphi \;=\; \vec\sigma\cdot\vec\pi\,\chi + (V + m)\varphi,\qquad i\partial_t\chi \;=\; \vec\sigma\cdot\vec\pi\,\varphi + (V - m)\chi.$$

Factor out the rest-energy oscillation: $\varphi = e^{-imt}\tilde\varphi,\ \chi = e^{-imt}\tilde\chi$. The bottom equation becomes $i\partial_t\tilde\chi = \vec\sigma\cdot\vec\pi\,\tilde\varphi + (V - 2m)\tilde\chi$. In the non-relativistic regime, $|i\partial_t\tilde\chi| \ll m|\tilde\chi|$ and $|V| \ll m$; the dominant term on the right is $-2m\tilde\chi$, so

$$\tilde\chi \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2m}\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec\pi\,\tilde\varphi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\tilde\chi|/|\tilde\varphi| \;\sim\; |\vec p|/m \;\ll\; 1.$$

The lower 2-spinor is suppressed by the small parameter $|\vec p|/m$ — but it is not zero. Substituting back into the upper equation:

$$i\partial_t\tilde\varphi \;=\; \frac{(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec\pi)^2}{2m}\,\tilde\varphi + V\tilde\varphi.$$

Apply the Pauli identity $(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec a)(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec b) = \vec a\cdot\vec b\,1 + i\vec\sigma\cdot(\vec a\times\vec b)$. With $\vec a = \vec b = \vec\pi$, the cross-product piece gives $\vec\pi\times\vec\pi = -ie\vec B$, so

$$\boxed{\;i\partial_t\tilde\varphi \;=\; \biggl[\,\frac{(\vec p - e\vec A)^2}{2m} \;+\; \frac{e}{2m}\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B \;+\; V\,\biggr]\tilde\varphi.\;}$$

This is the Pauli equation (1927) — Schrödinger's equation augmented by an explicit spin-magnetic-moment coupling $+(e/2m)\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B$ (with $\vec\pi = \vec p - e\vec A$ and $e$ taken as a generic charge parameter; for the physical electron $e<0$, so the coefficient is negative and the term reads $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$). The magnetic moment of the electron is $\vec\mu = (e/2m)\vec\sigma = g(e/2m)\vec S$ with gyromagnetic ratio $g = 2$ — exactly. A classical spinning charged sphere would give $g = 1$; relativistic Dirac kinematics doubles this. QED loop corrections add $g = 2(1 + \alpha/(2\pi) + \ldots)$, the celebrated anomalous magnetic moment, verified to twelve decimal places.

Pauli wrote his equation in 1927 as a phenomenological extension of Schrödinger to accommodate spin; Dirac's equation, written one year later, derives the spin term from first principles — every algebraic constraint imposed by the Clifford structure paid dividends in physics.

Connections

The Dirac equation anchors the spin-½ half of the Standard Model. It is not the end of the story — adding gauge interactions promotes $\partial_\mu \to D_\mu = \partial_\mu - igT^a A^a_\mu$ and produces QED, the electroweak theory, and QCD; Majorana variants are studied for neutrinos; the curved-spacetime Dirac equation introduces the spin connection (the gauge field that couples spinors to spacetime curvature, $\omega^{ab}_\mu$ in standard notation) and probes the geometry of $\mathrm{Spin}$ structures on a manifold (an obstruction-theoretic concept that does not apply to scalars). The open frontier includes: the strong-CP problem, the Majorana-vs-Dirac neutrino question (awaiting neutrinoless-double-beta-decay experiments), and Dirac operators on lattices in topological-insulator condensed-matter physics.

  • Klein-Gordon equation — the previous step in the relativistic-wave hierarchy. Each component of a Dirac spinor satisfies $(\Box + m^2)\psi_a = 0$ as a consequence of $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$ multiplied by $(i\gamma^\nu\partial_\nu + m)$.
  • Special relativity — relativistic energy-momentum — $E^2 = p^2 + m^2$ is the constraint Dirac was linearizing.
  • Schrödinger equation — recovered as the non-relativistic limit, with the Pauli spin-magnetic correction as a free byproduct.
  • Representation theory — the Dirac spinor is the simplest non-trivial finite-dimensional representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3) = \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, decomposing as $(1/2,0) \oplus (0,1/2)$ in the Weyl basis.
  • Quaternions, octonions, and division algebras — the Pauli matrices are the imaginary quaternions, and the Clifford algebras generalize this to higher dimensions; the Dirac-Clifford algebra is $C\ell_{1,3}(\mathbb{R})$.
  • Lie algebras — $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ and root systems — $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3) \cong \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ has Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})$, whose complexification splits as $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C})_{\mathbb{C}} \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbb{C}} \oplus \mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbb{C}}$ (after complexification only — as a real Lie algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb{C}) = \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is non-compact and not isomorphic to the compact $\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2) = \mathfrak{so}(4)$), and the spinor reps are the fundamental reps of the two $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ factors.
