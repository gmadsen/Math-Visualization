Mapping class groups & 4-manifolds

Surfaces have a hidden symmetry group $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$ that organises hyperbolic structures, foliations, and moduli. In dimension four the smooth and topological worlds diverge — Donaldson and Casson supply the gauge-theoretic invariants that detect the difference.

1. The mapping class group $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$

Let $S$ be a connected oriented surface — possibly closed, possibly with boundary components or punctures. Write $\mathrm{Homeo}^+(S)$ for the group of orientation-preserving self-homeomorphisms (or, in the smooth category, $\mathrm{Diff}^+(S)$). Two such maps are isotopic when they are connected by a continuous path of homeomorphisms. The mapping class group is the group of isotopy classes:

$$\mathrm{Mod}(S) \;=\; \pi_0\!\left(\mathrm{Homeo}^+(S)\right) \;=\; \mathrm{Homeo}^+(S)/\!\sim_{\text{isotopy}}.$$

Equivalently $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$ is the group of components of the topological group of self-homeomorphisms; it is countable and, when $S$ is closed of genus $g\ge 1$, finitely presented. The smooth and topological versions agree on surfaces — Earle, Eells, and Schatz showed every isotopy can be smoothed — so we will not distinguish them.

Why isotopy and not just homotopy?

On surfaces, homotopy and isotopy of self-homeomorphisms turn out to coincide (Baer's theorem for the closed case, an Epstein-Earle-Eells statement in general), so $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g) = \pi_0\,\mathrm{Diff}^+(\Sigma_g)$ is also $\mathrm{Aut}(\pi_1\Sigma_g)/\mathrm{Inn}$ — the outer automorphism group of the surface group, restricted to those automorphisms that preserve orientation. This places $\mathrm{Mod}$ between two extremes: it is much larger than the orientation-preserving isometry group of any hyperbolic structure on $S$ (which is finite for $g\ge 2$), but much smaller than the wild $\mathrm{Out}(\pi_1)$ for an arbitrary group.

The Birman exact sequence

If $S$ is obtained from $S'$ by puncturing at a point $p$, there is a forgetful map $\mathrm{Mod}(S)\to\mathrm{Mod}(S')$ — extend a homeomorphism across the puncture. The kernel records "where did the puncture go" and is realised as the group of point-pushing maps: drag $p$ around a loop $\gamma\in\pi_1(S',p)$ and bring it back, leaving behind a map of $S$ supported on a neighbourhood of $\gamma$. Concretely:

$$1 \longrightarrow \pi_1(S', p) \xrightarrow{\;\text{push}\;} \mathrm{Mod}(S) \longrightarrow \mathrm{Mod}(S') \longrightarrow 1.$$

For $S' = \Sigma_g$ with $g\ge 2$ this presents $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$ as an extension of $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ by the surface group $\pi_1\Sigma_g$. The pure mapping class group $\mathrm{PMod}(S)$ — fixing each puncture pointwise — is the subgroup that intervenes when we keep track of marked points one at a time.

Take-away. $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$ is the discrete shadow of the (continuous) symmetries of $S$. It is large enough to carry rich combinatorics — generated by Dehn twists, classified by Nielsen–Thurston, acting on Teichmüller space — but small enough to be presentable by hand for low genus.

2. Dehn twists and generation

The simplest non-trivial mapping class on a surface is a Dehn twist. Pick an oriented simple closed curve $\gamma\subset S$ and a tubular neighbourhood $A$ of $\gamma$, identified with an annulus $S^1\times[0,1]$. The Dehn twist $T_\gamma$ is the homeomorphism that is the identity outside $A$ and on $A$ is

$$(\theta, t) \;\longmapsto\; (\theta + 2\pi t,\; t).$$

Cut along $\gamma$, give one side a full $360^\circ$ twist, and reglue. The result depends only on the isotopy class of $\gamma$, and $T_\gamma$ has infinite order in $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$ as soon as $\gamma$ is essential (not bounding a disk and not bounding an annulus to a puncture). The inverse $T_\gamma^{-1}$ twists the opposite way.

Lickorish–Humphries generation

How many Dehn twists do we need to generate $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$? Lickorish (1964) showed that $3g-1$ explicit twists suffice. Humphries (1979) sharpened this to a minimal generating set of size $2g+1$: a sequence $a_1,\dots,a_g$ of twists about disjoint non-separating curves cutting along the "long axis" of the genus-$g$ surface, plus $b_1,\dots,b_g$ about transverse curves, plus one more curve $c$ that picks up the last handle. Below the threshold $2g+1$, no set of twists can generate.

Three signature relations

Among the relations holding in $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$, three are the workhorses of every computation.

  • Braid relation. If $\gamma_1,\gamma_2$ intersect transversely in one point, then $T_{\gamma_1}T_{\gamma_2}T_{\gamma_1} = T_{\gamma_2}T_{\gamma_1}T_{\gamma_2}$. If they are disjoint, the twists commute.
  • Lantern relation. On a sphere with four holes (or its embedding in any larger $\Sigma_g$), four boundary-parallel twists equal three "interior" twists: $T_{x}T_{y}T_{z} = T_{a}T_{b}T_{c}T_{d}$, where $a,b,c,d$ are the four boundary curves and $x,y,z$ are three internal simple closed curves arranged in a triangle. Discovered by Dehn, popularised by Johnson; the sole tool for showing $H_1(\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g))=0$ for $g\ge 3$.
  • Chain relation. For a chain $c_1,\dots,c_k$ of simple closed curves with $c_i\cap c_{i+1}$ a single point and otherwise disjoint, $(T_{c_1}\cdots T_{c_k})^{2k+2} = T_{d_1}T_{d_2}$ when $k$ is odd (and a single $T_d$ when $k$ is even), where $d,d_1,d_2$ are the boundary curves of a regular neighbourhood of $c_1\cup\cdots\cup c_k$.

Wajnryb assembled exactly these relations into a finite presentation of $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ for all $g\ge 2$. Since then, alternative presentations (Matsumoto, Gervais) trade fewer generators for more relations, but the lantern is always present.

3. Nielsen–Thurston classification

What does a "generic" mapping class look like? Nielsen knew the answer for the torus already: every $A\in\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathrm{Mod}(T^2)$ is conjugate to one of three types — finite-order ($|\mathrm{tr}\,A|<2$), reducible ($|\mathrm{tr}\,A|=2$, $A$ preserves a slope), or hyperbolic / Anosov ($|\mathrm{tr}\,A|>2$). Thurston (1976) extended this to all $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$.

Theorem (Nielsen–Thurston). Every $\varphi\in\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ is exactly one of:
  1. Finite-order ("periodic"): $\varphi^k = 1$ for some $k\ge 1$. Realised as an isometry of some hyperbolic structure.
  2. Reducible: $\varphi$ fixes (setwise) the isotopy class of some essential multicurve $\Gamma\subset\Sigma_g$. Cut along $\Gamma$; on each component $\varphi$ acts as a smaller Nielsen–Thurston map.
  3. Pseudo-Anosov: there exist transverse measured foliations $\mathcal{F}^u$ (unstable) and $\mathcal{F}^s$ (stable) and a real number $\lambda>1$, the dilatation, such that $\varphi^*\mathcal{F}^u = \lambda\,\mathcal{F}^u$ and $\varphi^*\mathcal{F}^s = \lambda^{-1}\mathcal{F}^s$.

The dilatation $\lambda$ is an algebraic integer — in fact a Perron number — and is the spectral radius of the action of $\varphi$ on (a suitable model for) the singular train-track that carries $\mathcal{F}^u$. Its logarithm $\log\lambda$ equals the topological entropy of $\varphi$ and the translation length of $\varphi$ on Teichmüller space (in the Teichmüller metric). Pseudo-Anosov mapping classes are the surface analogue of Anosov diffeomorphisms of the torus, and like them they are everywhere expanding in one direction and contracting in the other.

Why the trichotomy?

Thurston's proof works on Teichmüller space. A mapping class acts on $\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g)$ as a biholomorphism of a complex manifold of dimension $3g-3$. This action either fixes a point (finite-order), or fixes a stratum of the Thurston boundary corresponding to a multicurve (reducible), or has a unique invariant axis along which it acts by translation in the Teichmüller metric (pseudo-Anosov, with dilatation factor $\lambda^2$ per period). The boundary $\partial\mathcal{T}$ is the space of projective measured laminations, a sphere $S^{6g-7}$ on which $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ acts in a way analogous to how $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ acts on $\partial\mathbb{H}^2$.

The smallest dilatations among pseudo-Anosovs on $\Sigma_g$ — Lehmer-style problems — are conjecturally bounded below by $1 + c/g$ for some absolute $c>0$. The minimum on $\Sigma_2$ is the largest root of $x^4 - x^3 - x^2 - x + 1$, $\lambda\approx 1.7220838$ (Lanneau–Thiffeault), tantalisingly close to Lehmer's number for the Mahler-measure problem.

4. Teichmüller space and the $\mathrm{Mod}(S)$ action

For $g\ge 2$, every closed surface $\Sigma_g$ admits hyperbolic structures — complete Riemannian metrics of constant curvature $-1$. Two such metrics are marked-equivalent when there is an isometry between them isotopic to the identity. The space of marked hyperbolic structures up to this equivalence is Teichmüller space $\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g)$.

Three constructions of $\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g)$ produce homeomorphic spaces:

  • Hyperbolic. Pairs $(g, \mathrm{marking})$ where $g$ is a hyperbolic metric and the marking is an isotopy class of homeomorphism $\Sigma_g\to(X,g)$. The forgetful map to $\mathcal{M}_g$ ignores the marking.
  • Complex-analytic. Marked Riemann surface structures on $\Sigma_g$, equivalent by uniformisation.
  • Algebraic / representation. Conjugacy classes of discrete faithful representations $\rho\colon\pi_1\Sigma_g\to\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ with quotient homeomorphic to $\Sigma_g$.
$$\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g) \;=\; 6g - 6, \qquad \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{M}_g \;=\; 3g - 3.$$

Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates make this dimension count concrete. Pick a pants decomposition $P = \{\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_{3g-3}\}$ — a maximal collection of disjoint simple closed curves cutting $\Sigma_g$ into $2g-2$ thrice-punctured spheres ("pants"). Each pair of pants is determined by the lengths of its three boundary components (hyperbolic geometry of pants is uniquely fixed by these). Gluing two pants along a common boundary $\gamma_i$ is parameterised by a length $\ell_i$ and a twist parameter $\tau_i\in\mathbb{R}$. Thus

$$(\ell_1,\tau_1, \dots, \ell_{3g-3}, \tau_{3g-3}) \;\in\; \mathbb{R}_{>0}^{3g-3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3g-3}$$

parameterises $\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g)$ as a real-analytic manifold homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^{6g-6}$.

The mapping class action and the moduli quotient

$\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ acts on $\mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g)$ by changing the marking: $\varphi\cdot[(g,m)] = [(g, m\circ\varphi^{-1})]$. The action is

  • Properly discontinuous — every point has a neighbourhood meeting only finitely many translates. This is what allows the quotient to be a sensible orbifold.
  • By isometries of the Weil–Petersson metric (a Kähler metric on $\mathcal{T}$, finite distance to the boundary).
  • By isometries of the Teichmüller metric (a complete Finsler metric, infinite distance to the boundary). On this metric, pseudo-Anosov classes act by translation along their Teichmüller axes.

The quotient $\mathcal{M}_g = \mathcal{T}(\Sigma_g)/\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ is the moduli space of Riemann surfaces of genus $g$, a complex orbifold of dimension $3g-3$. Its singular points are exactly the surfaces with non-trivial automorphism groups — for $g=2$, the hyperelliptic involution makes every point of $\mathcal{M}_2$ a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-orbifold point, while higher-genus generic curves have trivial automorphism group.

5. 4-manifold fundamentals

Surfaces are dimension 2. Three-manifolds, after Thurston and Perelman, are organised by geometric structures. Four-manifolds are the dimension where every classical tool fails simultaneously: the Whitney trick collapses, the smooth $h$-cobordism theorem fails, and the smooth and topological categories tear apart.

Freedman's topological classification

Let $X$ be a closed connected oriented topological 4-manifold. Its second homology $H_2(X;\mathbb{Z})$ is a free abelian group of finite rank $b_2$, and the cup product (Poincaré dual to intersection) defines a symmetric bilinear form

$$Q_X\colon H_2(X;\mathbb{Z})\times H_2(X;\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z}, \qquad Q_X(\alpha,\beta) = \langle \alpha\smile\beta, [X]\rangle,$$

the intersection form. By Poincaré duality $Q_X$ is unimodular: $\det Q_X = \pm 1$. Its key invariants are the signature $\sigma(X) = b_2^+ - b_2^-$ (signature of the form), the parity (even iff $Q_X(\alpha,\alpha)\in 2\mathbb{Z}$ for every $\alpha$, equivalently $w_2 = 0$), and one $\mathbb{Z}/2$ Kirby–Siebenmann invariant $\mathrm{ks}(X)$.

Theorem (Freedman, 1982). The map $X\mapsto (Q_X, \mathrm{ks}(X))$ is a bijection between closed simply-connected topological 4-manifolds and pairs (unimodular form, $\mathbb{Z}/2$) — with the constraint that $\mathrm{ks}=0$ when $Q_X$ is even. In particular every odd unimodular form occurs, and so does every even unimodular form (with both $\mathrm{ks}$-values).

The exotic phenomenon

Donaldson's diagonalisability theorem (1983) says that a definite (signature $= \pm b_2$) unimodular form realised by a smooth simply-connected 4-manifold must be diagonal over $\mathbb{Z}$ — i.e. $\langle \pm 1, \pm 1, \dots, \pm 1\rangle$. But Freedman's classification produces a topological 4-manifold for every definite unimodular form, including the famous $E_8$ form (rank 8, even, definite). So:

  • The Freedman manifold $M_{E_8}$ with $Q = E_8$ is a closed simply-connected topological 4-manifold which carries no smooth structure at all.
  • The smooth and topological categories agree in every dimension except $4$.
  • The standard $\mathbb{R}^4$ admits uncountably many distinct smooth structures (Taubes, 1987), each detectable only by gauge-theoretic invariants of compact pieces. No other $\mathbb{R}^n$ has any non-standard smooth structure.
  • The smooth $h$-cobordism theorem fails in dimension 4: there exist 5-dimensional $h$-cobordisms $W\colon X_0\rightsquigarrow X_1$ between smooth simply-connected 4-manifolds with $X_0,X_1$ homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic. The Whitney trick that resolves this in higher dimension cannot be carried out smoothly here.

6. Casson invariant and the Donaldson preview

Andrew Casson (1985) introduced an integer-valued invariant $\lambda(\Sigma)\in\mathbb{Z}$ of integral homology 3-spheres: oriented closed 3-manifolds $\Sigma$ with $H_*(\Sigma;\mathbb{Z})\cong H_*(S^3;\mathbb{Z})$. Despite living three dimensions below Donaldson's main targets, the Casson invariant is the simplest member of the gauge-theoretic family that culminates in Donaldson and Seiberg–Witten 4-manifold invariants.

Definition via $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ representations

Let $\mathcal{R}(\Sigma) = \mathrm{Hom}(\pi_1\Sigma, \mathrm{SU}(2))/\mathrm{conj}$ be the $\mathrm{SU}(2)$-character variety of $\Sigma$. The trivial representation is always there, and Heegaard-splitting $\Sigma = H_+ \cup_{F_g} H_-$ along a genus-$g$ surface presents

$$\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(\Sigma) \;=\; \mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(H_+) \cap \mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(H_-) \;\subset\; \mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(F_g).$$

For a generic Heegaard splitting, the two factors $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(H_\pm)$ are smooth Lagrangian half-dimensional submanifolds of the symplectic moduli space $\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(F_g)$ (Atiyah–Bott). Their intersection is a finite collection of signed points. Casson's invariant is

$$\lambda(\Sigma) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\#_{\mathrm{alg}}\bigl(\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(H_+) \cap \mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{irr}}(H_-)\bigr).$$

The factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ accounts for an $\mathrm{SO}(3)$-versus-$\mathrm{SU}(2)$ doubling. The result is independent of the Heegaard splitting and so an invariant of $\Sigma$ alone.

The surgery formula and Floer's lift

If $\Sigma$ and $\Sigma'$ differ by $\pm 1$-surgery on a knot $K\subset\Sigma$, Casson's surgery formula relates them:

$$\lambda(\Sigma_{+1/n}(K)) - \lambda(\Sigma) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta_K''(1),$$

where $\Delta_K(t)$ is the Alexander polynomial of $K$, normalised so $\Delta_K(1)=1$. This makes $\lambda$ explicitly computable from any surgery presentation. As a bonus: $\lambda \pmod 2$ equals the Rohlin invariant, so $\lambda$ refines Rohlin's $\mathbb{Z}/2$-invariant to a $\mathbb{Z}$-invariant.

Floer's lift. Taubes interpreted $\lambda(\Sigma)$ as half the Euler characteristic of an instanton homology theory $HF^*(\Sigma)$, whose chain complex is generated by irreducible flat $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ connections on $\Sigma$ and whose differential counts ASD instantons on $\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}$ — solutions to $F_A^+ = 0$ on a 4-manifold. This is the bridge: Floer's instanton homology of 3-manifolds and Donaldson's polynomial invariants of 4-manifolds fit together as a $(3+1)$-dimensional TQFT, and Casson's $\lambda$ is the Euler characteristic of the 3-dimensional half.

Where this leads. Casson and Donaldson together fix the picture sketched in gauge-theory § 7: ASD $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ moduli on 4-manifolds, instanton-Floer homology on their boundaries, Witten's relation between Donaldson and Seiberg–Witten invariants, and the unique-to-dimension-4 phenomenon of exotic smooth structures. Mapping class groups re-enter when one studies surface bundles over $S^1$ — every fibered 3-manifold is the mapping torus of some $\varphi\in\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$, with Casson's $\lambda$ depending on the Nielsen–Thurston type of $\varphi$.

Connections

Mapping class groups and 4-manifolds are the heart of low-dimensional topology, and the questions get harder fast. Open problems include: a positive answer to the smooth 4-dimensional Poincaré conjecture (is every smooth 4-manifold homeomorphic to $S^4$ also diffeomorphic to it?); the precise minimum dilatation in $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$; whether $\mathrm{Mod}(\Sigma_g)$ is linear (admits a faithful finite-dimensional representation) for $g\ge 3$.
