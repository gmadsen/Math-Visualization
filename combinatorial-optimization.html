Combinatorial optimization

Linear programming, network flows, and matchings, glued together by duality — every $\max$ over a discrete structure equals a $\min$ over a dual one. From the simplex method walking vertices of a polytope to LP relaxation feeding constant-factor approximation algorithms, the subject turns "find the best subset" into geometry.

1. Linear programming and the simplex method

A linear program in standard form asks

$\max\; c^\top x \quad \text{subject to}\quad Ax \le b,\; x \ge 0,$

where $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^m$, $c\in\mathbb{R}^n$, and the inequalities are entrywise. The set $P=\{x\ge 0 : Ax\le b\}$ is a polyhedron: an intersection of finitely many closed half-spaces. When $P$ is bounded, it is a polytope.

The first structural fact is the fundamental theorem of LP:

Theorem. If the optimum is finite, it is attained at a vertex of $P$ — a point where $n$ linearly independent constraints meet with equality.

Vertices are zero-dimensional faces; the linear objective $c^\top x$, restricted to any bounded face, is again linear, so its maximum sits at a corner. This converts a continuous optimization to a combinatorial search: among the $\binom{n+m}{n}$ basic feasible solutions (BFS), find the best one.

The simplex method (Dantzig, 1947) walks adjacent vertices in directions of strictly increasing objective. At each BFS we pick a non-basic variable with positive reduced cost ($c_j - c_B^\top B^{-1} A_j > 0$) to enter the basis, then a ratio test picks the leaving variable so feasibility is preserved. The algorithm terminates either because no improving entry exists (optimality, via complementary slackness) or because the ratio test signals unboundedness.

The Klee–Minty cube (1972) shows worst-case exponential pivots; the smoothed-analysis result of Spielman–Teng (2004) explains why simplex is empirically fast — random perturbations smooth the worst case to polynomial.

2. LP duality and min-max theorems

To every primal LP $\max\; c^\top x : Ax \le b,\; x\ge 0$ there is a dual

$\min\; b^\top y \quad \text{s.t.}\quad A^\top y \ge c,\; y \ge 0.$

Weak duality is one line: if $x$ is primal-feasible and $y$ dual-feasible, then $c^\top x \le (A^\top y)^\top x = y^\top A x \le y^\top b$. So every dual feasible $y$ certifies an upper bound, and every primal $x$ a lower bound, with no gap larger than $b^\top y - c^\top x$.

Strong duality (von Neumann, 1947) says the gap closes whenever either LP has a finite optimum: $\max c^\top x = \min b^\top y$. The optimal values match. This is special to linear constraints; convex programs can have a positive duality gap in general.

The combinatorial face of LP duality is a small zoo of min–max theorems:

max matching in bipartite $G$$=$min vertex coverKönig–Egerváry
max $s$–$t$ flow$=$min $s$–$t$ cutFord–Fulkerson / Menger
max payoff (row player)$=$min payoff (col player)von Neumann minimax
max edge-disjoint $s$–$t$ paths$=$min edge $s$–$t$ separatorMenger (edge form)

Each row is LP duality applied to a $\{0,1\}$-relaxation whose constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so the LP optimum is automatically integral (next section).

3. Network flows: max-flow / min-cut

Fix a directed graph $G=(V,E)$ with non-negative capacities $c:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$, a source $s\in V$, and a sink $t\in V$. A flow is $f:E\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ obeying

  • capacity: $0 \le f(e) \le c(e)$ for every edge $e$;
  • conservation: at every vertex $v\notin\{s,t\}$, total flow in equals total flow out.

The value $|f|$ is the net flow out of $s$ (equivalently, into $t$).

An $s$–$t$ cut is a partition $V = S\cup T$ with $s\in S$, $t\in T$; its capacity is the sum of $c(e)$ over edges from $S$ to $T$. Trivially, every flow value is bounded by every cut capacity.

Max-flow / min-cut theorem (Ford–Fulkerson, 1956). $\max_f |f| = \min_{S,T} \operatorname{cap}(S,T)$.

This is LP duality in disguise: the LP $\max\sum_{e\,\text{out of}\,s} f(e)$ subject to capacities and conservation has the cut-LP as its dual, and the constraint matrix (incidence + capacity) is totally unimodular, so the optimum is integral when capacities are integers.

The algorithmic side is the augmenting-path idea. Define the residual graph $G_f$: for each edge $e=(u,v)$ with $f(e)<c(e)$, add a forward residual edge of capacity $c(e)-f(e)$; for each edge with $f(e)>0$, add a backward residual edge of capacity $f(e)$. An augmenting path is any $s$–$t$ path in $G_f$; pushing flow equal to its bottleneck capacity along the path strictly improves $|f|$. When no augmenting path exists, the reachable vertices from $s$ in $G_f$ define a min cut, and we are done.

Choosing augmenting paths via BFS (shortest-edge-count) gives the Edmonds–Karp algorithm, whose $O(|V||E|^2)$ bound is the first proof that max-flow is strongly polynomial.

4. Matching and assignment

Given a graph $G=(V,E)$, a matching is a set of edges $M\subseteq E$ such that no two edges share a vertex. A maximum matching has the largest possible $|M|$. For bipartite $G$ with parts $L\sqcup R$, the problem reduces to max-flow on the network $s\to L\to R\to t$ with unit capacities — so the König–Egerváry theorem $\max|M|=\min|\text{vertex cover}|$ falls out for free from min-cut.

The algorithmic engine is augmenting paths. Berge's lemma: $M$ is maximum iff there is no $M$-augmenting path — a path that alternates non-matching / matching edges, starting and ending at unmatched vertices. Flipping such a path's edge-labels turns $M$ into $M'$ with $|M'|=|M|+1$.

The Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955) maintains feasible node weights $\alpha_u,\beta_v$ with $\alpha_u + \beta_v \ge w(uv)$ on a weighted bipartite graph, alternates augmenting-path searches on the equality subgraph (edges with $\alpha_u+\beta_v=w(uv)$), and adjusts weights when the search fails. Its $O(n^3)$ complexity solves the assignment problem (best matching by total weight) in polynomial time.

For general (non-bipartite) graphs, augmenting-path search is delicate because of odd cycles. Edmonds (1965) introduced the blossom contraction — shrink an odd cycle to a single super-vertex, recurse, then expand — giving the first polynomial algorithm for maximum matching in general graphs and the polyhedral description that defines this subject.

5. Integer programming and total unimodularity

An integer linear program (ILP) adds the constraint $x\in\mathbb{Z}^n$ to an LP. Unlike LP, ILP is NP-hard in general — 0-1 integer programming appears directly on Karp's original list of 21 NP-complete problems (reduced from SAT). But there is a rich class of polynomial integer programs, characterised by structure of the constraint matrix.

A matrix $A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}$ is totally unimodular (TU) if every square submatrix has determinant in $\{-1,0,+1\}$.

Theorem (Hoffman–Kruskal, 1956). If $A$ is TU and $b$ is integer, then every vertex of $\{x\ge 0 : Ax\le b\}$ has integer coordinates.

So when an LP relaxation has a TU constraint matrix, the LP optimum is automatically integral — no rounding needed. The canonical examples are:

  • Bipartite incidence matrices (vertex–edge incidence of a bipartite graph) are TU. Hence max bipartite matching is solvable by LP.
  • Network-flow constraint matrices (signed edge–vertex incidence of any directed graph) are TU. Hence max-flow has an integral optimum when capacities are integer.
  • Interval matrices with consecutive-ones property (each column has a contiguous block of ones) are TU.

The characterisation: $A$ is TU iff for every subset of rows there is a $\pm 1$ sign pattern making the column sums lie in $\{-1,0,+1\}$ (Ghouila-Houri). Failure of TU isn't fatal — the LP can still be a useful relaxation giving bounds and approximation guarantees (see § 7).

6. Polyhedral combinatorics

Given a combinatorial family $\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^E$ (matchings, spanning trees, stable sets, …) of a finite ground set $E$, its characteristic polytope is

$P(\mathcal{F}) = \operatorname{conv}\{\chi_F : F \in \mathcal{F}\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^E,$

where $\chi_F\in\{0,1\}^E$ is the indicator vector. Linear optimization over $\mathcal{F}$ — find $F$ maximising $\sum_{e\in F} w_e$ — equals max $w^\top x$ over $P(\mathcal{F})$, by the LP fundamental theorem.

The art of polyhedral combinatorics is to describe $P(\mathcal{F})$ by linear inequalities. When you find such a description, integer optimization over $\mathcal{F}$ becomes solving an LP — and the dual gives min–max combinatorial theorems for free.

Edmonds' matching polytope theorem. For a general graph $G$, the matching polytope $P_{\mathrm{match}}(G)$ is exactly

$\bigl\{x\ge 0 : \sum_{e\ni v} x_e \le 1\ \forall v,\quad \sum_{e\subseteq S} x_e \le \tfrac{|S|-1}{2}\ \forall \text{odd }S\subseteq V\bigr\}.$

The first family says "match each vertex at most once" — these alone suffice for bipartite graphs. The odd-set inequalities are the new content: every odd-sized vertex subset contains at most $\lfloor |S|/2\rfloor$ matching edges. There are exponentially many such inequalities, but they suffice.

Separation = optimization (Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, 1981). A polytope admits polynomial-time linear optimization iff it admits a polynomial-time separation oracle — given $x\notin P$, return a violated inequality. Combined with the ellipsoid method (Khachiyan, 1979), this gives polynomial algorithms for LPs with exponentially many constraints, as long as the separation problem is tractable.

For matching, separation amounts to finding an odd subset whose constraint is violated — solvable by a min-cut computation. Hence Edmonds' polynomial matching algorithm follows from ellipsoid + matching polytope description, an alternate route to blossoms.

Chvátal–Gomory cuts systematically tighten an LP relaxation: from any inequality $a^\top x \le b$ valid on $P$, infer the integer-rounded inequality $a^\top x \le \lfloor b\rfloor$, which is valid on $P\cap\mathbb{Z}^E$. Iterating closes the integrality gap; the Chvátal rank measures how many rounds are needed.

7. LP rounding and approximation algorithms

When the constraint matrix is not TU and the integrality gap is positive, LP relaxation still buys a great deal. Solve the LP, then round the fractional solution to an integer one, paying a controlled approximation ratio.

Vertex cover, 2-approximation. Given $G=(V,E)$ with vertex weights $w_v$, the IP is $\min\sum w_v x_v$ subject to $x_u+x_v\ge 1\ \forall(u,v)\in E$, $x\in\{0,1\}^V$. The LP relaxation drops $x\in\{0,1\}$ to $x\in[0,1]$. Solve the LP, then round: set $\hat x_v = 1$ if $x_v\ge 1/2$, else $0$. Every edge has $x_u+x_v\ge 1$, so at least one endpoint is $\ge 1/2$ — feasibility is preserved — and the cost at most doubles. This is the textbook 2-approximation; it remains the best known under UGC (Khot–Regev 2008).

Set cover, $\ln n$-approximation. The LP $\min\sum c_S x_S : \sum_{S\ni e} x_S\ge 1\ \forall e\in U$ has a famous logarithmic gap. Two algorithms achieve $H_n = 1+\tfrac12+\cdots+\tfrac1n \le \ln n + 1$: the greedy algorithm (Johnson 1974), and randomised rounding with repetition (Raghavan–Thompson 1987). The greedy matches the LP lower bound via the dual fitting analysis; randomised rounding shows the LP is tight up to log factors.

Max-cut, Goemans–Williamson (1995). Maximize the number of cut edges in $G$. The IP is quadratic, but a semidefinite relaxation lifts each vertex to a unit vector $v_i\in S^{n-1}$ with the cut counted as $\sum_{ij\in E}(1-v_i\cdot v_j)/2$. Round by a random hyperplane $r\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, assigning vertex $i$ to side $+$ or $-$ by $\operatorname{sign}(r\cdot v_i)$. The probability $(u,v)$ is cut is $\theta(u,v)/\pi$, where $\theta$ is the angle. Comparing $\theta/\pi$ to $(1-\cos\theta)/2$ gives the celebrated ratio $\alpha_{\mathrm{GW}} \approx 0.878567$, the best polynomial-time approximation under UGC.

Connections

Combinatorial optimization is the meeting place between linear algebra, polyhedral geometry, and graph algorithms. Almost every structural fact above is a special case of LP duality; almost every algorithmic improvement comes from finding a smaller polyhedral description of the integer hull. The frontier is approximation under hardness: which problems admit constant-factor approximation (vertex cover, max-cut, set cover) and which inherit super-constant gaps from PCP and the Unique Games Conjecture (e.g. max-coverage variants, label cover).

  • Spectral graph theory — Cheeger's inequality — the spectral half of expansion bounds; LP/SDP and spectral give two routes to the same min-cut intuition.
  • Probabilistic method — concentration inequalities — randomised rounding's correctness proofs are Chernoff / Lovász local lemma in disguise.
  • Complexity theory — NP-completeness — ILP is NP-hard; the line between polynomial (TU, matroid intersection) and hard (general ILP) is the central classification question.
  • Information theory — KL divergence — entropy-based proofs of LP duality bounds in coding/source theory share the convex-dual machinery.
  • Matroid theory — the greedy algorithm — matroid intersection generalises bipartite matching; the polyhedral description (matroid polytope intersection) is another TU-like miracle.
