Kalman filtering and state estimation

Track a hidden state through noisy measurements. Every Bayesian step is a Gaussian collapse; every prediction is an ellipse puffing up.

1. The estimation problem

You can't see the thing you care about. A satellite's true position, a circuit's hidden voltage, a stock's latent volatility — the state $x_t$ is hidden behind a noisy sensor reading $y_t$, and the sensor lies. The state itself moves under its own dynamics, which you only know up to a noise term. Estimation is the art of fusing what physics predicts with what the sensor saw, and keeping a calibrated sense of your own uncertainty as you go.

The linear-Gaussian setting writes both pieces as $$x_{t+1} = F\,x_t + w_t,\qquad y_t = H\,x_t + v_t,$$ with mutually independent zero-mean Gaussian noises $w_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)$ and $v_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)$. The matrix $F$ encodes the dynamics ("if I'm at $x$ now, where will I be next?"), $H$ encodes which projection of the state the sensor sees, and $Q$, $R$ encode how much you trust each side.

The miracle that makes Kalman work: if you start with a Gaussian prior over $x_0$, then every $p(x_t\mid y_{1:t})$ stays Gaussian forever. Two numbers per dimension — a mean and a covariance — describe your belief exactly, with no moment approximation.

2. Bayesian fusion of two Gaussians

Before tackling time, do the static problem: two independent witnesses report scalar Gaussian beliefs about the same quantity. Witness 1 says $\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)$; witness 2 says $\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)$. Bayes multiplies the two densities and renormalises. Both exponents are quadratic in $x$, so the log-posterior is

$$-\tfrac12\!\left[\frac{(x-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2}+\frac{(x-\mu_2)^2}{\sigma_2^2}\right] = -\tfrac12\!\left[\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_1^2}+\tfrac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\right)\!x^2 - 2\!\left(\tfrac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2}+\tfrac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2}\right)\!x\right] + \text{const}.$$

The coefficient of $x^2$ is the posterior precision (inverse variance); the coefficient of $-2x$ divided by twice the precision is the posterior mean. Read them off:

$$\frac{1}{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_2^2},\qquad \mu = \sigma^2\left(\frac{\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2}+\frac{\mu_2}{\sigma_2^2}\right).$$

Precisions add. The combined width is sharper than either input — even a hazy second witness narrows the picture. The combined mean is the precision-weighted average of $\mu_1,\mu_2$: the witness you trust more pulls the answer toward itself.

Two Gaussians multiply into a sharper Gaussian
Shrink either standard deviation; watch the posterior snap toward that witness.
This precision-weighted mean is the Kalman update. The rest of the algorithm pushes a Gaussian through linear dynamics so this same one-line trick can be reused step after step.

3. The 1D Kalman cycle — predict and update

Step the scalar state $x_{t+1}=a\,x_t+w_t$ forward, then observe $y_t = x_t + v_t$. Run the belief $\mathcal{N}(\hat x_t,\sigma_t^2)$ through two micro-moves:

StepMeanVariance
predict (no data)$\hat x_{t+1\mid t} = a\,\hat x_{t\mid t}$$\sigma_{t+1\mid t}^2 = a^2\sigma_{t\mid t}^2 + q$
update (with $y_{t+1}$)$\hat x_{t+1\mid t+1} = \hat x_{t+1\mid t} + K_{t+1}(y_{t+1}-\hat x_{t+1\mid t})$$\sigma_{t+1\mid t+1}^2 = (1-K_{t+1})\sigma_{t+1\mid t}^2$

The scalar gain is $K_{t+1} = \sigma_{t+1\mid t}^2/(\sigma_{t+1\mid t}^2 + r)$ — exactly the fusion formula in disguise, applied to "my prediction" and "the new observation." A noisy observation ($r$ large) sends $K\to0$ and the filter ignores $y$; a clean observation ($r$ small) sends $K\to1$ and the filter accepts $y$ wholesale.

Iterating predict + update settles the variance into a fixed point $\sigma_*^2$ that solves $\sigma_*^2 = (1-K_*)(a^2\sigma_*^2 + q)$ — the scalar steady-state Riccati equation (a generic name for the quadratic-in-$\sigma^2$ recurrences that govern covariance evolution in linear-Gaussian estimation and control). After that the filter runs in a regime where the predict-inflation and update-shrinkage exactly cancel.

The widget below specialises to $a=1$ (pure random walk) so the recurrence is easy to read; for $|a|<1$ the steady-state $\sigma_*^2$ would be even smaller (mean-reverting dynamics damp predict-inflation), and for $|a|>1$ the variance can blow up faster than the filter can shrink it. The qualitative pattern of breathe-and-settle is the same.

4. Vector form and the covariance ellipse

Lift everything to $\mathbb{R}^n$. The scalar variance $\sigma^2$ becomes a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ — same role, just symmetric and positive-definite instead of scalar (implementations often call this matrix $P$; both symbols mean the same thing, and the §6 widget exposes $P$ in its readout to match the convention used in tracking code).

The mean $\hat x$ is a vector; the covariance $\Sigma$ is a positive-definite matrix you can picture as an ellipse $\{x : (x-\hat x)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-\hat x) = 1\}$. The principal axes of that ellipse are the eigenvectors of $\Sigma$, and their lengths are the square roots of the eigenvalues.

The two moves become matrix moves:

$$\Sigma_{t+1\mid t} = F\,\Sigma_{t\mid t}\,F^\top + Q,\qquad \Sigma_{t+1\mid t+1} = (I - K_{t+1}H)\,\Sigma_{t+1\mid t}.$$

Read the prediction step geometrically: $F$ rotates and stretches the ellipse (the deterministic part of "where will I be next?"); then $+Q$ puffs it up isotropically (or anisotropically — $Q$ has its own shape). The update step is the dual move. The observation matrix $H$ picks out a subspace; the gain $K$ collapses the ellipse along that subspace by as much as the observation's precision allows.

A 2D belief stepped through predict + update
Predict puffs the ellipse; update slices it along the observed axis.

Watch the determinant of $\Sigma$ in the readout: it grows during predict (volume in state space spreads) and shrinks during update (the slice picks up information). Pick the $y$-axis to observe and the predict step's shear has more to fix; pick the $x$-axis and the cross-correlation is what gets collapsed.

5. The Kalman gain as optimal mixing

The matrix $K_t = \Sigma_{t\mid t-1}H^\top(H\Sigma_{t\mid t-1}H^\top + R)^{-1}$ is forced on you by an optimality principle. Hold the prior $\Sigma_{t\mid t-1}$ fixed and ask: among all linear update rules $\hat x_{t\mid t} = \hat x_{t\mid t-1} + L\,(y_t - H\hat x_{t\mid t-1})$ (the residual in parentheses is called the innovation), which $L$ minimises the trace of the posterior covariance? Differentiating in $L$ and setting the gradient to zero gives $L = K_t$ on the nose. Three estimation frameworks land at the same matrix:

  • MMSE (minimum mean-squared error) — pick $\hat x$ to minimise $\mathbb{E}\|\hat x - x\|^2$ given $y$.
  • MAP (maximum a posteriori) — pick the $\hat x$ that maximises $p(x\mid y)$; equivalently, minimises $\tfrac12(x-\hat x_{t\mid t-1})^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\cdots) + \tfrac12(y-Hx)^\top R^{-1}(\cdots)$.
  • WLS (weighted least squares) — stack the prediction $\hat x_{t\mid t-1}$ and the $m$-dimensional observation $y_t$ as a joint residual and minimise with the inverse-noise weight matrices.

This three-way agreement is special to the linear-Gaussian world — once the model is nonlinear or the noise is heavy-tailed, the three frameworks disagree and Kalman becomes a heuristic instead of an exact filter.

A concrete check: with prior $\Sigma_{\mathrm{pred}}=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0.5\end{pmatrix}$, $H=[1\;0]$, $R=0.25$, the innovation covariance is $H\Sigma H^\top + R = 1.25$ and $K = \Sigma H^\top/1.25 = (0.8,\,0)^\top$. The filter moves $\hat x$ by $0.8$ of the residual in the $x$-direction; the unobserved $y$-coordinate is unmoved because the prior was uncorrelated. After update, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{post}} = \mathrm{diag}(0.2,\,0.5)$ — the observed axis shrinks by the factor $R/(H\Sigma H^\top+R) = 0.2$; the unobserved axis is untouched.

The letter $R$ is overloaded across the section: here it is the observation-noise covariance; in LQR it is the control-effort cost matrix; in mathematical finance it can be the risk-free rate. They are conceptually different objects that happen to share the symbol — when reading LQG literature (the LQR + Kalman bundle) both $R$'s appear in the same equations, so context-switching is mandatory.

6. Tracking a 2D moving target

Lift to the running example that built the whole field. The constant-velocity (CV) model treats acceleration as white noise: state is $(p_x,p_y,v_x,v_y)$, dynamics over time-step $\Delta t$ propagate position by $v\Delta t$ and leave velocity nominally unchanged, with all uncertainty injected through the acceleration channel.

$$F = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & \Delta t & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & \Delta t\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The sensor sees only position, never velocity directly. Yet $\Sigma_t$ develops nonzero $(p,v)$ off-diagonal entries after a few steps — exactly because the filter has noticed that the residuals $y_t - H\hat x_{t\mid t-1}$ are correlated across time in a way consistent with a moving target.

Watch the off-diagonal coupling appear in one step. Take the 1D slice $(p,v)$ with $F=\begin{pmatrix}1 & \Delta t\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and a diagonal prior $\Sigma_0 = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_p^2,\sigma_v^2)$. Then

$$F\Sigma_0 F^\top = \begin{pmatrix}\sigma_p^2 + \Delta t^2\sigma_v^2 & \Delta t\,\sigma_v^2\\ \Delta t\,\sigma_v^2 & \sigma_v^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

The off-diagonal $\Delta t\,\sigma_v^2$ wasn't there a moment ago. Add the CV process noise $Q$ (which also has a $\Delta t^2/2$ off-diagonal — see the quiz) and the coupling deepens. Once the prior has that off-diagonal, the next position observation's update touches the velocity components too: that's mechanically how observing only positions improves the velocity estimate. Eliminating velocity from the picture by hand throws this signal away; that's the whole reason the Kalman tracker beats every naive position-only smoother.

A Kalman tracker following a wobbly trajectory
Press play. Crank $R$ and watch the ellipses balloon.

Two qualitative behaviours to play with:

  • Crank $R$ up — sensor goes noisy. The filter trusts its dynamic model more and the yellow trace becomes a smoothed version of the truth that lags the high-frequency wiggles in the observations.
  • Crank $Q$ up — process noise admits the model could be wrong. The filter trusts observations more and chases each measurement; uncertainty ellipses stay roughly the same size but the trace gets jittery.
The diagonal of $Q$ in this widget is structured as $q\cdot\bigl(\Delta t^3/3,\Delta t^3/3,\Delta t,\Delta t\bigr)$ with off-diagonal $q\Delta t^2/2$ couplings — the standard "noise on acceleration, propagated through the kinematics" choice for a CV model. That structure is why the velocity components stay correlated with positions through prediction.

Connections

Kalman is exact only in the linear-Gaussian box. The frontier is everything outside it — nonlinear dynamics (EKF, UKF), non-Gaussian posteriors (particle filters), partial observability with unknown parameters (joint state-parameter estimation), and the data-association problem (which measurement corresponds to which target). Variational and message-passing formulations recover Kalman as a special case of belief propagation on a Gaussian factor graph, opening the door to large-scale graphical-model inference.

  • Optimal control — Linear-quadratic regulator — LQR pairs with the Kalman filter to give LQG, the canonical separation-principle controller for noisy linear systems.
  • Stochastic calculus — applications — the continuous-time Kalman–Bucy filter is a Riccati ODE driven by an innovation process; the discrete recursion is its sampled version.
  • Mathematical finance — Itô diffusions — latent-volatility models for option pricing reduce to a Kalman update when the volatility-driving SDE is linearised around the current estimate.
  • Probability theory — conditional probability and Bayes — the entire filter is one repeated Bayesian update, made tractable by closure of the Gaussian family.
  • Convex optimization — gradient methods — the WLS view of the update recasts Kalman as a recursive quadratic optimisation step, the natural bridge to recursive estimation in machine learning.
