Deep learning theory

Why do deep neural networks work? Three questions — can they represent the target (approximation), can training find it (optimization), and does fitting the data control error on new data (generalization) — and the surprising answers that overturn the classical theory.

1. Universal approximation

Start with the most basic question: can a neural network even represent the function we want? A one-hidden-layer network with input $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $m$ hidden units, activation $\sigma$, and a linear output computes

$f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i\,\sigma(w_i\cdot x + b_i).$

The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko 1989 for sigmoidal $\sigma$; Hornik 1991 for any non-polynomial $\sigma$; Leshno et al. 1993 for the sharp condition) says these finite sums are dense in $C(K)$, the continuous functions on a compact set $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$, in the sup norm:

Theorem (universal approximation). Let $\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuous and not a polynomial. Then for every $g\in C(K)$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist $m\in\mathbb{N}$, weights $w_i$, biases $b_i$, and coefficients $c_i$ with $$\sup_{x\in K}\Big|\,g(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i\,\sigma(w_i\cdot x+b_i)\,\Big| < \varepsilon.$$

Two things to internalize. First, this is a statement about width, not depth: a single hidden layer suffices, but the number of units $m$ needed to reach accuracy $\varepsilon$ is the resource that must grow. Second, the hypothesis on $\sigma$ is tight — non-polynomial is exactly the condition. A polynomial activation would only ever produce polynomials of bounded degree, a finite-dimensional space that cannot be dense in $C(K)$.

The theorem is an existence result: it promises an approximant exists but says nothing about how large $m$ must be (it can be exponential in $1/\varepsilon$ for badly behaved targets) nor whether gradient descent can find the weights. Those are the optimization and generalization questions of the later sections.

Worked mini-example: a bump from two ReLUs

With $\sigma=\mathrm{ReLU}$, $\sigma(t)=\max(0,t)$, two units already build a one-sided ramp and four units a localized bump. The difference $\mathrm{ReLU}(t)-\mathrm{ReLU}(t-1)$ is a unit-height ramp that saturates; differencing two shifted ramps gives a plateau. Summing many narrow plateaus tiles any continuous profile — this is the constructive heart of the proof for $n=1$.

2. Depth & expressivity

Universal approximation says width suffices — but at what cost? The companion question is expressivity: how complex a function can a network of a given size compute, and why is depth worth more than width? The cleanest setting is the ReLU network, because every such network computes a continuous piecewise-linear function.

Each ReLU unit splits its input space with a hyperplane: it is linear on each side of $\{w\cdot x+b=0\}$. Composing and summing units partitions the input domain into linear regions, on each of which the whole network is an affine map. Counting these regions measures expressive power.

Region count: width vs. depth. A single layer of $m$ ReLU units on a $1$-D input creates at most $m+1$ linear pieces — the count grows linearly in width. But stacking $L$ layers can compose foldings: a layer that folds the line in half doubles the number of pieces of whatever it is applied to. So $L$ layers of width $w$ can produce on the order of $w^{L}$ regions — exponential in depth. (Telgarsky 2016 makes this a hard separation: there are functions a depth-$L$ network represents with $O(L)$ units that any depth-$O(1)$ network needs $2^{\Omega(L)}$ units to approximate.)

The mental image: each layer acts like a paper fold. One fold of a sawtooth doubles its teeth. After $L$ folds you have $2^{L}$ teeth from only $O(L)$ parameters — a depth that no shallow network can match without exponential width. This is the formal sense in which deep learning is more than wide learning.

Worked mini-example: the sawtooth

Let $T(x)=\mathrm{ReLU}(2x)-\mathrm{ReLU}(4x-2)$ on $[0,1]$ be the triangle (tent) map: it rises then falls, one peak. The $L$-fold composition $T^{\circ L}$ is a sawtooth with $2^{L-1}$ peaks, hence $2^{L}$ linear regions — yet it is built from $L$ layers of just two units each. A shallow ReLU net needs $\Omega(2^{L})$ units to match it.

3. The neural tangent kernel

How does training actually move the weights? In the infinite-width limit a startling simplification appears (Jacot, Gabriel & Hongler 2018). Linearize the network around its initialization $\theta_0$:

$f(x;\theta)\ \approx\ f(x;\theta_0)+\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0)\cdot(\theta-\theta_0).$

Gradient descent on the squared loss then moves $\theta$ only through this linear model, and the relevant geometry is captured by the neural tangent kernel

$\Theta(x,x')\ =\ \big\langle\nabla_\theta f(x;\theta_0),\ \nabla_\theta f(x';\theta_0)\big\rangle.$

Theorem (NTK regime). As the width $\to\infty$ with the standard parametrization, (i) $\Theta$ converges at initialization to a deterministic kernel $\Theta_\infty$ depending only on the architecture, and (ii) $\Theta$ stays constant throughout training. Consequently full-batch gradient descent on the squared loss drives $f$ to the kernel regression solution with kernel $\Theta_\infty$: $$f_\infty(x)=\Theta_\infty(x,X)\,\Theta_\infty(X,X)^{-1}\,y.$$

This is the lazy training picture: the weights barely move (each individual weight changes by $O(1/\sqrt{\text{width}})$), the network stays essentially equal to its own linearization, and the whole nonlinear apparatus collapses to a fixed positive-definite kernel. Training a wide net by gradient descent is kernel regression — and the kernel is an RKHS kernel, so the entire representer-theorem machinery applies.

The NTK is both a triumph and a limitation. It rigorously explains why overparameterized nets train to zero loss and generalize in a regime we understand. But real finite-width nets are not lazy — feature learning (the kernel changing during training) is exactly what the NTK linearization throws away, and is believed to be where deep learning's advantage over fixed kernels lives.

Worked mini-example: the NTK of a one-unit linear model

Take $f(x;w,a)=a\,(w\cdot x)$ with scalar $a$ and vector $w$. Then $\nabla_a f=w\cdot x$ and $\nabla_w f=a\,x$, so at init $(a_0,w_0)$, $$\Theta(x,x')=(w_0\cdot x)(w_0\cdot x')+a_0^2\,(x\cdot x').$$ It is a deterministic positive-definite kernel built from inner products — already a kernel before any training, exactly as the theorem predicts.

4. Non-convex optimization

The training loss $L(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i(f(x_i;\theta)-y_i)^2$ is non-convex in $\theta$ — it has saddle points, flat regions, and in principle bad local minima. Classical optimization theory offers no guarantee that gradient descent reaches a global minimum. And yet stochastic gradient descent reliably drives deep nets to (near) zero training loss. Why?

The modern answer is overparameterization: when the number of parameters far exceeds the number of data points, the loss landscape becomes benign. There are so many directions to descend that bad isolated minima essentially vanish, and the set of global minimizers forms a high-dimensional connected manifold. The sharp version uses the Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) condition.

PL condition $\Rightarrow$ linear convergence. $L$ satisfies the PL inequality with constant $\mu > 0$ if $$\tfrac12\,\|\nabla L(\theta)\|^2\ \ge\ \mu\,\big(L(\theta)-L^\star\big)\quad\text{for all }\theta.$$ This does not require convexity. Under PL with an $\ell$-smooth $L$, gradient descent with step $\eta=1/\ell$ converges linearly: $$L(\theta_t)-L^\star\ \le\ \big(1-\tfrac{\mu}{\ell}\big)^{t}\,\big(L(\theta_0)-L^\star\big).$$

The point is that PL replaces "the landscape is bowl-shaped (convex)" with the far weaker "the gradient is large whenever the loss is large." Overparameterized networks satisfy a PL-type condition in a neighborhood of initialization — essentially because the tangent kernel (§3) is positive-definite there, so the gradient cannot vanish until the loss does. That is enough for global linear convergence even though $L$ has no global convex structure.

SGD adds a twist: its gradient noise is anisotropic, larger in sharp directions, which biases iterates toward flat minima — empirically the ones that generalize. So the optimizer's noise is not just tolerated but is part of why the solution it finds is good.

Worked mini-example: width smooths the landscape

Fit $f(x;\theta)$ to two points with a one-unit model: the loss surface in the two weights has a curved valley with a saddle, and GD can stall. Add units: the global-minimum set inflates to a manifold, the saddles between basins disappear, and GD started anywhere flows straight down. The very thing that makes the model "too big" is what makes it easy to optimize.

5. The generalization puzzle & double descent

Here is the scandal. A deep net with millions of parameters can fit a training set of random labels to zero error (Zhang et al. 2017) — its capacity is effectively unbounded, its VC dimension astronomically larger than the sample size. The classical theory of §4 in statistical learning theory then predicts catastrophic overfitting: the uniform-convergence bound $R\le\widehat R+O(\sqrt{d/n})$ is vacuous (the bound exceeds $1$). Yet on real labels the same network generalizes beautifully. The classical capacity-control picture is simply the wrong lens.

The empirical signature of the modern regime is double descent (Belkin et al. 2019). Plot test error against model size:

  • Underparameterized ($p<n$): the classical U-shaped bias–variance curve. Test error falls, then rises as the model starts to overfit.
  • Interpolation threshold ($p=n$): the model has just enough capacity to fit every training point exactly. Test error peaks here — the fit is forced through every point with no slack, so the interpolant is wildly unstable.
  • Overparameterized ($p>n$): a second descent. With many interpolating solutions available, gradient descent's implicit bias (§6) selects a smooth, low-norm one, and test error falls again — often below the classical sweet spot.
Why the peak, then descent. At $p=n$ there is exactly one interpolant and it is determined by an ill-conditioned linear system: tiny label noise blows up. For $p>n$ the interpolant is underdetermined, so the learner gets to choose among many — and choosing the minimum-norm one (the next section) is a strong implicit regularizer. More parameters means more freedom to pick a simpler interpolant. Capacity and complexity come apart.

So "more parameters always overfits" is false in the interpolating regime. The right complexity measure is not parameter count but something like the norm of the learned solution, which can shrink as the model grows.

Worked mini-example

Random-feature ridgeless regression with $p$ features on $n=40$ noisy points reproduces the curve exactly: test error climbs as $p\to n$ (the Gram matrix becomes singular), spikes at $p=n$, then falls as $p$ grows past $n$ and the minimum-norm interpolant smooths out.

6. Implicit bias of gradient descent

Double descent leaves a question hanging: when many parameter settings fit the training data perfectly, which one does gradient descent pick? The answer is the key to generalization without explicit regularization — GD has an implicit bias toward special, low-complexity solutions.

Least squares: the minimum-norm interpolant

Consider an underdetermined linear system $Xw=y$ with $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}$, $p>n$, so infinitely many $w$ interpolate. Gradient descent on the squared loss started from $w_0=0$ stays in the row space of $X$ and converges to the unique minimum-$\ell_2$-norm solution:

$w_\star\ =\ \arg\min_{Xw=y}\|w\|_2\ =\ X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}y.$

No regularizer was written down — yet GD behaves as if it minimized $\|w\|_2$ subject to fitting the data. The reason is geometric: gradients of the squared loss lie in the row space of $X$, so iterates never leave $w_0+\mathrm{row}(X)$; the unique interpolant there is the min-norm one.

Separable classification: the max-margin solution

For binary classification on linearly separable data with the logistic or exponential loss, the loss has no finite minimizer — the optimizer pushes $\|w\|\to\infty$. But the direction converges (Soudry et al. 2018): $w(t)/\|w(t)\|$ tends to the $\ell_2$ max-margin (hard-SVM) separator

$\hat w\ =\ \arg\min\|w\|_2\quad\text{s.t.}\quad y_i\,(w\cdot x_i)\ge1\ \forall i.$

The unified message. Among the infinitely many zero-loss solutions, gradient descent silently selects the one of minimum norm (regression) or maximum margin (classification). This implicit regularization — not any explicit penalty — is a leading explanation for why overparameterized networks generalize: the optimizer's geometry, not the model's size, controls complexity.

Worked mini-example: $w_\star=X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}y$

Take $n=1$, $p=2$, the single equation $w_1+2w_2=5$. Infinitely many solutions; the minimum-norm one is found by $X^{\top}(XX^{\top})^{-1}y$ with $X=[1\ 2]$: $XX^{\top}=5$, so $w_\star=[1,2]^{\top}\cdot 5/5=[1,2]^{\top}$, giving $w_\star=(1,2)$, $\|w_\star\|^2=5$. Every other solution, e.g. $(5,0)$ with norm$^2=25$, is larger. GD from $0$ lands exactly on $(1,2)$.

Connections

The three pillars above — approximation, optimization, generalization — are individually understood in idealized limits (infinite width, linearly separable data, kernel regimes) but the finite-width, feature-learning regime where deep learning's real power lives remains an open frontier. Why feature learning beats fixed kernels, what the right capacity measure is, how architecture (convolutions, attention, residual connections) shapes the inductive bias, and whether a single theory unifies all three pillars are all actively researched. A beginner expecting a settled subject is often surprised that the most basic question — why does this work? — is still being answered.

  • Statistical learning theory — Rademacher complexity — the classical capacity bounds that go vacuous for interpolating nets, and the norm/margin-based refinements built to replace them.
  • Kernel methods and RKHS — reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces — the NTK is an RKHS kernel; infinite-width training is exactly kernel regression there.
  • Convex optimization — gradient and proximal methods — the gradient/SGD machinery, here pushed into the non-convex regime where PL conditions take over from convexity.
  • Probabilistic graphical models — variational inference — the Bayesian counterpart: weight-space posteriors, the evidence lower bound, and PAC-Bayes generalization bounds.
  • High-dimensional geometry — concentration of measure — the blessing of dimensionality behind why overparameterized landscapes are benign and random features concentrate.

Forward to come: feature-learning beyond the NTK, scaling laws, and the inductive biases of transformers and convolutional architectures — the questions that turn this from a collection of limiting theorems into a theory of why deep learning works.
