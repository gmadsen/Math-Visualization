Positivity and ample line bundles

Which line bundles embed a variety into projective space? The answer is a convex cone in $N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}$, and almost every theorem in algebraic geometry lives at one of its faces.

1. Divisors, line bundles, and the Picard group

Fix a smooth projective variety $X$ over an algebraically closed field $k$. A Weil divisor is a finite formal $\mathbb{Z}$-sum $D = \sum n_i [Y_i]$ of irreducible codimension-$1$ subvarieties $Y_i \subset X$. A divisor is effective if every $n_i \ge 0$. The most basic example is the principal divisor of a non-zero rational function: for $f \in k(X)^\times$,

$$\mathrm{div}(f) = \sum_{Y} \mathrm{ord}_Y(f) \cdot [Y]$$

where $\mathrm{ord}_Y(f)$ is the order of vanishing (positive at zeros, negative at poles). The total degree of $\mathrm{div}(f)$ on a smooth projective variety is always zero — what goes up must come down.

The Picard group is

$$\mathrm{Pic}(X) \;=\; \frac{\{\text{Cartier divisors}\}}{\{\text{principal divisors}\}} \;\cong\; \frac{\{\text{line bundles on } X\}}{\text{isomorphism}}.$$

The two pictures — divisors modulo linear equivalence, and isomorphism classes of line bundles — coincide on smooth varieties via $D \mapsto \mathcal{O}(D)$. Sections of $\mathcal{O}(D)$ are exactly the rational functions whose pole orders are bounded by $D$:

$$H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D)) \;=\; \{\, f \in k(X)^\times : \mathrm{div}(f) + D \ge 0 \,\} \cup \{0\}.$$

This is the dictionary that lets us trade divisor questions (where do these zeros and poles fit?) for line-bundle questions (how many global sections does this bundle have?). Every later definition in this page — ample, nef, big — is a property of the divisor class, equivalently of the corresponding line bundle.

2. Very ample, ample, and Serre's theorem

The fundamental tension in algebraic geometry: most varieties are intrinsic (gluings of affine pieces), but the powerful tools all live in projective space. A line bundle that embeds $X$ into $\mathbb{P}^N$ is a bridge between the two.

Definition (very ample). A line bundle $L$ on a projective variety $X$ is very ample if the global sections $s_0, \ldots, s_N \in H^0(X, L)$ have no common zero, and the resulting map

$$\varphi_L\colon X \to \mathbb{P}^N, \qquad x \mapsto [s_0(x) : \cdots : s_N(x)]$$

is a closed embedding.

Two pieces of geometric content are folded into "closed embedding": the map separates points (no two distinct $x, y$ share image) and separates tangent vectors (the differential is injective at every point). Both of these can fail for a line bundle that has lots of sections — what is needed is enough sections, and enough generic ones.

Definition (ample). $L$ is ample if $L^{\otimes n}$ is very ample for some $n \ge 1$.

Why the relaxation? Because very ampleness is genuinely fragile — even on an elliptic curve $E$, a degree-$1$ line bundle is ample but not very ample. (By Riemann–Roch, $h^0 = 1$ in degree $1$, so there are not even enough sections to define a map; degree $2$ gives the $2:1$ cover of $\mathbb{P}^1$ and still cannot separate $P, -P$ — you need degree $\ge 3$.) Replacing $L$ by $L^{\otimes n}$ for $n$ large enough always cures the problem. The flexibility makes ampleness the right invariant: it is both intrinsic to the line bundle and stable under operations like tensoring with a power.

Serre's theorem: ampleness as a cohomological condition

The first miracle is that ampleness — defined geometrically by embeddings — is purely cohomological:

Serre's theorem. Let $X$ be projective. A line bundle $L$ on $X$ is ample if and only if for every coherent sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$, there exists $n_0 = n_0(\mathcal{F})$ such that

$$H^i(X, \mathcal{F} \otimes L^{\otimes n}) \;=\; 0 \quad \text{for all } i > 0 \text{ and all } n \ge n_0.$$

Equivalently, $\mathcal{F} \otimes L^{\otimes n}$ is generated by global sections for $n \gg 0$.

This is a profound restatement. Ampleness is not really about how many sections $L$ has — it is about whether twisting any sheaf by enough copies of $L$ kills its higher cohomology. The proof goes by induction: ample line bundles let you build an exact sequence for any $\mathcal{F}$ that resolves it by direct sums of $L^{\otimes -m}$, and the cohomology of those is computable on $\mathbb{P}^N$ via the embedding.

3. Nakai–Moishezon and the ample cone

Serre's theorem is striking but not computable: to verify ampleness via "all coherent $\mathcal{F}$ have eventually-vanishing higher cohomology" you have to check infinitely many sheaves. The Nakai–Moishezon criterion replaces this with a finite, computable test based on intersection numbers.

Nakai–Moishezon criterion. Let $X$ be a projective variety and $L$ a line bundle on $X$. Then $L$ is ample if and only if

$$L^{\dim Y} \cdot Y \;>\; 0 \qquad \text{for every irreducible subvariety } Y \subseteq X \text{ of positive dimension.}$$

Read the formula carefully: $L^{\dim Y}$ is the $(\dim Y)$-fold self-intersection, and $L^{\dim Y} \cdot Y$ is its restriction to $Y$ (equivalently, the top self-intersection of $L|_Y$ on $Y$). On $X$ itself this is $L^{\dim X} > 0$; on a curve $C \subset X$ it is the degree $\deg_C L|_C > 0$; on a divisor $D \subset X$ it is $L^{\dim X - 1} \cdot D > 0$, and so on.

So: ampleness is checkable in linear algebra. The intersection products live in the Chow ring (or, for $X = \mathbb{P}^N$, in $\mathbb{Z}[h]/(h^{N+1})$), and the criterion just says all the relevant entries are positive.

The ample cone and the Néron–Severi group

Two divisors $D_1, D_2$ are numerically equivalent ($D_1 \equiv D_2$) if $D_1 \cdot C = D_2 \cdot C$ for every curve $C$. The quotient

$$N^1(X) \;=\; \mathrm{Pic}(X) / \!\equiv$$

is the Néron–Severi group. The Néron–Severi theorem says it has finite rank as an abelian group; its rank $\rho(X)$ is the Picard number. Tensor with $\mathbb{R}$ to get a finite-dimensional real vector space $N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}$ in which we can do convex geometry.

Inside $N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}$ sits the ample cone:

$$\mathrm{Amp}(X) \;=\; \{\, [L] \in N^1(X)_\mathbb{R} : L \text{ ample}\,\}.$$

By Nakai–Moishezon, this is the locus where every "test polynomial" $L^{\dim Y} \cdot Y$ is strictly positive — an open convex cone. Real ampleness questions ("is this $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor in the closure of the ample cone?") become questions about a finite-dimensional polyhedral or semi-algebraic region.

On a blown-up surface, or a more general projective variety, the ample cone takes more interesting shape — sometimes only piecewise polyhedral, sometimes round. The Mori program below will give us systematic tools for tracking it.

4. Nef, big, and the cone of curves

Two relaxations of ample sit just outside the ample cone — and both are essential to the modern toolkit.

Definition (nef). A line bundle $L$ on a projective $X$ is nef ("numerically eventually free") if $L \cdot C \ge 0$ for every irreducible curve $C \subset X$.

Definition (big). $L$ is big if there exist $c > 0$ and $m_0$ with $h^0(X, L^{\otimes m}) \ge c \cdot m^{\dim X}$ for all $m \ge m_0$.

Bigness measures whether $L$ has the maximal-possible growth rate of sections — the same growth rate as an ample line bundle. Equivalently, by Kodaira's lemma, $L$ is big iff $L \equiv A + E$ for some ample $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $A$ and effective $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor $E$.

Inside $N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}$ we now have a chain of cones

$$\mathrm{Amp}(X) \;\subseteq\; \mathrm{Nef}(X) \;\subseteq\; N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}, \qquad \mathrm{Amp}(X) \;\subseteq\; \mathrm{Big}(X) \;\subseteq\; N^1(X)_\mathbb{R}.$$

Kleiman's theorem and the Mori cone

The first deep structural result is that nef is exactly the topological closure of ample:

Kleiman (1966). $\mathrm{Nef}(X) = \overline{\mathrm{Amp}(X)}$, and $\mathrm{Amp}(X) = \mathrm{int}\,\mathrm{Nef}(X)$. Equivalently, $L$ is nef iff $L$ is the limit of ample $\mathbb{Q}$-classes.

The second is the duality. In $N_1(X)_\mathbb{R}$, the dual space, sits the Mori cone $\overline{NE}(X)$: the closed convex cone generated by classes of irreducible curves. The intersection pairing

$$N^1(X)_\mathbb{R} \times N_1(X)_\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad ([L], [C]) \mapsto L \cdot C$$

identifies them via a perfect pairing.

Kleiman duality. $\mathrm{Nef}(X)$ is the dual cone of $\overline{NE}(X)$:

$$\mathrm{Nef}(X) \;=\; \{\, [L] : L \cdot \alpha \ge 0 \text{ for all } \alpha \in \overline{NE}(X)\,\}.$$

This duality is the bridge between divisors (codimension $1$) and curves (dimension $1$). Mori's contraction theorem then says that for $X$ smooth (or, more generally, klt) projective, the $K_X$-negative extremal rays of $\overline{NE}(X)$ admit geometric contractions, and these are the elementary moves of the minimal model program: for each extremal ray you contract a curve class, producing a new variety that is one step closer to "as positive as possible."

5. Kodaira embedding theorem

So far everything has been algebraic: divisors, intersection numbers, cohomology over a field. But the deepest justification of the ample = positive equation comes from complex geometry. Kodaira's embedding theorem is the bridge.

Let $X$ be a compact complex manifold and $L$ a holomorphic line bundle equipped with a Hermitian metric $h$. The curvature form

$$\Theta(L, h) \;=\; -\partial\bar\partial \log h \;\in\; A^{1,1}(X)$$

is a real $(1,1)$-form. We say $(L, h)$ is positive if $\frac{i}{2\pi}\Theta(L, h)$ is a positive $(1,1)$-form — i.e., its associated Hermitian form is positive-definite at every point.

A compact complex manifold $X$ is Kähler if it admits a Hermitian metric whose associated $(1,1)$-form $\omega$ is closed. The Kähler condition is mild: every projective complex manifold is Kähler (the Fubini–Study form restricts to one). The remarkable converse is the question Kodaira's theorem answers:

Kodaira embedding (1954). Let $X$ be a compact complex manifold. Then $X$ is projective if and only if $X$ admits a positive line bundle $L$. Concretely, for any positive $L$ and $n$ sufficiently large, the map

$$\varphi_{L^{\otimes n}}\colon X \to \mathbb{P}\bigl(H^0(X, L^{\otimes n})^\vee\bigr), \qquad x \mapsto [s \mapsto s(x)]$$

is a holomorphic embedding.

Two consequences are worth pulling out. First, positive line bundle = ample line bundle on a projective manifold. The differential-geometric notion (curvature is a Kähler form) and the algebraic notion (some power has enough sections to embed) coincide. Second, Kodaira's theorem characterizes which Kähler manifolds are projective: those for which some integral Kähler class can be realized as the Chern class of a holomorphic line bundle.

The case of complex tori

The example is famously sharp. A complex torus $X = \mathbb{C}^g/\Lambda$ is automatically Kähler (the flat Hermitian metric on $\mathbb{C}^g$ descends). But $X$ is projective iff $\Lambda$ admits a Riemann form: a positive-definite Hermitian form on $\mathbb{C}^g$ whose imaginary part takes integer values on $\Lambda \times \Lambda$.

  • For $g = 1$, every lattice admits a Riemann form: every elliptic curve is projective (and the Weierstrass embedding is the explicit Kodaira map).
  • For $g \ge 2$, generic complex tori have no Riemann form. They are Kähler but not projective.
  • The ones that are projective — abelian varieties — form a meager subset of all complex tori, but they are exactly the ones with arithmetic and algebro-geometric content.

Kodaira's theorem is the deepest reason "ample" is the right notion. Algebraic ampleness was defined by counting sections; complex-analytic positivity was defined by curvature; both measure the same thing. The proof, originally Kodaira's, runs through the vanishing theorem of the next section: a positive line bundle has enough sections precisely because its higher cohomology can be killed.

6. Kodaira and Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing

The technical engine that makes ampleness do its work is a vanishing theorem for higher cohomology. Serre's theorem said the cohomology of $\mathcal{F} \otimes L^{\otimes n}$ vanishes for $n \gg 0$, but did not say which $n$. Kodaira's theorem replaces "$n \gg 0$" with "$n = 1$" — at the price of a single twist by the canonical bundle.

Kodaira vanishing (1953). Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety over $\mathbb{C}$ of dimension $d$, with canonical bundle $K_X = \det \Omega^1_X$. If $L$ is an ample line bundle on $X$, then

$$H^i(X, K_X \otimes L) \;=\; 0 \quad \text{for all } i > 0.$$

The factor of $K_X$ is essential. Without it, the cohomology $H^i(X, L)$ can be non-zero even for line bundles that are positive in a weaker sense — e.g. on an elliptic curve $E$, the trivial bundle $\mathcal{O}_E$ is nef (and even base-point-free) yet $H^1(E, \mathcal{O}_E) \cong \mathbb{C}$, so dropping the $K_X$ twist breaks vanishing already on the simplest non-rational curve. Kodaira vanishing says: tilt by $K_X$ first, then everything above $H^0$ vanishes for ample twists.

The proof, in modern form, uses Hodge theory: harmonic representatives of $K_X \otimes L$-classes at degree $i$ would have to be holomorphic $(d, i)$-forms with values in $L$, but ampleness of $L$ rules these out via a Bochner-style positivity argument on the curvature. The argument fails — and the theorem fails — in positive characteristic. Raynaud constructed counterexamples in characteristic $p$.

Kawamata–Viehweg: the workhorse of MMP

Kodaira vanishing's killer feature in algebraic geometry is that it works for nef and big line bundles, not just ample ones. This relaxation is the entire reason the minimal model program is even possible.

Kawamata–Viehweg (1982). Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety over $\mathbb{C}$ and $L$ a $\mathbb{Q}$-divisor on $X$. If $L$ is nef and big, then

$$H^i(X, K_X + \lceil L \rceil) \;=\; 0 \quad \text{for all } i > 0.$$

More generally, the same holds when the fractional part of $L$ has simple normal crossings support (allowing klt singularities downstream).

Why is this so much more useful than Kodaira? Because the basic operations of the MMP — contracting an extremal ray, performing a flip — preserve nef-ness of certain natural divisors but generally destroy ampleness. Without Kawamata–Viehweg, the cohomological control needed to push the program through every step would be lost.

The applications cascade. Kodaira's vanishing immediately implies: surjectivity of restriction maps for ample line bundles (reading off the long exact sequence of $0 \to L \otimes \mathcal{O}(-D) \to L \to L|_D \to 0$); base-point-freeness for sufficiently positive $L$ (Reider's theorem on surfaces, Fujita conjecture in higher dimensions); and the entire engine of the MMP, where every contraction step is justified by a vanishing application that controls how cohomology behaves under birational moves.

7. Connections

Positivity is the gate from cohomology to geometry. Once you know which line bundles are ample, you know which varieties are projective; once you know the ample cone, you know which embeddings exist; and once you have vanishing, you have the cohomological tools that make the minimal model program go. The frontier today is what happens at the boundary — birational geometry of the boundary of the nef and big cones (the foundational language of the MMP) and how positivity behaves under reduction modulo $p$, where Kodaira vanishing breaks and the geometry is still being mapped out.

  • Sheaf cohomology — Picard groups and Serre duality signals — the cohomological interpretation of $\mathrm{Pic}(X)$ that powers Serre's theorem.
  • Intersection theory — Intersection product — the Nakai–Moishezon criterion lives in $A^*(X)$, and the ample cone is a convex region defined by intersection-positivity inequalities.
  • Algebraic curves: higher genus — The canonical embedding — for $g \ge 2$ non-hyperelliptic curves, the canonical bundle $K_C$ is very ample, and $|K_C|$ realises the most natural projective model.
  • Hodge theory — Hodge decomposition — Kodaira's positivity criterion lives downstream of Hodge symmetry; the proof of Kodaira vanishing uses harmonic representatives.
  • Moduli spaces — projective embeddings of moduli spaces are the engine of the GIT construction; ample line bundles on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$ are an active research frontier.
