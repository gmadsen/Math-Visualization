Condensed mathematics

Clausen and Scholze's foundational reset: replace topological abelian groups (which fail to form an abelian category) with sheaves on the pro-étale site of a point. The result — condensed, solid, and liquid theories — gives algebraic topology, $p$-adic Hodge theory, and functional analysis a single derived-category home, climaxing in the formally verified Liquid Tensor Experiment.

1. Condensed sets: sheaves on the pro-étale site of a point

Topological spaces are awkward as a categorical foundation. Quotients fail to be Hausdorff, products with infinite factors are too coarse, the category of topological abelian groups is not abelian — surjections like $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{disc}} \to \mathbb{Z}_p$ have zero cokernel on the underlying group level but a nontrivial topology, so the first isomorphism theorem breaks. Clausen and Scholze (2018–2019) propose to replace $\mathbf{Top}$ with a category of sheaves on a Grothendieck site whose covers are designed to make exactly the homological algebra one wants work.

Let $*$ be a point and consider its pro-étale site: objects are extremally disconnected profinite sets (Stonean spaces — compact Hausdorff totally disconnected spaces in which the closure of every open set is open), morphisms are continuous maps, covers are finite jointly-surjective families. A condensed set is a sheaf on this site — equivalently a presheaf $X\colon \mathrm{ExtrDisc}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathrm{Set}$ satisfying the descent condition

$$X(S \sqcup T) \;=\; X(S) \times X(T)$$

and similarly for nonempty profinite covers (which one reduces to the sum case using extremal disconnectedness). Up to set-theoretic size issues, this is the same data as a sheaf for the pro-étale topology on $*$.

Topological spaces $T$ embed into condensed sets via the underlying-condensed-set functor

$$\underline T(S) \;:=\; \mathrm{Cont}(S, T),$$

the set of continuous maps from a profinite $S$ into $T$. The functor $T \mapsto \underline T$ is fully faithful on compactly generated weak Hausdorff spaces — the standard convenient category — so condensed sets are a genuine enlargement of $\mathbf{CGWH}$ rather than a replacement that drops information. Discrete sets sit inside as locally constant sheaves; profinite sets sit inside as the representables themselves.

Why extremally disconnected and not arbitrary profinite? Two reasons: (i) every profinite set has a surjection from an extremally disconnected one (the Stone–Čech compactification of its underlying discrete set works), so the smaller site has the same sheaves; (ii) on extremally disconnected $S$ the sheaf condition trivializes for finite covers (because Stonean spaces are projective in compact Hausdorff), so one only needs the disjoint-union axiom. The category $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Set})$ has all small limits and colimits, is Cartesian closed, and — modulo a single set-theoretic universe choice — is locally small.

2. Condensed abelian groups and homological algebra

Apply the same recipe to abelian groups: a condensed abelian group is a sheaf $M\colon \mathrm{ExtrDisc}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathrm{Ab}$ with the same descent axiom $M(S \sqcup T) = M(S) \oplus M(T)$. The category $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$ has two crucial properties that topological abelian groups never had:

  • It is abelian in the strict sense. Kernels, cokernels, images, and coimages all exist and the canonical map $\mathrm{coim}\,f \to \mathrm{im}\,f$ is an isomorphism for every morphism $f$. The first isomorphism theorem holds verbatim. Snake lemma, five lemma, and long exact sequence all carry over from $\mathrm{Ab}$.
  • It has enough projectives and injectives. Sheafification of the free-module functor on extremally disconnected sets supplies projectives; an injective-resolution argument analogous to Grothendieck's tohoku theorem (every Grothendieck abelian category has enough injectives) supplies injectives.

The contrast with topological abelian groups is sharp. Topological $\mathrm{Ab}$ is only quasi-abelian in Schneiders's sense: it has kernels and cokernels, but the canonical comparison $\mathrm{coim}\,f \to \mathrm{im}\,f$ is generally not an iso. The standard counterexample: the inclusion $\mathbb{Q} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ as topological abelian groups has trivial kernel (so coimage is $\mathbb{Q}$ with the subspace topology) and non-closed dense image (so image is $\mathbb{R}$); the comparison from coimage to image is injective with dense, non-closed image — not an isomorphism. In $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$ this pathology disappears because the underlying functor records only the descent data, not a fixed point-set topology.

Topological subcategories embed cleanly: condensed Banach spaces, condensed locally compact abelian groups, and condensed finite-dimensional vector spaces are full subcategories. On Banach spaces the embedding is fully faithful as condensed abelian groups, and $\mathrm{Ext}^*$ in $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$ recovers continuous group cohomology. On locally compact abelian groups one recovers Pontryagin duality at the derived level — the duality $M \mapsto \underline{\mathrm{Hom}}(M, \mathbb{T})$ is exact in $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$ where it was only a left-exact mess in topological $\mathrm{Ab}$.

Why this matters for downstream theory: every classical homological-algebra technique — derived functors, spectral sequences, Tor and Ext computations, six-functor formalism — runs on $D(\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab}))$ without the case-by-case "is the limit complete? is the quotient Hausdorff?" hypotheses that bedevil functional analysis. The price is one universe-size axiom (Grothendieck universes for the site).

3. Solid abelian groups: the closed monoidal subcategory

Inside $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$ Clausen and Scholze isolate a full subcategory tailored to completed arithmetic and $p$-adic algebra: the solid abelian groups. Define the free condensed abelian group on a profinite set $S$ as $\mathbb{Z}[S]$ (the sheafification of the presheaf $T \mapsto \bigoplus_{S(T)} \mathbb{Z}$). One has a canonical comparison map

$$\alpha_S\colon \mathbb{Z}[S] \otimes M \;\longrightarrow\; \mathrm{Maps}(S, M)$$

from the levelwise tensor product to the condensed mapping object. A condensed abelian group $M$ is solid if $\alpha_S$ is an isomorphism for every profinite $S$ — equivalently, the completed tensor product $\widehat{\bigoplus}_{S}\mathbb{Z}\,\widehat\otimes\, M$ agrees with the genuine continuous-functions object.

The solidity condition picks out exactly those condensed abelian groups whose values on profinite probes reflect a complete topology in the appropriate sense. Concrete examples that are solid:

  • Every profinite abelian group ($\mathbb{Z}_p$, $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, finite groups, Galois groups);
  • Formal-power-series rings $\mathbb{Z}[[x]], \mathbb{Z}_p[[x]]$ as condensed abelian groups;
  • Mittag-Leffler $\lim$'s of solid groups (closure under sequential limits with surjective transitions);
  • $\mathbb{Q}_p, \mathbb{C}_p$ — the field of $p$-adic numbers and its completed algebraic closure.

And examples that are not solid:

  • $\mathbb{R}$ as a condensed abelian group (it has a non-discrete archimedean topology — solidity is fundamentally non-archimedean);
  • Complex Banach spaces (also archimedean).

The key theorem (Clausen–Scholze): $\mathrm{Solid}(\mathrm{Ab}) \subset \mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$ is a closed monoidal abelian subcategory. It is closed under all small limits and colimits computed in $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Ab})$, under extensions in short exact sequences, and carries a "solid tensor product" $\otimes^{\blacksquare}$ that derives correctly. $\mathrm{Solid} \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Cond}$ is a reflective subcategory: solidification ${}^{\blacksquare}$ is left adjoint to the inclusion.

Operationally this means: the derived category $D(\mathrm{Solid}(\mathrm{Ab}))$ is the right home for completed/profinite topological algebra. Tensor products of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ with itself give $\mathbb{Z}_p$ (idempotence of solid completion), $\mathrm{RHom}_{\mathrm{Solid}}(\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Z}_p)$ recovers $\mathbb{Z}_p$, and the Mayer–Vietoris / Čech sequences for solid sheaves on a $p$-adic ring all behave as a working algebraist would expect — and as classical topological-Ab homological algebra absolutely did not.

4. Liquid $\mathbb{R}$-vector spaces

Solid theory is built around non-archimedean completion — Mittag-Leffler limits of finite-rank pieces. It is precisely the wrong setting for $\mathbb{R}$-vector spaces, which are the home of classical functional analysis. The category of condensed real vector spaces $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Vect}_{\mathbb{R}})$ is abelian (good), but does not have a satisfactory tensor product (bad — for analogous reasons that solid was needed: archimedean subtleties).

The fix: liquid structures, parameterised by a real number $0 < p \le 1$. A condensed real vector space $V$ is $p$-liquid if it admits a measure-like structure: a continuous bilinear pairing

$$\mu\colon \mathcal{M}_p(V) \;\to\; V$$

where $\mathcal M_p(V)$ is the space of $p$-summable measures with values in $V$, satisfying axioms generalising "you can integrate a $p$-summable measure against a continuous function". When $p < 1$ the condition genuinely strengthens what $V$ is, and one obtains:

  • $\mathrm{Liquid}_p(\mathbb{R})$ is a full abelian subcategory of $\mathrm{Cond}(\mathrm{Vect}_{\mathbb{R}})$.
  • It is closed under (small) limits and colimits, kernels, cokernels, and extensions.
  • It carries a liquid tensor product $\otimes^{\liq}$ (depending on $p$) that is symmetric monoidal and exact (so derives correctly to a derived tensor on $D(\mathrm{Liquid}_p(\mathbb{R}))$).

The classical Banach tensor product $\widehat{\otimes}_\pi$ — the projective tensor product of Grothendieck — fails to be exact: there exist short exact sequences of Banach spaces $0 \to V' \to V \to V'' \to 0$ for which $0 \to V' \widehat\otimes_\pi W \to V \widehat\otimes_\pi W \to V'' \widehat\otimes_\pi W \to 0$ is not exact on the left. This pathology forces functional analysts to work with bornological / quasi-abelian replacements rather than honest derived categories. The liquid tensor product $\otimes^{\liq}$ fixes this — for Banach spaces in the appropriate range of $p$, the liquid tensor matches the projective tensor up to a controlled error and is exact.

Classical objects sit inside $\mathrm{Liquid}_p(\mathbb{R})$ as full subcategories:

  • Banach spaces are $p$-liquid for every $0 < p \le 1$;
  • Fréchet spaces (countable inverse limits of Banach) are $p$-liquid;
  • Bornological vector spaces with reasonable boundedness structure;
  • $L^p$ spaces ($1 \le p < \infty$, as Banach spaces) embed into $\mathrm{Liquid}_{p'}(\mathbb{R})$ for every $0 < p' \le 1$;
  • $C(X, \mathbb{R})$ for $X$ compact, locally convex topological vector spaces in general — the entire functional-analysis cast.

Why parameterise by $p$? The property strengthens as $p$ approaches $1$: $1$-liquid is the weakest condition (everything reasonable is $1$-liquid but the tensor product breaks at the boundary), and small $p$ is the most permissive end of the range (more spaces qualify, the tensor structure is well-behaved). The sweet spot for the theory is some $p_0 < 1$; for the headline theorem in §5, both the input and output sit in this $p$-liquid range, with a strict inequality $p' < p$ supplying the room to manoeuvre the proof requires.

5. The Liquid Tensor Experiment

The homological cornerstone of the liquid theory is a concrete vanishing statement. Fix $0 < p' < p \le 1$, let $V$ be a $p$-liquid real vector space, and let $M_{p'}(S)$ denote the space of $p'$-summable measures on a profinite set $S$ with values in $\mathbb{R}$ (an explicit "completed measure" object). The theorem (Clausen–Scholze):

$$\mathrm{Ext}^i_{\mathrm{Cond}(\mathbb{R})}\bigl(M_{p'}(S),\, V\bigr) \;=\; 0 \qquad \text{for all } i \ge 1.$$

Equivalently $M_{p'}(S)$ is "compact-projective" relative to $V$ — every short exact sequence ending in a $p$-liquid space splits when probed against $M_{p'}$. This is the technical engine that powers exactness of the liquid tensor product and the existence of well-behaved derived functors on $\mathrm{Liquid}(\mathbb{R})$. Without it the liquid theory collapses.

The proof is a tour de force of $50$+ pages, with intricate inequalities about $p'$-summable norms and a delicate induction whose base case relies on a real-analytic estimate Scholze called "the most fragile thing I have ever proved". In December 2020 Scholze posted a challenge on Kevin Buzzard's Xena blog: formally verify this theorem in Lean. None of his collaborators believed it was right; all of them lacked confidence in their own line-by-line check.

A community led by Johan Commelin (Utrecht/Mainz) took the bait. Using Lean's mathlib library and a careful translation of Scholze's manuscript, the team — Adam Topaz, Riccardo Brasca, Patrick Massot, Damiano Testa, Filippo Nuccio, Kevin Buzzard, Bhavik Mehta, and many others — formalised the proof step by step over 18 months. The main theorem (the inductive heart of the result) was discharged in May 2022; the full statement, including the technically painful base case, was complete by July 2022; further generalisations and tightenings landed through 2023.

The cultural significance went well beyond the theorem itself. This was the first time a major living mathematician used Lean to gain confidence in a theorem they personally doubted. Formal verification graduated from "proves things you already know" to "proves things you don't know yet", crossing a working-mathematician acceptability threshold that the field had been near for years but had never quite reached. The infrastructure built — Lean tactics for limits of profinite spaces, a $p$-summable-measure formalisation, a working derived-category framework — became foundational to subsequent mathlib efforts.

6. Applications: analytic geometry, $p$-adic Hodge theory, analytic stacks

Condensed mathematics is not an end in itself but a foundation for several adjacent enterprises that previously suffered the same "topological abelian groups don't form an abelian category" pathology. Three programmes have been explicitly rebuilt on condensed/solid/liquid foundations:

Analytic geometry over $\mathbb{Q}_p$

Classical $p$-adic analytic geometry — rigid-analytic spaces (Tate), formal schemes (Raynaud), adic spaces (Huber), Berkovich spaces — each compromise differently between "preserve $p$-adic topology" and "have a tractable category of sheaves". Solid abelian groups give the first uniform answer: an analytic ring over $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is, in the Clausen–Scholze rebuild, a condensed ring $R$ together with a class of "discrete" $R$-modules (a full subcategory of $\mathrm{Cond}(R\text{-mod})$ closed under suitable operations). The classical rigid/adic/Berkovich examples all assemble into analytic rings; the resulting derived categories of completed coherent sheaves admit a six-functor formalism with proper-base-change, projection formula, Verdier duality — all the machinery $p$-adic Hodge theory needs but classical rigid-analytic frameworks struggled to provide.

Prismatic cohomology and $p$-adic Hodge theory

Bhatt–Morrow–Scholze's prismatic cohomology $\mathrm{R}\Gamma_{\mathbb{\Delta}}(X/\mathbb{Z}_p)$ unifies étale, de Rham, and crystalline $p$-adic cohomology theories into one sheaf-theoretic object. Its natural derived-category home is solid abelian groups: prismatic cohomology lands in $D(\mathrm{Solid}(\mathbb{Z}_p))$, and the integral comparison theorems (étale-vs-prismatic, crystalline-vs-prismatic, de Rham-vs-prismatic) all become statements about quasi-isomorphisms in this fixed solid derived category, removing case-by-case "now we choose a topology / completion" passes.

Analytic stacks

The newest output: Clausen and Scholze's program on analytic stacks, building on solid theory the same way Deligne–Mumford stacks build on schemes. An analytic stack is, roughly, a stack on the category of analytic rings with an étale-style topology — it includes rigid-analytic spaces, adic spaces, formal stacks, and entirely new objects unifying all three. Lectures by Scholze at the IHÉS in 2023–2024 sketched the framework; Clausen's 2024 series at Bonn carried the technical core. Open frontier: integrating this with the Fargues–Scholze geometric Langlands programme, and connecting to Lurie's analytic $\infty$-topoi.

Connections

Condensed mathematics is the algebraic foundation Clausen and Scholze proposed to give topology, $p$-adic analysis, and functional analysis a single derived-category home. Open frontiers: the analytic-stacks programme is still under construction (Bonn lectures 2024 onward); the Fargues–Scholze geometric Langlands relies on condensed methods that the broader community is still digesting; integrating liquid theory with motivic cohomology for $\mathbb{R}$-coefficients is largely open. The Liquid Tensor Experiment closed one specific question while opening a much larger one — how much of working mathematics could profitably be ported to the condensed framework, and how much will be?

  • Grothendieck topologies & sites — sheaves on $(C, J)$ — condensed sets are sheaves on the pro-étale site of a point; the entire framework is a sheaf-theoretic enlargement of $\mathbf{Top}$.
  • Derived categories — $D(\mathcal A)$ — solid and liquid theories are designed so that $D(\mathrm{Solid})$ and $D(\mathrm{Liquid})$ are well-behaved derived categories, replacing the failed $D(\mathrm{TopAb})$.
  • Banach and Hilbert spaces — Banach spaces sit inside $\mathrm{Liquid}_p(\mathbb{R})$ as a full subcategory; the liquid tensor product replaces the projective tensor where exactness is needed.
  • Comparison theorems: étale vs. singular, de Rham, crystalline — prismatic cohomology lives naturally in $D(\mathrm{Solid}(\mathbb{Z}_p))$ and the integral comparison isomorphisms are statements there.
  • Models of HoTT — the Liquid Tensor Experiment was formalised in Lean's mathlib; formal verification's cultural shift toward "verifying things mathematicians don't yet trust" arguably dates from this project.
