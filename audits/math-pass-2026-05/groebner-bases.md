- **Cancellation property** (line 741): leading terms cancel by construction — correct.
- **Buchberger's criterion** (lines 743–746): $G$ is GB iff every $\overline{S(g_i,g_j)}^G = 0$ — standard.
- **Worked S-polynomial examples** (widget lines 829–836):

## Issues found

### Major: §8 implicitization widget claims a non-Gröbner basis is a Gröbner basis (lines 1281–1285)

The widget says:

> Step 2 — Gröbner basis under lex with t > x > y > z:
>   reduce y - t² mod (x - t):  …  ≡ y - x² (sub t=x)
>   reduce z - t³ mod (x - t):  …  ≡ z - x³
>   ⇒ G = { t - x,  y - x²,  z - x³ }

**$\{t - x, y - x^2, z - x^3\}$ is NOT a Gröbner basis** of $J = \langle x-t, y-t^2, z-t^3\rangle$ under lex with $t > x > y > z$.

Verified by computing $S(t-x, y-x^2)$. With LT$(t-x) = t$ and LT$(y-x^2) = -x^2$, $\gamma = tx^2$:
$$S = x^2(t-x) - (-t)(y-x^2) = tx^2 - x^3 + ty - tx^2 = ty - x^3.$$
Reducing $ty - x^3$ by $G$: subtract $y(t-x) = ty - xy$ (kills $ty$), giving $xy - x^3$. Then subtract $1\cdot(z-x^3) = z - x^3$ (kills $-x^3$), giving $xy - z$. None of LT$\{t, -x^2, -x^3\}$ divides $xy$ or $-z$, so the remainder is $xy - z \neq 0$. Buchberger's criterion fails for the pair $(g_1, g_2)$.

Continuing Buchberger one would adjoin at least $\{xy - z, xz - y^2, y^3 - z^2, \dots\}$ to reach an actual reduced lex GB. The standard reduced lex GB of the twisted-cubic ideal under $x > y > z$ alone (after $t$-elimination) is $\{x^2 - y, xy - z, xz - y^2, y^3 - z^2\}$ or similar — strictly larger than $\{y - x^2, z - x^3\}$.

**However**, the widget's conclusion that **the implicit ideal of the twisted cubic is $\langle y - x^2, z - x^3\rangle$ is correct** — these two polynomials *do* generate the ideal as an ideal (the quotient $k[x,y,z]/\langle y-x^2, z-x^3\rangle \cong k[x]$ is an integral domain isomorphic to the parametrization's image). So the final answer is right; the route to it (calling $\{t-x, y-x^2, z-x^3\}$ "the Gröbner basis") misuses the Elimination Theorem.

**Fix:** either (a) compute the actual reduced lex GB and present it (involves several more polynomials), or (b) drop the words "Gröbner basis" and just say "after eliminating $t$, the implicit ideal is generated by $\{y - x^2, z - x^3\}$" — substantively true and avoids the misstatement.

### Minor: Mayr–Meyer attribution (line 891)

"Mayr and Meyer constructed ideals … whose Gröbner bases contain elements of degree doubly exponential in $n$." Mayr–Meyer 1982 actually proved EXPSPACE-completeness of the **ideal-membership problem**; the doubly-exponential **upper bound** on Gröbner-basis element degree is from Dube 1990. The doubly-exponential **lower bound** for GB degrees does follow from Mayr–Meyer-type ideals, so the spirit of the attribution is right, but a working mathematician would split the citation. Not a math error per se — a literature-attribution sharpness issue.

### Minor: §6 widget reduction trace omits an interreduction detail (line 1097)

In Basis A reduction, after step 1 leaves $\{y^2-1, x-y\}$, step 2 says "interreduce: each $g$ mod the others". The trace says:

> y²-1 mod {x-y}: no monomial of y²-1 has an x-factor → unchanged

This is the correct conclusion but the **reason** is not quite right: the criterion is whether any monomial of $g$ is divisible by LT($g'$). LT$(x-y) = x$. So we need to check whether any monomial of $y^2 - 1$ (i.e. $y^2$ or $1$) is divisible by $x$. Neither is, so $y^2-1$ is unchanged. The phrase "has an x-factor" gets the right answer but is slightly imprecise (e.g. a monomial like $x$ has an "$x$-factor" but is not divisible by $x^2$). For a divisor with LT exactly $x$, "has an $x$-factor" coincides with "divisible by LT", so no actual error — just unsharp wording. **Skip-worthy** by the audit's threshold.

### Minor: §6 reduction widget — candidate redundancy in starting basis B (line 1083)

Basis B is given as $\{xy-1, y^2-1, x-y+y^2-1, xy+y^2-y-1\}$ and described as a "non-reduced Gröbner basis" of the same ideal. Let me verify it is actually a GB. We need every S-pair to reduce to 0 mod the basis. With four generators and LTs $\{xy, y^2, x, xy\}$, $\langle\mathrm{LT}(B)\rangle = \langle x, y^2\rangle$ (since $x \mid xy$ trivially). So $\langle\mathrm{LT}(B)\rangle = \langle\mathrm{LT}(I)\rangle$ — yes, B is a Gröbner basis. ✓
