Fixed-point theorems

Four classical existence results — Banach, Brouwer, Schauder, Lefschetz — plus Kakutani's set-valued upgrade, organized by the structure they exploit: completeness, compactness, convexity, and homology.

1. Banach contraction principle

If $(X,d)$ is a complete metric space and $f\colon X\to X$ satisfies a uniform Lipschitz bound $d(f(x),f(y))\le L\,d(x,y)$ with $L<1$, then $f$ has exactly one fixed point. The proof is constructive: pick any $x_0$, iterate $x_{n+1}=f(x_n)$, and the sequence is Cauchy with geometric decay $d(x_n,x_*) \le \tfrac{L^n}{1-L}d(x_0,x_1)$.

The two hypotheses are tight. Drop completeness and the iteration may flee the space; relax $L<1$ to $L\le 1$ (or even $|f'(x)|<1$ pointwise) and a fixed point can fail to exist — try $f(x)=x+e^{-x}$ on $\mathbb{R}$.

2. Brouwer fixed-point theorem

Every continuous map $f\colon D^n\to D^n$ from the closed $n$-ball to itself has a fixed point. Equivalently, every continuous self-map of a non-empty compact convex subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ has a fixed point. The theorem fails on the circle $S^1$ (antipodal map) and on the open ball (translation toward the boundary), so both compactness and contractibility matter.

The standard proof is a contradiction: if $f$ had no fixed point, the ray from $f(x)$ through $x$ would meet the boundary at a point $r(x)\in S^{n-1}$, defining a continuous retraction $r\colon D^n\to S^{n-1}$. But $H_{n-1}(D^n)=0$ while $H_{n-1}(S^{n-1})=\mathbb{Z}$, so the composition $S^{n-1}\hookrightarrow D^n\xrightarrow{r}S^{n-1}$ cannot be the identity — contradiction.

3. Schauder fixed-point theorem

The infinite-dimensional generalisation: if $C$ is a non-empty closed convex subset of a Banach space $X$ and $T\colon C\to C$ is continuous with $T(C)$ contained in a compact set, then $T$ has a fixed point. The variant most used in PDE: a compact operator $T$ mapping a closed bounded convex set into itself has a fixed point.

Why compactness can't be dropped: in any infinite-dimensional Banach space the closed unit ball is non-compact (Riesz's lemma), and one can construct fixed-point-free continuous self-maps of it — Kakutani's example $T(x_1,x_2,\ldots) = (\sqrt{1-\|x\|^2},x_1,x_2,\ldots)$ on the closed unit ball of $\ell^2$ is the textbook witness.

4. Lefschetz fixed-point theorem

Let $X$ be a compact triangulable space and $f\colon X\to X$ continuous. The Lefschetz number is $$\Lambda(f) = \sum_{k\ge 0} (-1)^k\,\tr\bigl(f_*\colon H_k(X;\mathbb{Q})\to H_k(X;\mathbb{Q})\bigr).$$ If $\Lambda(f)\ne 0$, then $f$ has a fixed point. The implication is one-way: $\Lambda(f)=0$ is silent. For $f=\mathrm{id}$ the trace on $H_k$ is $b_k$, so $\Lambda(\mathrm{id})=\chi(X)$, the Euler characteristic — Lefschetz recovers the fact that a non-zero Euler characteristic forbids a fixed-point-free self-homeomorphism homotopic to the identity.

Brouwer is the contractible special case: on $D^n$ all higher homology vanishes, so $\Lambda(f)=\tr(f_*\colon H_0\to H_0)=1\ne 0$.

5. Kakutani fixed-point theorem

Replace single-valued continuous $f$ with a set-valued map $\varphi\colon C\rightrightarrows C$ assigning to each $x\in C$ a non-empty closed convex subset $\varphi(x)\subseteq C$. If $C$ is non-empty compact convex (in $\mathbb{R}^n$) and $\varphi$ has closed graph, then there exists $x^*\in C$ with $x^*\in\varphi(x^*)$.

The motivating example is Nash's existence proof: in a finite game each player's best-response set $B_i(\sigma_{-i})$ is non-empty compact convex but typically not a singleton when ties occur. The product correspondence $\sigma\mapsto\prod_i B_i(\sigma_{-i})$ on the product of mixed-strategy simplices satisfies Kakutani's hypotheses, so a fixed point — a Nash equilibrium — exists.

6. Caristi's fixed-point theorem

Banach's contraction is a metric inequality between $x$ and $Tx$; Caristi's theorem replaces it with a Lyapunov-style budget. There is no Lipschitz constant and the map need not be continuous — only the existence of a non-negative weight that the orbit consumes.

Theorem (Caristi, 1976). Let $(X,d)$ be a complete metric space and $T\colon X\to X$ any map (continuity not assumed). Suppose there is a lower-semicontinuous function $\varphi\colon X\to[0,\infty)$ such that $$d\bigl(x,T(x)\bigr)\;\le\;\varphi(x)-\varphi\bigl(T(x)\bigr)\qquad\text{for every }x\in X.$$ Then $T$ has a fixed point.

Read the inequality as an accounting identity: each step of the orbit pays at least $d(x,Tx)$ out of the budget $\varphi(x)$, and $\varphi\ge 0$ caps the total expenditure. Iterating gives $\sum_{k\ge 0} d(x_k,x_{k+1})\le\varphi(x_0)<\infty$, so $\{x_k\}$ is Cauchy; completeness produces a limit $x_*$, and the lower-semicontinuity of $\varphi$ at $x_*$ forces $d(x_*,Tx_*)=0$.

Banach as a special case. If $T$ is a Lipschitz contraction with constant $L<1$, set $$\varphi(x)=\frac{1}{1-L}\,d\bigl(x,T(x)\bigr).$$ A single application of the contraction inequality gives $d(Tx,T^2x)\le L\,d(x,Tx)$, hence $$\varphi(x)-\varphi(Tx)=\tfrac{1}{1-L}\bigl[d(x,Tx)-d(Tx,T^2x)\bigr]\ge\tfrac{1-L}{1-L}\,d(x,Tx)=d(x,Tx).$$ Caristi's hypothesis holds and recovers Banach's fixed point. The leverage in Caristi is that $\varphi$ need not be defined as a function of $T$ — any independent Lyapunov-style weight will do.

A concrete non-contraction. Let $X=[0,1]$ with the usual metric, $T(x)=x-x^2/2$ on $(0,1]$ and $T(0)=0$. Then $T'(1)=0$ but $T'(0)=1$, so no global Lipschitz constant $L<1$ exists — Banach does not apply. Take $\varphi(x)=2x$: a quick computation yields $$\varphi(x)-\varphi(Tx)=2x-2(x-x^2/2)=x^2\ge x^2/2=d(x,Tx),$$ so Caristi forces a fixed point. (Indeed $0$ is fixed; the orbit from any $x_0\in(0,1]$ decreases monotonically to $0$.)

Equivalence with Ekeland. Caristi's theorem is equivalent (logically and proof-wise) to Ekeland's variational principle: on a complete metric space, every lower-semicontinuous bounded-below $\varphi$ has, near any near-minimizer, a strict minimizer of a small perturbation $\varphi+\varepsilon\,d(\cdot,x_0)$. The fixed-point form follows by selecting $T(x)$ to be any point that fails Ekeland's $\varepsilon$-strict-minimum condition; the variational form follows by feeding Caristi the multivalued map $T(x)=\{y:\varphi(y)+d(x,y)\le\varphi(x)\}$. This duality makes Caristi the workhorse behind existence proofs in non-smooth optimization, viscosity solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi equations, and metric-fixed-point theory on incomplete spaces.

Why no continuity. Caristi's proof never evaluates $T$ at the limit $x_*$. It uses the inequality only to construct a Cauchy orbit and then shows $\varphi$-equality forces the displacement to vanish. The price of dropping continuity of $T$ is that the fixed point need not be unique — there can be a whole set on which $\varphi$ is locally constant.

7. Knaster–Kuratowski–Mazurkiewicz lemma

Brouwer is topological — it speaks of continuous self-maps of a ball. The KKM lemma is its covering form: the same content stated as an intersection theorem for closed sets on a simplex. Out of KKM falls Brouwer, Sperner's lemma, the von Neumann minimax, and an entire fixed-point theory for set-valued maps.

KKM lemma (Knaster–Kuratowski–Mazurkiewicz, 1929). Let $\Delta_n=\mathrm{conv}\{v_0,\ldots,v_n\}$ be the standard $n$-simplex and let $C_0,\ldots,C_n\subseteq\Delta_n$ be closed sets satisfying the KKM covering condition: $$\mathrm{conv}\{v_i:i\in S\}\;\subseteq\;\bigcup_{i\in S}C_i\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq\{0,\ldots,n\}.$$ Then $\bigcap_{i=0}^n C_i\ne\emptyset$.

The covering condition says every face $\Delta_S$ is covered by the $C_i$ whose vertices it contains. Specialising to $S=\{i\}$ forces $v_i\in C_i$. Specialising to the full set $S=\{0,\ldots,n\}$ forces $\Delta_n\subseteq\bigcup C_i$, but the conclusion is far stronger than this single covering — it says all $n+1$ pieces share a common point, not merely that they tile the simplex.

Sperner's lemma — the discrete sibling. Triangulate $\Delta_n$ and label every vertex of the triangulation with an integer in $\{0,\ldots,n\}$, subject to the rule that a vertex on a face $\Delta_S$ receives only labels from $S$. A small simplex of the triangulation is fully labelled if its vertices realise all $n+1$ labels. Sperner asserts: the number of fully labelled small simplices is odd, in particular non-zero. Letting the mesh shrink and applying compactness recovers KKM (each $C_i$ is the closure of vertices labelled $i$, the fully labelled cells condense to a common limit point), and conversely KKM implies Sperner via a generic-position argument. The combinatorial proof of Sperner is a one-page induction on dimension — making the entire Brouwer–KKM circle elementary.

KKM proves Brouwer. Given continuous $f\colon\Delta_n\to\Delta_n$, write $x=\sum_i x_i v_i$ in barycentric coordinates and set $$C_i\;=\;\{x\in\Delta_n:f(x)_i\le x_i\}.$$ Each $C_i$ is closed by continuity. To check the KKM covering condition, pick any face $\Delta_S$ and any $x\in\Delta_S$ (so $x_j=0$ for $j\notin S$, and $\sum_{i\in S}x_i=1$). Since $f(x)\in\Delta_n$ also has barycentric coordinates summing to $1$, at least one index $i\in S$ must satisfy $f(x)_i\le x_i$ — otherwise every $i\in S$ would have $f(x)_i>x_i$, summing to $>1$, contradiction. So $x\in C_i$ for some $i\in S$. KKM gives a common point $x_*\in\bigcap C_i$, where $f(x_*)_i\le x_*\,_i$ for every $i$; equality of sums forces $f(x_*)=x_*$.

Fan-KKM and minimax. Ky Fan upgraded KKM to non-compact convex sets in topological vector spaces and dropped the closedness in favor of a closed-graph condition on a set-valued map $G\colon\Delta_n\rightrightarrows\Delta_n$ with $\mathrm{conv}\{v_i:i\in S\}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in S}G(v_i)$. The conclusion is the same — non-empty intersection — and applied to the map $G(y)=\{x:f(x,y)\le f(y,y)\}$ for a continuous saddle function $f$, Fan-KKM yields the von Neumann minimax theorem. From there it is a short hop to Nash equilibrium (recovering Kakutani as a corollary) and to set-valued fixed-point theorems for upper-hemicontinuous correspondences with non-convex values via Browder's selection theorem.

Why KKM is the cleanest gateway. Brouwer needs degree theory or singular homology; KKM needs only Sperner, which is a finite combinatorial argument. Once KKM is in hand, Brouwer, Schauder (via finite-dimensional approximation), Kakutani (via the Browder selection), and the von Neumann/Nash existence proofs all fall out by short reductions. Functional analysts often cite KKM as the right starting axiom for fixed-point theory.

Closed-star cover

The first widget realises the closed-cover form. Three closed stars $A_i = \{x\in\Delta : x_i\ge t_i\}$ around each vertex. The KKM covering condition (every face $\Delta_S$ covered by indexed $A_i$ for $i \in S$) reduces, in this closed-star formulation, to the single inequality $\sum_i t_i \le 1$ — and KKM's conclusion is then exactly $\bigcap_i A_i \ne \emptyset$, the green sub-triangle. The intersection contracts to a point as $\sum t_i \to 1$, and goes empty the moment the cover hypothesis fails: in this formulation cover and intersection are governed by the same condition, gating one another via KKM.

Sperner — discrete sibling

The combinatorial mirror image: triangulate $\Delta$ at depth $n$, apply a Sperner labelling (vertex on face $\Delta_S$ uses only labels from $S$), and count the rainbow sub-triangles. Sperner asserts the count is always odd — in particular non-zero — by a one-page parity argument on edge counts. As $n\to\infty$, by compactness the rainbow cells condense to a Brouwer fixed point of any continuous map represented by the labelling: this is precisely how §2 Brouwer falls out of the discrete combinatorics, with no degree theory or homology.

8. Applications across mathematics

Every theorem above shows up as the existence engine in a downstream result:

  • Picard–Lindelöf for ODEs — Banach contraction. The Picard operator $T(\varphi)(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^t f(s,\varphi(s))\,ds$ is a contraction on $C([t_0-h,t_0+h])$ when $f$ is Lipschitz in its second argument and $h$ is small.
  • Implicit function theorem — Banach contraction. A Newton-style operator $y\mapsto y-D_yF(x_0,y_0)^{-1}F(x,y)$ is a contraction on a small ball, and its fixed point is the implicit solution $y=g(x)$.
  • Markov-chain stationary distribution — Brouwer. The simplex $\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ is non-empty compact convex; $\pi\mapsto\pi P$ is continuous; a stationary $\pi^*$ exists for any stochastic $P$.
  • Existence of solutions to nonlinear elliptic PDEs — Schauder. A compact solution operator on a closed bounded set in a function space provides existence without uniqueness.
  • Nash equilibrium — Kakutani. The best-response correspondence on the product simplex satisfies the hypotheses; its fixed point is the equilibrium.

Connections

Fixed-point machinery is the existence proof of last resort across analysis, geometry, and economics. The frontier today: variational and topological methods (Conley index, mountain-pass) for fixed points of non-compact maps on infinite-dimensional manifolds; computational degree theory; and equilibrium existence for general-equilibrium economies where Kakutani is replaced by Eilenberg–Montgomery or Browder.

  • Functional analysis — compact operators — the natural input class for Schauder.
  • Algebraic topology — singular homology — supplies the trace whose alternating sum is the Lefschetz number.
  • Point-set topology — compactness — the abstract hypothesis common to Brouwer, Schauder, Kakutani, and Lefschetz.
  • Partial differential equations — Schauder gives existence for nonlinear elliptic problems via a compact solution operator.
  • Dynamical systems — periodic orbits are fixed points of iterated maps, opening the door to Lefschetz–Nielsen counting.
