Mathematical biology

Genetic variation is a stochastic process: the allele frequencies in a finite population drift, mutate, and are filtered by selection. Wright–Fisher and Moran put a Markov chain on the gene pool; Kimura's diffusion takes the continuum limit; Kingman's coalescent traces ancestry backward. The deterministic side — Lotka–Volterra predator–prey, replicator dynamics on the simplex, mutation–selection balance — sits inside the same toolkit. This page is the population-genetics half of the section; ecology and evolutionary game theory are on the back half.

1. The Wright–Fisher model

Fix a haploid population of $N$ individuals carrying $2N$ alleles at a single locus (the diploid convention). The Wright–Fisher model evolves the population in discrete, non-overlapping generations: each of the $2N$ offspring in generation $n+1$ independently picks a parent uniformly at random from the $2N$ alleles in generation $n$. If $X_n$ is the count of the A-allele at time $n$, then conditionally on $X_n=k$,

$X_{n+1}\mid X_n=k \;\sim\; \mathrm{Binomial}\!\left(2N,\;\tfrac{k}{2N}\right).$

Two features make this the founding object of mathematical population genetics:

  • Markov chain. The next generation depends only on the current allele count — no memory of how the population got here.
  • Absorbing endpoints. $0$ and $2N$ are absorbing states; from any interior state both are reachable in finite expected time. Every neutral allele eventually fixes (reaches $2N$) or is lost (reaches $0$).

The fixation probability from initial count $k$ is, by the martingale property $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid X_n]=X_n$ plus optional stopping,

$\mathbb{P}_k[\text{fix}]\;=\;\frac{k}{2N}.$

That is: with no selection, every founder allele's fate is decided by genetic drift alone, and the fairness of the lottery is exactly its initial share. Heterozygosity $H_n = 2 p_n(1-p_n)$ decays in expectation by a factor $1-\tfrac{1}{2N}$ per generation — variation bleeds out at rate $\sim 1/(2N)$.

Why $2N$ and not $N$. The doubling counts alleles, not individuals. For a diploid species each individual carries two copies, so the effective gene pool is $2N$. Plant and animal populations swap between diploid and haploid conventions; bacterial models drop the factor entirely. The takeaway: effective population size $N_e$ replaces literal census size $N$, and demographic effects (bottlenecks, sex bias) shrink $N_e$ below $N$.

2. The Moran process

Wright–Fisher is discrete-generation: every individual dies and is replaced every step. Moran's 1958 alternative is continuous-time and overlapping: at each "event" (Poisson clock rate $N$), one individual is picked uniformly to die and one is picked uniformly to reproduce (independently — possibly the same one). The chain $X_n\in\{0,1,\dots,2N\}$ now jumps by $\pm 1$ or stays put.

$\mathbb{P}[X_{n+1}=k+1\mid X_n=k]\;=\;\mathbb{P}[X_{n+1}=k-1\mid X_n=k]\;=\;\frac{k(2N-k)}{(2N)^2}.$

The crucial feature: the chain is a simple symmetric random walk on $\{0,1,\dots,2N\}$ — biased only by the probability of doing nothing. Both absorbing endpoints are still $0$ and $2N$. The fixation probability from $k$ is again $k/(2N)$ (gambler's ruin on the integers), so Wright–Fisher and Moran agree on this fundamental quantity even though the timescales differ by a factor of $\sim N/2$.

What Moran buys you

Selection enters by giving the focal allele relative fitness $r=1+s$: when an individual is chosen to reproduce, the type-A individuals are sampled with weight proportional to $r$. Standard manipulation gives the explicit fixation probability with selection,

$\rho_k \;=\; \frac{1 - r^{-k}}{1 - r^{-2N}},$

which is a clean closed form Wright–Fisher cannot match. For $r=1$ (neutral) one recovers $\rho_k = k/(2N)$ by L'Hôpital; for a single advantageous mutant ($k=1$, $r$ slightly above $1$), $\rho_1 \approx 1 - r^{-1} = s/(1+s) \approx s$ — Haldane's classic " probability of fixation of a new beneficial mutation is approximately its selection coefficient."

The crossover happens at $2Ns\sim 1$: this is exactly Ohta's nearly neutral regime, and the reason small populations are dominated by drift while large ones obey deterministic selection.

3. Genetic drift and the Kimura diffusion

The CLT story for allele frequencies: rescale time by $2N$ generations and let $N\to\infty$. With selection scaled as $s = \sigma/(2N)$ (so $2Ns=\sigma$ remains $O(1)$), the discrete Wright–Fisher chain $p_n = X_n/(2N)$ converges in distribution to the Kimura diffusion on $[0,1]$:

$dp_t \;=\; \sigma\, p(1-p)\,dt \;+\; \sqrt{p(1-p)}\,dW_t.$

This is an Itô SDE on the unit interval: the drift $\sigma p(1-p)$ is the selection pressure, the diffusion coefficient $p(1-p)$ degenerates at the absorbing boundaries $\{0,1\}$. The infinitesimal generator is

$\mathcal{L} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,p(1-p)\,\partial_p^2 \;+\; \sigma\, p(1-p)\,\partial_p,$

and the backward Kolmogorov equation $\partial_t u = \mathcal{L} u$ gives the fixation probability $u(p)=\mathbb{P}_p[\text{fix at }1]$ as the solution of $\mathcal{L} u = 0$ with $u(0)=0$, $u(1)=1$:

$u(p) \;=\; \frac{1 - e^{-2\sigma p}}{1 - e^{-2\sigma}}.$

This is the continuous-time Moran formula with $r\to e^{s}$ and $k/(2N)\to p$. It is the exact answer to the question "what is the fixation probability of a mutant present at frequency $p$ with selection coefficient $s$?", and is the reference point for every population-genetics calculation that follows.

Kimura's fixation curve
drag $\sigma = 2Ns$ from drift-dominated to selection-dominated

The dimensionless parameter $\sigma = 2Ns$ is the only thing that matters — not $N$ and $s$ separately. When $|\sigma|\le 1$ the curve is approximately the neutral diagonal $u(p)=p$; once $|\sigma|\gtrsim 4$, a single rare mutant ($p\approx 0$) already has fixation probability near $\sigma/(1-e^{-2\sigma})$. The shape is the population-genetics analogue of an Arrhenius factor.

Why $2Ns$ and not $s$. The diffusion limit only sees one parameter, the product $\sigma = 2Ns$. This is one of the cleanest examples of a "scaling regime" in stochastic analysis: the discrete chain has two free parameters ($N$ and $s$); after CLT-style rescaling they collapse into one. Numerically: a $1\%$ selection coefficient ($s=0.01$) is invisible in a population of $50$ ($\sigma=1$, drift-dominated) and a juggernaut in a population of $10^6$ ($\sigma=2\times 10^4$, deterministic).

4. Kingman's coalescent

Wright–Fisher and Moran run time forward: pick parents, watch frequencies drift. The clever inversion (Kingman, 1982) runs time backward: given a present-day sample of $n$ individuals, ask what their genealogy looks like.

Take a single generation under Wright–Fisher: two distinct sampled lineages share a parent (i.e. coalesce) with probability $1/(2N)$. With $k$ lineages drawn from the sample, the probability that any pair coalesces in a given generation is $\binom{k}{2}/(2N) + O(1/N^2)$. Rescaling time by $2N$ generations and letting $N\to\infty$ converts this geometric waiting time into an exponential:

$T_k \;\sim\; \mathrm{Exp}\!\left(\binom{k}{2}\right),\qquad \mathbb{E}[T_k] \;=\; \frac{1}{\binom{k}{2}}\;=\;\frac{2}{k(k-1)}.$

The $n$-coalescent is the random binary tree obtained by: start with $n$ leaves at the present, wait $T_n\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\binom{n}{2})$, merge a uniformly random pair, repeat with $n-1$ lineages, $\ldots$, until one lineage remains (the most recent common ancestor, MRCA). The total height is

$\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathrm{MRCA}}]\;=\;\sum_{k=2}^{n}\frac{2}{k(k-1)}\;=\;2\!\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{n}\right) \;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\; 2,$

in units of $2N$ generations. The MRCA depth is bounded by $2$ regardless of sample size — most of the genealogy lives near the leaves, and adding more samples gives diminishing depth returns.

What the coalescent is actually good for. It is the engine of modern population genetics: every neutral genetic-variation statistic on a sample is a functional of its underlying tree, and the coalescent is the prior over trees implied by Wright–Fisher. Tajima's $D$, the site-frequency spectrum, $F_{ST}$ between populations, the rejection sampler at the heart of ABC — all of them are coalescent computations. Hudson's ms simulator and its successors (msprime) sample coalescent trees in seconds for $n\sim 10^5$.

5. Lotka–Volterra predator–prey

Switch from stochastic alleles to deterministic populations. Lotka (1925) and Volterra (1926) — independently, motivated by the Adriatic fishery records — wrote down the simplest coupled ODE for two species in a predator–prey relationship:

$\dot x \;=\; a x - b x y,\qquad \dot y \;=\; -c y + d x y,\qquad a,b,c,d>0.$

Here $x(t)$ is the prey biomass, $y(t)$ the predator biomass. Without predators ($y=0$) the prey grow exponentially at rate $a$; without prey ($x=0$) the predators decay at rate $c$. The interaction terms $b x y$ and $d x y$ encode mass-action kinetics — predation rate is proportional to encounter probability.

Fixed points and the conserved quantity

Setting $\dot x = \dot y = 0$ gives two fixed points: the trivial $(0,0)$ (saddle: unstable along $x$, stable along $y$) and the non-trivial $(c/d, a/b)$. Linearizing at the non-trivial equilibrium yields eigenvalues $\pm i\sqrt{ac}$ — purely imaginary, so the fixed point is a centre (a degenerate spiral of period $2\pi/\sqrt{ac}$). Centres are non-generic: the centre status survives the linearization only because Lotka–Volterra has a global conserved quantity. Direct calculation:

$H(x,y) \;=\; d x - c \ln x \;+\; b y - a \ln y$

is constant along orbits ($\dot H = 0$ by the chain rule). Each level set $\{H = \mathrm{const}\}$ is a closed curve around the centre, so orbits are periodic. Period depends on amplitude — there's no single oscillation frequency, in contrast to a harmonic oscillator.

Why centres are biologically suspicious. A centre is a non-generic structure: arbitrarily small perturbations turn it into a spiral (either inward — stable focus — or outward — unstable focus). Adding a self-limitation term $-\varepsilon x^2$ to the prey equation collapses the closed orbits into a stable spiral converging to a new equilibrium. So the conserved-orbit picture is a structurally unstable artefact of the conservation law; real predator-prey dynamics seldom show truly periodic boom-bust cycles — but the qualitative phase-lag prediction (prey peak slightly before predators) survives generically.

6. The replicator equation

Replicator dynamics is the deterministic limit of an evolutionary game played by a continuum of agents. Strategies $\{1,\dots,m\}$ have frequencies $x_i \in [0,1]$ with $\sum x_i = 1$ (the simplex $\Delta^{m-1}$). Given a payoff matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$, the fitness of strategy $i$ in the current mix is $f_i(x) = (Ax)_i$, and the population-average fitness is $\bar f(x) = x^\top A x$. The replicator equation says each strategy grows at a rate proportional to its excess fitness:

$\dot x_i \;=\; x_i\bigl(f_i(x) - \bar f(x)\bigr),\qquad i=1,\dots,m.$

This is the right form: it preserves the simplex ($\sum \dot x_i = 0$) and the boundary faces (if $x_i=0$ then $\dot x_i = 0$, so empty strategies stay empty). The fixed points are the points where either $x_i = 0$ or $f_i(x) = \bar f(x)$ — and the latter is exactly the Nash equilibrium condition. So Nash equilibria are the interior fixed points of replicator dynamics.

Rock-paper-scissors and the heteroclinic cycle

Take $m=3$ with the antisymmetric payoff matrix

$A \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & +1 \\ +1 & 0 & -1 \\ -1 & +1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$

Rock beats scissors, scissors beats paper, paper beats rock. The barycentre $(1/3, 1/3, 1/3)$ is the unique interior fixed point (a Nash equilibrium for the mixed strategy "play each with probability $1/3$"). Linearizing there gives imaginary eigenvalues — another centre — and the same conservation argument as Lotka–Volterra: $H(x) = -\sum_i \ln x_i$ (up to constants) is preserved by the antisymmetric replicator flow. Orbits cycle around the barycentre. Adding the slightest payoff asymmetry sends them to either a heteroclinic cycle on the boundary or a stable spiral inward.

Replicator vs. Lotka–Volterra. The two are conjugate in $m=3$: the projection $(x_1,x_2,x_3)\to(x_1/x_3, x_2/x_3)$ from the simplex onto the positive quadrant turns generic replicator dynamics into Lotka–Volterra dynamics with $m-1 = 2$ "effective species." So Lotka–Volterra and replicator are not two different theories — they are the same theory in different coordinates. Smith and Price's evolutionary stable strategies (ESS) are an additional stability concept beyond Nash that picks out the replicator-stable fixed points.

7. Mutation–selection balance

Real alleles aren't neutral or static — there's a constant flux of new mutants. Let $A$ be the wildtype with relative fitness $1$ and $a$ a deleterious mutant with fitness $1-s$ (so $s>0$ is the selection coefficient against $a$). Each generation, every wildtype mutates to $a$ with probability $u$ (the per-locus mutation rate, $u\sim 10^{-8}$ for point mutations in mammals).

In a large population (ignore drift), the deleterious-allele frequency $q$ obeys

$\Delta q \;=\; \underbrace{u(1-q)}_{\text{mutational input}} \;-\; \underbrace{s\, q(1-q)}_{\text{selection removal}}.$

Setting $\Delta q = 0$ and assuming $u\ll s\ll 1$ gives the mutation–selection balance equilibrium

$\hat q \;\approx\; \frac{u}{s}.$

The mutational load — the average fitness reduction relative to a hypothetical mutation-free population — is $L = s\hat q = u$, independent of the selection coefficient. This is Haldane–Muller's principle: load depends only on the mutation rate, not on how harmful each mutation is. Strongly deleterious mutations are kept rare; weakly deleterious ones accumulate, but their per-individual cost is small. The total cost is the same either way.

Mutation–selection equilibrium
drag $u, s$; watch $\hat q$ trace $u/s$

Cyan: deterministic trajectory $q_{n+1} = q_n + u(1-q_n) - s q_n(1-q_n)$ starting from $q_0 = 0$. Yellow dashed: theoretical equilibrium $\hat q = u/s$. The relaxation timescale is $\sim 1/s$ generations — strongly deleterious mutations reach balance fast; weakly deleterious mutations approach $u/s$ slowly but eventually arrive at a higher equilibrium frequency.

The mutation-load paradox in plain English. If every individual carries thousands of deleterious mutations (a real measurement for mammals with $u \sim 1$ deleterious mutation per genome per generation), how does any population survive? Haldane's answer: load is the cost of generating mutations, not the cost of carrying them. Population fitness drops by $u$ — typically a few percent — and stays there. Crow's mutational-meltdown corollary: if $Ns \ll 1$, drift overpowers selection, mutations accumulate without bound, and small populations (endangered species, isolated lineages) can spiral to extinction.

Connections

Population genetics sits at the intersection of probability, dynamical systems, and biology. The frontier today is multi-locus stochastic processes (genealogies on recombining DNA, the ancestral recombination graph), models with structured populations (geographic and demographic effects on coalescent shape), and explicit modelling of selection at very many sites at once (the "background selection" and "genetic draft" theories of Gillespie). Statistical inference from genomic data — ABC, composite likelihood, deep learning on coalescent simulations — is currently the most active applied direction.

  • Probability theory — Markov chains — Wright–Fisher and Moran are the canonical biology examples of finite-state Markov chains with absorbing boundaries.
  • Stochastic processes — discrete martingales — the Wright–Fisher allele frequency is a bounded martingale; fixation probabilities follow from optional stopping.
  • Stochastic calculus — SDE foundations — the Kimura diffusion is the diffusion limit of Wright–Fisher; questions of well-posedness at the degenerate boundary motivated Feller's classification.
  • Dynamical systems — phase portraits — Lotka–Volterra and the replicator are textbook 2D ODEs whose conserved-quantity-and-centre structure is non-generic but pedagogically clean.
  • Large deviations — fixation times in the strongly deleterious regime ($Ns\gg 1$) are large-deviation events; the rate function is Kimura's $1-e^{-2Ns}$.
  • Information theory — Fisher information on allele frequencies (the basis of mutation-rate inference from polymorphism data) and entropy of the site-frequency spectrum.
