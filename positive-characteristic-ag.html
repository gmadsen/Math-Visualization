Positive-characteristic algebraic geometry

Frobenius is the central operator. It splits sheaves (Mehta–Ramanathan), defines $F$-singularities, ramifies wildly (Artin–Schreier), and lifts to the de Rham–Witt complex over Witt vectors. A catalog of phenomena that distinguish characteristic-$p$ geometry from characteristic $0$, and a reminder that "the same theorem, but in characteristic $p$" is rarely the same theorem.

1. The absolute Frobenius and its pushforward

Let $k$ be a perfect field of characteristic $p$ and $X$ a scheme over $k$. The absolute Frobenius is the map $F_X\colon X\to X$ that is the identity on the underlying topological space and acts on sections of $\mathcal{O}_X$ by $f\mapsto f^p$. Over the affine piece $\Spec\,A$, $A$ a $k$-algebra, this is $\Spec$ of the ring map $\varphi\colon A\to A$, $a\mapsto a^p$ — a ring map only because the binomial coefficients $\binom{p}{i}$ vanish mod $p$ for $0

The absolute Frobenius is not a morphism over $k$ — it is the identity on residue fields only because $k$ is perfect, and even then it is $k$-semilinear: $\varphi(\lambda a)=\lambda^p a^p$, not $\lambda\varphi(a)$. To get a genuine $k$-morphism one introduces a base change. Write $X^{(p)} := X\times_{k,\sigma} k$, the Frobenius twist of $X$, where $\sigma\colon k\to k$ is the Frobenius of the base field. The relative Frobenius $F_{X/k}\colon X\to X^{(p)}$ is the unique $k$-morphism filling the square below; composing with the structure projection $X^{(p)}\to X$ recovers the absolute $F_X$.

For $X=\Spec\,A$ smooth over $k$ of dimension $n$, the Frobenius pushforward $F_*\mathcal{O}_X$ is locally the $A$-module $A$, but with the new action given by Frobenius — that is, $a\cdot m := a^p m$. Equivalently, $F_*\mathcal{O}_X$ is a sheaf of $\mathcal{O}_X$-algebras locally free of rank $p^n$, with basis given by the monomials $x_1^{i_1}\cdots x_n^{i_n}$ for $0\le i_jonly for $F$ finite, which on smooth varieties is automatic; over a non-perfect field the picture deteriorates and many statements below require working over a perfect base.

Frobenius is finite of degree $p^{\dim X}$. On a smooth $n$-fold the rank is $p^n$, so $F$ has degree $p^n$. This is the algebraic shadow of "raising to the $p$-th power is a $p$-to-$1$ map on the affine line."

2. Frobenius splittings and Mehta–Ramanathan vanishing

The natural map $\mathcal{O}_X\to F_*\mathcal{O}_X$ sending $f$ to $f$ (as an element of the pushforward, where $\mathcal{O}_X$ acts by $a\cdot f=a^pf$) is the algebraic incarnation of the inclusion of constants into degree-$p$ monomials. A scheme $X$ is Frobenius split if this map admits an $\mathcal{O}_X$-linear retraction $\varphi\colon F_*\mathcal{O}_X\to\mathcal{O}_X$ with $\varphi(1)=1$.

Equivalent local form. On a smooth affine $\Spec\,A$ with étale coordinates $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, a splitting is an $A$-linear map $\varphi\colon A\to A$ (where the source $A$ is the rank-$p^n$ module $F_*A$) with $\varphi(1)=1$ and $\varphi(a^p f)=a\varphi(f)$. The "trace" splitting $\varphi(x_1^{i_1}\cdots x_n^{i_n})=$ the monomial obtained by replacing each $x_j^{i_j}$ by $x_j^{(i_j-(p-1))/p}$ when all $i_j=p-1$ and zero otherwise has $\varphi(x_1^{p-1}\cdots x_n^{p-1})=1$.

Frobenius splittings are not generic: most smooth varieties are not split. But many natural examples are: smooth toric varieties, flag varieties $G/B$, Schubert varieties, and ordinary abelian varieties all carry canonical splittings. 3. The Cartier operator

The de Rham complex $\Omega^\bullet_{X/k}$ behaves strangely in characteristic $p$. Its cohomology is the algebraic de Rham cohomology, but the Frobenius action introduces an extra structure: although $d(f^p)=pf^{p-1}df=0$, the form $f^{p-1}df$ is genuinely closed and not always exact. The Cartier operator packages this into an isomorphism.

Cartier isomorphism (1957). For $X/k$ smooth of dimension $n$ with $k$ perfect, there is a canonical isomorphism of $\mathcal{O}_{X^{(p)}}$-modules $$C^{-1}\colon\,\Omega^i_{X^{(p)}/k}\;\xrightarrow{\;\sim\;}\;\mathcal{H}^i(F_*\Omega^\bullet_{X/k})$$ characterised on degree-$1$ generators by $C^{-1}(d\bar x)=[x^{p-1}dx]$ in the cohomology of the Frobenius pushforward of the de Rham complex.

The Cartier operator proper is its inverse $C\colon\mathcal{H}^i(F_*\Omega^\bullet_{X/k})\to\Omega^i_{X^{(p)}/k}$. The point: in characteristic $0$ the de Rham complex of a smooth affine is exact in positive degrees (Poincaré lemma); in characteristic $p$ it is not, and the Cartier operator measures the failure exactly. The kernel of $C$ is generated by exact differentials, but the image of $C$ identifies the de Rham cohomology with a Frobenius twist of the original differential forms.

The widget exhibits the core mechanism: the Cartier operator extracts the "$x^{p-1}\,dx$"-type terms (the genuinely $p$-typical differentials) and discards the rest (which lie in the image of $d$ and hence vanish in cohomology). The Cartier isomorphism is the Hodge-to-de-Rham degeneration in characteristic $p$, and the Deligne–Illusie theorem (1987) lifts it to a quasi-isomorphism $\bigoplus_i\Omega^i_{X/k}[-i]\xrightarrow{\sim}F_*\Omega^\bullet_{X/k}$ when $X$ admits a $W_2(k)$-lift — proving Hodge-to-de-Rham degeneration without complex-analytic methods.

4. $F$-singularities

The Frobenius pushforward is a finite-rank $\mathcal{O}_X$-module whose structure detects how singular $X$ is. The "$F$-singularity" zoo classifies local rings by how nicely $F_*\mathcal{O}_X$ behaves — a positive-characteristic analog of the singularities-of-the-MMP hierarchy (klt, log-canonical, rational, terminal) but defined intrinsically by Frobenius rather than by resolutions.

Reduction mod $p$ as a bridge. Hara–Watanabe (klt $\Leftrightarrow$ $F$-regular for $p\gg 0$), Smith and Hara independently (rational $\Leftrightarrow$ $F$-rational for $p\gg 0$): the MMP hierarchy in characteristic $0$ is the $F$-singularity hierarchy under reduction mod $p$, the equivalence holding in the asymptotic-$p$ sense (a fixed singularity may be klt yet $F$-singular at finitely many bad primes). The positive-characteristic side is intrinsic (no resolution required); the bridge is mediated by the asymptotic behaviour of Frobenius. Tight closure (Hochster–Huneke 1990) is the technical engine.

5. Wild ramification and Artin–Schreier covers

The completion of a discrete valuation ring of mixed or equal characteristic $p$ admits Galois extensions of a fundamentally different shape than its characteristic-$0$ analog. The basic example: Artin–Schreier covers.

Definition. Let $k$ be a field of characteristic $p$ and $f(x)\in k[x]$. The Artin–Schreier curve is the affine plane curve $\mathcal{C}_f\colon\, y^p-y=f(x)$ over $k$. The projection $\mathcal{C}_f\to\mathbb{A}^1_k$, $(x,y)\mapsto x$, is a finite étale Galois cover of degree $p$ with Galois group $\mathbb{Z}/p$ generated by $\tau\colon y\mapsto y+1$, away from the locus where $f$ has poles after compactification.

What happens at the poles of $f$ on $\mathbb{P}^1$ is dramatic: the cover is wildly ramified. The ramification jump (Swan conductor) records not just whether $f$ has a pole but the order of the pole, after stripping out a "tame" piece via the Artin–Schreier symbol $f\sim f+\wp(g)$ where $\wp(g)=g^p-g$. The genus computation reflects this — Riemann–Hurwitz gives $g(\overline{\mathcal{C}_f})$ as a function of the Swan conductors at each pole.

The genus jumps in unfamiliar steps: holding $f=x^3$ and varying $p\in\{2,3,5,7\}$, the genus goes $1\to 0\to 4\to 6$. The drop at $p=3$ comes from $p\mid m$ — the Artin–Schreier substitution $y\to y-x$ trivialises the cover. Wild ramification is sensitive to the residue characteristic in a way no tame cover is: in characteristic $0$ the genus depends only on the degree and the branch profile, not on which prime "is" the characteristic.

Why "wild". The inertia group at a wild ramification point has a nontrivial $p$-part. Higher-ramification filtration of inertia (upper numbering) gives the Swan conductor as an integer-valued invariant; it is the "right" measure of wildness and enters the Grothendieck–Ogg–Shafarevich Euler-characteristic formula on a curve.

6. Witt vectors and the de Rham–Witt complex

The Witt vectors $W(k)$ are the universal characteristic-$0$ DVR with residue field $k$: for $k=\mathbb{F}_p$, $W(\mathbb{F}_p)=\mathbb{Z}_p$; for $k=\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$, $W(\mathbb{F}_{p^n})$ is the unique unramified extension of $\mathbb{Z}_p$ of degree $n$. The construction is striking: as a set, $W_n(A)=A^n$, but the ring operations are given by universal Witt polynomials that look insane (the addition formula for $W_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$ involves a "carry bit" $C_p(a_0,b_0)=\frac{(a_0+b_0)^p-a_0^p-b_0^p}{p}$ that is genuinely a polynomial over $\mathbb{Z}$).

The de Rham–Witt complex $W_n\Omega^\bullet_X$ of Bloch–Illusie is the "right" cohomology that interpolates between de Rham and crystalline: $W_1\Omega^\bullet_X = \Omega^\bullet_X$, and $H^*_{\mathrm{cris}}(X/W) = \varprojlim_n H^*(X, W_n\Omega^\bullet_X)$. Each $W_n\Omega^j_X$ is a sheaf of $W_n(k)$-modules, and the complex carries three operators interlocking on a bicomplex: differential $d$, Frobenius $F\colon W_{n+1}\Omega^j\to W_n\Omega^j$ (raising slopes by $p$), and Verschiebung $V\colon W_n\Omega^j\to W_{n+1}\Omega^j$ (the "shift" embedding lower levels). $F$ and $V$ satisfy $FV=p=VF$ on slopes, and the slope filtration recovers the Newton polygon of Frobenius on crystalline cohomology.

For $X/k$ smooth proper the Bloch–Illusie–Deligne theorem says $H^*_{\mathrm{cris}}(X/W) = H^*(X_{\mathrm{Zar}}, W\Omega^\bullet_X)$ — crystalline cohomology is the hypercohomology of the de Rham–Witt complex. The Newton polygon decomposition of $\varphi$ on crystalline cohomology corresponds exactly to the slope decomposition of $W\Omega^\bullet$. So the de Rham–Witt complex is the computational engine of crystalline cohomology, just as the de Rham complex is the engine of algebraic de Rham cohomology.

7. Pathologies of positive characteristic

Many theorems that hold in characteristic $0$ fail in characteristic $p$, sometimes in spectacular ways. The pathology catalog is part of the subject — these are not bugs to be patched but features that constrain what theorems can possibly be true.

The Bloch–Kato conjecture's positive-char roots. Many of the deepest results in $p$-adic Hodge theory (Bloch–Kato, Fontaine–Mazur, the Voevodsky proof of Milnor's conjecture) are positive-characteristic origin stories — the $p$-adic completions and motivic structures necessary to even state the conjectures came out of trying to fix the gaps that characteristic-$p$ pathologies opened. Failure modes are productive: they force new invariants.

Connections

Positive-characteristic algebraic geometry is partly a closed theory (Frobenius splittings, $F$-singularities, Cartier operator, de Rham–Witt) and partly a programmable bridge into adjacent areas. Open frontiers include resolution of singularities in characteristic $p$ for dimension $\ge 4$, the full positive-characteristic MMP, the precise correspondence between $F$-singularities and MMP singularities for all primes, and structural understanding of liftability obstructions.

  • Crystalline cohomology — the $\ell=p$ gap — the de Rham–Witt complex computes crystalline cohomology; that's the direct downstream consumer.
  • Étale cohomology — Frobenius trace — Frobenius eigenvalues on $\ell$-adic and crystalline match (Katz–Messing), closing the Weil-conjectures package at every prime.
  • Frobenius elements — the Galois-theoretic and geometric Frobenius are the same operator viewed two ways; Artin–Schreier covers exhibit the geometric side.
  • Algebraic de Rham complex — Cartier isomorphism is the de Rham-of-Frobenius-pushforward; Deligne–Illusie lifts it.
  • Reduction mod $p$ — the route by which characteristic-$0$ varieties enter positive-characteristic geometry; $F$-singularities probe the resulting fibres.
  • Resolution of singularities — Hironaka's theorem holds in characteristic $0$; in characteristic $p$ it is the major open problem for $\dim\ge 4$.
