Spectral methods for massive data sets

When a data matrix is too big to look at, its top few singular vectors usually are. SVD compresses, PCA denoises, the graph Laplacian clusters, PageRank ranks, and randomized projections shrink — five spectral moves that turn $n \times n$ problems into $k \times k$ ones with $k \ll n$.

1. The singular value decomposition

Every real $m\times n$ matrix factors as

$A \;=\; U \Sigma V^{T} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^{T},$

where $U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal, $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is "diagonal" with non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_r > 0$ down its leading block, and $r = \operatorname{rank} A$. The $\sigma_i$ are the singular values; the columns of $U$ and $V$ are the left and right singular vectors. Geometrically, $A$ acts as rotate by $V^T$, scale axis $i$ by $\sigma_i$, rotate by $U$: any linear map is three rigid moves and a stretch.

The decomposition exists because $A^T A$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, so it diagonalises orthogonally: $A^T A = V\,\Sigma^T\Sigma\, V^T$ gives the right singular vectors and the squared singular values. Setting $u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i$ for $\sigma_i > 0$ extends to a full orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^m$. The image $\operatorname{range}(A) = \operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_r)$ and the kernel $\operatorname{ker}(A) = \operatorname{span}(v_{r+1},\dots,v_n)$ are read off the columns.

Eckart–Young: optimal rank-$k$ approximation

The decomposition encodes how compressible $A$ is. Let $A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i u_i v_i^{T}$ be the truncation at rank $k$. Then

$\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le k} \|A - B\|_{F}^{2} \;=\; \|A - A_k\|_{F}^{2} \;=\; \sum_{i=k+1}^{r}\sigma_i^{2},$

and the same minimum (with norm replaced by $\sigma_{k+1}$) holds in the operator $2$-norm. This is the Eckart–Young theorem (Frobenius and operator-2-norm cases); Mirsky's theorem (1960) extends optimality to any unitarily invariant norm, so the combined statement is often called Eckart–Young–Mirsky. The SVD is not just a low-rank approximation — among all rank-$\le k$ matrices, it is the best one in every such norm. Tail singular values are the irreducible error you accept by truncating.

Sense of scale. The full SVD costs $O(\min(mn^2, m^2n))$ — punishing when $m=n=10^6$. But the top $k$ singular vectors can be extracted by Krylov methods (Lanczos, ARPACK) in $O(k \cdot \mathrm{nnz}(A))$ time, and by randomized SVD (§6) in essentially $O(k\cdot mn)$ for dense $A$. The economics of spectral methods rest on never materialising the full decomposition.

2. Principal component analysis

Given $n$ data points $x_1,\dots,x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$, stack them row-wise into $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$. Principal component analysis is a three-step recipe:

  1. Center. Subtract the column mean: $\tilde X = X - \mathbf{1}\bar x^{T}$ where $\bar x = \tfrac1n \sum_i x_i$.
  2. Decompose. Compute the SVD $\tilde X = U\Sigma V^{T}$ (or equivalently the eigendecomposition of the sample covariance $S = \tfrac1n \tilde X^{T}\tilde X = V \tfrac{1}{n}\Sigma^{2} V^{T}$).
  3. Project. The first $k$ right singular vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k$ are the principal directions; the $i$th row of $\tilde X V_{:,1:k} = U_{:,1:k} \Sigma_{1:k}$ is the embedding of $x_i$ in $\mathbb{R}^k$.

The $j$th principal component captures variance $\sigma_j^{2}/n$, and by Eckart–Young the rank-$k$ projection is the variance-maximising orthogonal projection. The fraction $\sum_{j\le k}\sigma_j^{2}\;/\;\sum_{j\le r}\sigma_j^{2}$ is the variance explained; plotting it (or the singular values themselves) versus $k$ produces the scree plot — and the heuristic "elbow" $k^{*}$ at which the curve plateaus is the standard cutoff for how many components to keep.

Two warnings that real practitioners learn the hard way:

  • PCA is scale-sensitive. If the columns of $X$ have wildly different units (heights in metres, masses in grams), the biggest singular value comes from the noisiest variable. Always rescale features to comparable variance (the "correlation matrix" PCA) unless the units genuinely match.
  • Variance captured is not signal captured. If the noise has higher variance than the signal — say, sensor jitter dominating a faint structural mode — PCA happily denoises in the wrong direction. The Marchenko–Pastur distribution diagnoses this: any singular value inside the random-matrix bulk is consistent with noise and should be discarded.

3. The graph Laplacian

Given a weighted undirected graph $G=(V,E,w)$ on $n$ vertices, define the degree matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n)$ with $d_i = \sum_{j} w_{ij}$ and the adjacency matrix $A$ with $A_{ij}=w_{ij}$. The (combinatorial) Laplacian is

$L \;=\; D - A.$

$L$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite. The quadratic form $f^{T}Lf = \sum_{ij\in E} w_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^{2}$ is the Dirichlet energy — the discrete analogue of $\int \|\nabla f\|^{2}$. The spectrum $0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \cdots \le \lambda_n$ tells you:

  • Components. $\dim \ker L$ = number of connected components of $G$. The kernel is spanned by indicator vectors of components.
  • Algebraic connectivity. $\lambda_2$, the second-smallest eigenvalue, is zero iff $G$ disconnects. Its eigenvector — the Fiedler vector — bisects $G$ by sign.
  • Cluster count. If $\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_k$ are all small and $\lambda_{k+1}$ is much larger, the graph behaves like $k$ weakly-connected pieces. This is the foundation of spectral clustering.

Two normalised variants live in the literature. The symmetric normalised Laplacian $L_{\mathrm{sym}} = D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2} = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}$ has eigenvalues in $[0,2]$, with $2$ achieved iff $G$ has a bipartite component. The random-walk Laplacian $L_{\mathrm{rw}} = D^{-1}L = I - D^{-1}A$ is similar (not symmetric) but shares the same spectrum as $L_{\mathrm{sym}}$ and connects directly to the simple random walk on $G$ — see §5.

4. Spectral clustering

If a graph has $k$ near-components, the bottom $k$ eigenvectors of $L$ are "nearly piecewise constant" on those components. The Ng–Jordan–Weiss algorithm turns that observation into a clustering recipe:

  1. Compute $L_{\mathrm{sym}} = I - D^{-1/2} A D^{-1/2}$.
  2. Take the bottom $k$ eigenvectors $u_1,\dots,u_k$ and stack as columns of $U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$.
  3. Row-normalise to unit length: $\tilde U_{i\cdot} = U_{i\cdot}/\|U_{i\cdot}\|$.
  4. Run $k$-means on the rows of $\tilde U$.

Each vertex becomes a point on $S^{k-1}$ inside $\mathbb{R}^k$, and vertices in the same near-component land near each other. The reason it works is a relaxation story: the discrete normalised cut problem

$\min_{S\subset V} \;\mathrm{Ncut}(S, \bar S) \;=\; \frac{w(S, \bar S)}{\operatorname{vol}(S)} + \frac{w(S, \bar S)}{\operatorname{vol}(\bar S)}$

is NP-hard, but its continuous relaxation — drop the discreteness of the volume-weighted indicator $f_i \in \{\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(\bar S)/\operatorname{vol}(S)}, -\sqrt{\operatorname{vol}(S)/\operatorname{vol}(\bar S)}\}$ and allow $f$ to range over $\mathbb{R}^n$ — is exactly the eigenvalue problem $L_{\mathrm{sym}} u = \lambda u$. (The simpler $\pm 1$ relaxation belongs instead to RatioCut + unnormalised $L$.) The bottom $k$ eigenvectors are the relaxed optima; $k$-means in the embedded space is one way to project back to a real partition.

The quantitative half is Cheeger's inequality: for the second eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ of $L_{\mathrm{sym}}$,

$\frac{\lambda_2}{2} \;\le\; h(G) \;\le\; \sqrt{2\lambda_2}.$

The conductance $h(G) = \min_S w(S,\bar S)/\min(\operatorname{vol}(S),\operatorname{vol}(\bar S))$ measures how "bottlenecked" the graph is. The bound says: small $\lambda_2$ guarantees a small cut exists, and the proof's upper-bound construction — the spectral sweep cut — is a quadratic ($O(\sqrt{h_{\mathrm{OPT}}})$) approximation algorithm for sparsest cut: the bounds $\lambda_2/2 \le h \le \sqrt{2\lambda_2}$ themselves rule out a constant-factor guarantee. Constant-factor / $O(\log n)$ approximations require LP/SDP rounding (Leighton–Rao, Arora–Rao–Vazirani). The same machinery generalises to higher $\lambda_k$ via higher-order Cheeger inequalities (Lee–Oveis-Gharan–Trevisan 2014).

5. PageRank

Brin and Page's 1998 ranking algorithm imagined a "random surfer" who at each step follows an outgoing link uniformly at random — or, with probability $1-\alpha$, teleports to a random page chosen uniformly from the web. The stationary distribution of this Markov chain is what we call PageRank. Concretely, given a directed graph with adjacency $A$, let $P$ be the column-stochastic transition matrix ($P_{ij} = A_{ji}/d^{\mathrm{out}}_j$, with sinks restarted to the uniform distribution). The PageRank vector $r$ solves

$r \;=\; \alpha\, P r \;+\; (1-\alpha)\, \tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1},$

where $\alpha\approx 0.85$ is the damping factor. Equivalently, $r$ is the dominant eigenvector of the perturbed matrix $G_\alpha = \alpha P + (1-\alpha)\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}$. The teleportation $(1-\alpha)/n$ rank-one term guarantees $G_\alpha$ is irreducible and aperiodic, so Perron–Frobenius applies and the stationary distribution is unique, positive on every vertex, and reached from any starting distribution by power iteration:

$r^{(t+1)} \;=\; \alpha\, P\, r^{(t)} \;+\; (1-\alpha)\,\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}.$

The convergence rate is geometric in the second eigenvalue of $G_\alpha$, which is at most $\alpha$ — so $\alpha=0.85$ delivers $\sim 50$ iterations for $10^{-6}$ accuracy regardless of graph size. That is the engineering miracle that made PageRank ship: the spectral gap of the teleportation perturbation does not degrade with $n$.

PageRank's deep relative is the personalised PageRank, where the uniform teleport $\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}$ is replaced by a "preference" distribution $v$ supported near a user's interests. The same eigenvalue equation $r = \alpha P r + (1-\alpha) v$ then ranks pages by personalised reachability — and approximate personalised PageRank powers a surprising amount of modern recommendation infrastructure and local graph clustering (Andersen–Chung–Lang 2006). The PageRank vector is the discrete analogue of the heat kernel: it spreads mass from the seed at rate $\alpha$ with restart rate $1-\alpha$.

6. Randomized SVD and Johnson–Lindenstrauss

Computing the top $k$ singular vectors of an $n\times n$ dense matrix by Lanczos takes $O(kn^{2})$ flops — already painful at $n=10^{5}$. The randomized SVD (Halko–Martinsson–Tropp 2011) does it in a single dense matrix-matrix product plus a tiny SVD, with provable error bounds:

  1. Draw $\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times (k+p)}$ with i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ entries, where $p\ge 5$ is a small "oversampling" parameter.
  2. Compute $Y = A\,\Omega$. The columns of $Y$ span an approximate basis for the dominant subspace of $A$.
  3. QR-decompose $Y = QR$. Then $Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times (k+p)}$ has orthonormal columns and $A \approx QQ^{T} A$.
  4. Compute the small SVD $Q^{T}A = \hat U\hat\Sigma V^{T}$ and set $U = Q\hat U$.

The error obeys $\mathbb{E}\,\|A - QQ^{T}A\|_{F}^{2} \;\le\; \Bigl(1 + \tfrac{k}{p-1}\Bigr) \sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^{2}$ — only a small constant worse than Eckart–Young, and the total flop count is dominated by the single $A\,\Omega$ multiplication. The reason it works is that a random $k$-dim subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ has, with high probability, large overlap with any fixed $k$-dim subspace — so $\Omega$'s columns almost surely sit transverse to $\operatorname{ker}(A) + (\text{tail singular directions})$.

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

The same kind of random-projection magic is the engine of all distance-preserving sketches. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma: for any $n$ points $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and any $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, if $k \;\ge\; \tfrac{8\log n}{\varepsilon^{2}}$ and $\Pi\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$ has i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0,1/k)$ entries, then with probability $\ge 1-1/n$,

$(1-\varepsilon)\,\|x_i - x_j\|^{2} \;\le\; \|\Pi x_i - \Pi x_j\|^{2} \;\le\; (1+\varepsilon)\,\|x_i - x_j\|^{2} \quad \text{for all } i,j.$

The target dimension $k$ is independent of the original $d$ — only the number of points $n$ matters. This is the high-dimensional concentration story (cf. high-dimensional geometry): pairwise distances are stable under random rotations into $\sim\log n$ dimensions. Any algorithm that depends only on pairwise distances — nearest-neighbour search, $k$-means, MDS — can be applied to the sketch $\Pi x_i$ instead of $x_i$ with logarithmic dimension blowup.

Randomized SVD ≈ JL applied to columns. The randomized SVD's first step — $Y = A\,\Omega$ — is exactly a JL sketch of the column space of $A$. The reason both schemes work is the same concentration phenomenon: in high dimensions a random Gaussian almost surely sits well-aligned with any fixed low-dimensional subspace.

7. The Nyström method

The last spectral move is for kernel methods. Given a positive semidefinite kernel $K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ (Gaussian kernel, polynomial kernel, graph diffusion kernel, ...) that you want to use for SVM, kernel PCA, or Gaussian-process regression, the explicit matrix is unaffordable at $n=10^{6}$. The Nyström approximation: pick $m \ll n$ "landmarks" $i_1,\dots,i_m$, build the small blocks

$K_{mm} = K_{[i_1\cdots i_m],[i_1\cdots i_m]} \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}, \qquad K_{nm} = K_{[1\cdots n],[i_1\cdots i_m]} \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},$

and approximate the full kernel by

$K \;\approx\; \tilde K \;=\; K_{nm}\, K_{mm}^{+}\, K_{nm}^{T},$

where $K_{mm}^{+}$ is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. Storage drops from $O(n^{2})$ to $O(nm)$ and computing $\tilde K v$ costs $O(nm + m^{2})$ instead of $O(n^{2})$. Equivalently: read off the eigendecomposition $K_{mm} = U_m\Lambda_m U_m^{T}$ and extend each landmark eigenvector $u^{(m)}_\ell$ to a full-length eigenvector $\tilde u_\ell = \tfrac{1}{\lambda_\ell} K_{nm} u^{(m)}_\ell$ — these solve $\tilde K \tilde u_\ell = \lambda_\ell^{(\tilde K)} \tilde u_\ell$ with $\lambda_\ell^{(\tilde K)} = (n/m)\lambda_\ell$ (an implicit $n/m$ rescaling, equivalent to working with the symmetric form $K_{mm}^{-1/2} K_{nm}^T K_{nm} K_{mm}^{-1/2}$). The (rescaled) eigenvalues of $\tilde K$ approximate those of $K$; the eigenvectors are the natural out-of-sample extension from the landmark set to the full data.

How well does it work? When the kernel is smooth (decays fast in feature space) and the landmarks are picked by leverage-score sampling rather than uniformly, $m = O(k\log k / \varepsilon^{2})$ landmarks suffice to approximate the top-$k$ eigendecomposition to relative error $\varepsilon$ (Drineas–Mahoney 2005; Alaoui–Mahoney 2015). The landmark-selection step is itself a spectral task — leverage scores are diagonal entries of a projection onto the dominant subspace.

8. Connections

Spectral methods are the place where pure linear algebra meets the realities of $n=10^{9}$. The spectacular successes (SVD compression, PageRank, Laplacian eigenmaps) all share a structure — extract a rank-$k$ approximation, then do everything else in $\mathbb{R}^{k}$. The open frontier is when that compression is faithful: random matrix theory gives the asymptotic story, but quantitative deviation inequalities for spiked models, kernel matrices, and structured noise are an active area. On the algorithmic side, sketching, streaming, and "communication-avoiding" linear algebra push spectral methods into the regime where the matrix can't even fit on one machine.

  • Spectral graph theory — spectral clustering — the combinatorics view: Cheeger's inequality, expanders, Ramanujan graphs.
  • Random matrix theory — Marchenko–Pastur — when a singular value lies inside the noise bulk, throw it out.
  • High-dimensional geometry — Johnson–Lindenstrauss — the concentration of measure behind every random projection bound.
  • Random walks and mixing — spectral gap — PageRank's convergence rate is the spectral gap of the teleportation chain.
  • Information theory — the rate-distortion view: SVD truncation is the Gaussian information bottleneck.
