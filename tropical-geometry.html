Tropical geometry

Replace $(+,\cdot)$ with $(\min,+)$ and watch algebraic geometry collapse into piecewise-linear combinatorics — yet keep its core theorems: Bézout becomes mixed volume, Riemann–Roch becomes chip-firing, Gromov–Witten counts become Mikhalkin's lattice counts on a fan.

Reader's note. This page assumes a first acquaintance with algebraic curves, the Newton polygon of a polynomial, and the basic vocabulary of moduli. Tropical geometry is the combinatorial shadow of algebraic geometry: every variety has a piecewise-linear caricature that remembers enough of the original to compute intersection numbers, count curves, and even reconstruct the variety up to deformation in some cases. Aside from being a powerful computational tool, it is the natural arena for mirror symmetry, phylogenetics, and the limit-of-large-degenerations approach to enumerative geometry.

1. The tropical semiring

The tropical semiring $(\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}, \oplus, \otimes)$ is built from real numbers by the substitution

$a \oplus b \;:=\; \min(a,b), \qquad a \otimes b \;:=\; a + b.$

Both operations are commutative and associative; $\otimes$ distributes over $\oplus$ because $a + \min(b,c) = \min(a+b,a+c)$. The element $\infty$ is the additive identity ($\min(a,\infty)=a$) and $0$ is the multiplicative identity ($a+0=a$). What is missing is additive inverses: $\min(a,b)=\infty$ forces $a=b=\infty$, so most elements have no negative. Hence the structure is a semiring, not a ring — and it is the source of all the geometric peculiarities that follow.

A tropical polynomial in one variable is a finite tropical sum of tropical monomials,

$f(x) \;=\; a_0 \oplus (a_1 \otimes x) \oplus (a_2 \otimes x^{\otimes 2}) \oplus \cdots \;=\; \min_i (a_i + i\,x).$

This is the lower envelope of finitely many affine functions of $x$ — a piecewise-linear concave function with integer slopes. The corner locus $V(f)$, where the minimum is attained at least twice, is the tropical hypersurface: a finite set of points in dimension 1, a piecewise-linear graph in dimension 2.

Why the name "tropical"? The semiring (and its $(\max,+)$ twin) was studied by the Brazilian computer scientist Imre Simon in the 1980s; "tropical" was his French colleagues' nickname for the semiring, in honour of his being São Paulo-based. The name stuck.

Geometrically, $V(f)$ is exactly the set of break points of $f$ — the kinks of the piecewise-linear curve. In two variables, the same construction gives a piecewise-linear graph in $\mathbb{R}^2$: a tropical curve.

Take-away. One substitution — $(+,\cdot) \rightsquigarrow (\min,+)$ — promotes polynomial roots to corner loci of piecewise-linear functions. The combinatorial structure of these corners is the entire content of tropical geometry.

2. Tropical curves and the balancing condition

In two variables a tropical polynomial is

$f(x,y) \;=\; \min_{(i,j) \in S} \bigl(a_{ij} + i\,x + j\,y\bigr),$

indexed by a finite set $S \subset \mathbb{Z}^2_{\ge 0}$ — the support of $f$. Its corner locus $V(f) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is a tropical curve: a finite piecewise-linear graph whose edges have rational slopes, weighted by integers (the lattice length of the dual edge of the Newton polygon, see §3).

The simplest example is a tropical line $f(x,y) = \min(x, y, c)$: three half-rays meeting at the point $(c,c)$ in the directions $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(-1,-1)$. A tropical conic is a piecewise-linear graph with three trivalent vertices and six unbounded rays, each ray pointing in one of the directions normal to an edge of the Newton triangle $\{(0,0),(2,0),(0,2)\}$.

The combinatorial signature of being a 1-cycle is the balancing condition: at every vertex $v$, the weighted sum of primitive outgoing edge directions vanishes,

$\sum_{e \ni v} w_e \cdot u_e \;=\; 0 \in \mathbb{Z}^2,$

where $u_e \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ is the primitive integer vector pointing along $e$ away from $v$ and $w_e \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ is the edge weight. This is the discrete analog of the conservation law $\partial[C]=0$ for a $1$-cycle: edges flow in and out with conserved magnitude.

Lattice direction vectors come from the geometry of the Newton polygon: an edge of $V(f)$ dual to a Newton-polygon edge with primitive normal $u$ and lattice length $\ell$ has direction $u$ and weight $w_e = \ell$. Each unbounded ray of $V(f)$ corresponds to an edge of $\partial \mathrm{Newt}(f)$.

3. Tropicalization and the valuation map

Where do tropical curves come from? They are the shadows of honest algebraic varieties under a non-archimedean valuation. Let $K$ be a field with a non-archimedean valuation $v: K^* \to \mathbb{R}$ — for example, the Puiseux series $K = \mathbb{C}\{\!\{t\}\!\} = \bigcup_{n \ge 1} \mathbb{C}((t^{1/n}))$ with $v(\sum c_q t^q) = \min\{q : c_q \ne 0\}$. The valuation extends componentwise to $\mathrm{val}: (K^*)^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathrm{val}(z_1,\ldots,z_n) = (v(z_1),\ldots,v(z_n))$.

For a subvariety $X \subset (K^*)^n$, the tropicalization is

$\mathrm{Trop}(X) \;:=\; \overline{\mathrm{val}\bigl(X(\bar K)\bigr)} \subset \mathbb{R}^n.$

The Bieri–Groves theorem says $\mathrm{Trop}(X)$ is a polyhedral complex of pure dimension $\dim X$; if $X = V(f)$ is a hypersurface, $\mathrm{Trop}(X) = V(\mathrm{trop}(f))$, where the tropicalized polynomial is

$\mathrm{trop}\!\Bigl(\sum_\alpha c_\alpha\,z^\alpha\Bigr) \;=\; \min_\alpha\bigl(v(c_\alpha) + \alpha \cdot x\bigr).$

The Newton polytope $\mathrm{Newt}(f) = \mathrm{conv}\{\alpha : c_\alpha \ne 0\} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ records the supports. The valuations $v(c_\alpha)$ induce a regular subdivision of $\mathrm{Newt}(f)$ by lifting each vertex $\alpha$ to height $v(c_\alpha) \in \mathbb{R}$ and projecting the lower convex hull. The tropical hypersurface is dual to this subdivision: its vertices are dual to the regions of $\mathrm{Newt}(f)$, its edges dual to internal edges, its rays dual to boundary edges. This duality is what turns tropical geometry into discrete polytope combinatorics.

Two consequences worth taking with you. First, every algebraic curve over $K$ has a tropical shadow, and the shadow remembers the degree (its support is the Newton polygon up to translation), the genus (the first Betti number of the curve, by a theorem of Mikhalkin), and many intersection numbers. Second, conversely, every tropical curve lifts to an algebraic curve over some sufficiently large field — but the lift is highly non-unique. Tropicalization compresses an enormous moduli space onto a finite combinatorial gadget.

4. Bernstein's theorem and stable intersection

Bézout in disguise. Two plane curves of degrees $d_1, d_2$ in $\mathbb{P}^2(\mathbb{C})$ meet in $d_1 d_2$ points (counted with multiplicity). Tropically, the same count emerges as a mixed volume:

$|C_1 \cap C_2| \;=\; \mathrm{MV}\bigl(\mathrm{Newt}(f_1), \mathrm{Newt}(f_2)\bigr).$

For two curves of degrees $d_1,d_2$ both inscribed in a triangle, $\mathrm{MV}(\Delta_{d_1}, \Delta_{d_2}) = d_1 d_2$ — recovering classical Bézout. The far-reaching Bernstein–Kushnirenko theorem generalises this: the number of solutions in $(\mathbb{C}^*)^n$ of a generic system $f_1 = \cdots = f_n = 0$ equals the mixed volume $n!\,\mathrm{MV}(\mathrm{Newt}(f_1), \ldots, \mathrm{Newt}(f_n))$.

The stability problem. Naive set-theoretic intersection of tropical curves can be 1-dimensional: two tropical lines that share an unbounded ray meet along the entire ray, not in a point. The fix is to translate one curve generically and take a limit:

$C_1 \cdot_{\mathrm{stab}} C_2 \;:=\; \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+}\;C_1 \,\cap\, (C_2 + \varepsilon v),$

for $v \in \mathbb{R}^2$ generic. The result is a finite multiset of points whose total weight is exactly the mixed volume. This stable intersection is the right tropical analog of intersection multiplicity.

5. Tropical moduli and Riemann–Roch

An abstract tropical curve is a metric graph $\Gamma$: a finite graph whose edges have positive real lengths, possibly with infinite-length "leaves" carrying marked points. The genus of $\Gamma$ is the first Betti number $g(\Gamma) = |E| - |V| + 1$ — the number of independent cycles. For genus $g \ge 2$ stable means every vertex of valence $\le 2$ is removed (or has a positive genus weight). The set of stable tropical curves of genus $g$ with $n$ marks forms the tropical moduli space $M^{\mathrm{trop}}_{g,n}$, a polyhedral complex of dimension $3g - 3 + n$ (matching $\dim \mathcal{M}_{g,n}$). It is the skeleton of the Berkovich analytification of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$ — a recurring theme in non-archimedean / Berkovich geometry.

The companion theorem is Baker–Norine Riemann–Roch on a metric graph $\Gamma$ of genus $g$. Define a divisor $D = \sum_v D(v)\cdot v$ as a formal $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of points on $\Gamma$, and the rank $r(D)$ via the chip-firing game: $r(D) = \max\{r : \forall E \ge 0, \deg E = r \Rightarrow D - E \text{ effective up to chip-firing}\}$. Then:

$r(D) - r(K_\Gamma - D) \;=\; \deg D \,-\, g \,+\, 1,$

where $K_\Gamma = \sum_v (\deg(v) - 2)\,v$ is the canonical divisor of the graph. This is identical in form to the classical Riemann–Roch on a curve — and the two are linked by the analytification.

Two corollaries are immediate. First, since chip-firing equivalence is a finite computation on a finite graph, divisor classes on $\Gamma$ are computable — making Baker–Norine an algorithmic Riemann–Roch. Second, by analytification, the picture lifts back to algebraic curves: a smooth projective curve $C$ over a non-archimedean field has a skeleton $\mathrm{Sk}(C^{\mathrm{an}})$ that is a metric graph of the same genus, and ranks of divisors are bounded above by their tropical counterparts (the specialization inequality).

6. Mikhalkin's correspondence and mirror symmetry

The signature accomplishment of tropical geometry is Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem (2005): the number of complex algebraic curves of degree $d$ and geometric genus $g$ in $\mathbb{P}^2$ passing through $3d - 1 + g$ generic points equals the number of tropical curves of the same combinatorial type passing through the tropicalized points, weighted by an explicit lattice multiplicity. Concretely, for genus zero,

$N_d \;=\; \sum_{\substack{T \text{ trop. curve through} \\ 3d-1 \text{ generic points}}} \mu(T),$

where $\mu(T) = \prod_{v} \mathrm{mult}(v)$ is the product over trivalent vertices of $\det(u_1, u_2)$ (any two of the three primitive outgoing directions). For $d=1$ there is one tropical line through any 2 points: $N_1 = 1$. For $d=2$ there are tropical conics through any $5$ generic points, total weight $1$: $N_2 = 1$. For $d=3,4$: $N_3 = 12, N_4 = 620$. The combinatorial computation tracks the holomorphic count exactly.

Beyond the plane, tropical methods compute Gromov–Witten invariants of toric surfaces, Hurwitz numbers, and double-Hurwitz numbers; the Cavalieri–Johnson–Markwig formulas express $\psi$-class intersections on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$ as tropical sums.

Mirror symmetry: the SYZ picture

Strominger–Yau–Zaslow (1996) proposed that mirror symmetry between Calabi–Yau pairs $(X, X^\vee)$ is fiberwise T-duality: $X \to B$ and $X^\vee \to B$ are special-Lagrangian torus fibrations over the same base $B$, and the mirror operation swaps each torus $T^n \subset X$ with its dual $\hat T^n \subset X^\vee$. The base $B$ is an integral affine manifold — the data of such a base is exactly the tropical building block. Singularities of the affine structure correspond to singular fibers (focus-focus points), and the Gross–Siebert program reconstructs the full Calabi–Yau pair from the pair (tropical base, singular locus) by a degeneration / scattering algorithm.

Outside enumerative geometry

Tropical mathematics applies broadly:

  • Phylogenetics. The Billera–Holmes–Vogtmann space of phylogenetic trees on $n$ taxa is a fan in tropical projective space — the metric on tree space is the tropical metric. Speyer–Sturmfels identify it with the tropical Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,n)^{\mathrm{trop}}$.
  • Combinatorial optimization. Shortest-path algorithms, scheduling, and certain economic equilibria are computed in $(\min,+)$ algebra; Hopfield-Tank min-plus networks turn convex optimization into matrix products.
  • Auctions and matching markets. Tropical methods underpin equilibrium analysis of multi-unit auctions (Tomas Klimpel, Elizabeth Baldwin, Paul Klemperer).

Connections

Tropical geometry is a young field with active frontiers: tropical foundations (matroids, semiring schemes), tropical Hodge theory (Itenberg–Katzarkov–Mikhalkin–Zharkov), and tropical compactifications of algebraic moduli. Many basic questions remain open, such as a complete characterisation of liftable tropical curves in higher genus, and a tropical analog of the Hodge conjecture.

  • Higher-genus algebraic curves — Riemann–Roch on a curve — the classical theorem whose tropical mirror is Baker–Norine on metric graphs.
  • Bézout's theorem — the projective intersection count whose tropicalisation is the mixed-volume formula on Newton polytopes.
  • Toric varieties — every fan in $\mathbb{R}^n$ defines a toric variety; tropicalisations are fans, so toric geometry and tropical geometry share their combinatorial spine.
  • Mirror symmetry — Gross–Siebert reconstruction and the SYZ fibration are inherently tropical / integral-affine objects.
  • Moduli spaces — $M^{\mathrm{trop}}_{g,n}$ is the Berkovich skeleton of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$; the tropical side is the ridiculously cheap combinatorial probe of the algebraic moduli.
