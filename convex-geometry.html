Convex geometry

Convex bodies in $\mathbb{R}^n$ sit at the crossroads of analysis, combinatorics, and probability. The same body acquires four different personalities — its support function, its polar dual, its face lattice, and its John ellipsoid — and each one captures a different inequality. Brunn–Minkowski is the master inequality; Dvoretzky's theorem is the punchline; everything else is a step between.

1. Convex bodies & supporting hyperplanes

A convex body is a compact convex subset $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ with non-empty interior. Convexity says that for any $x,y\in K$ and $t\in[0,1]$ the point $(1-t)x+ty$ also lies in $K$. Compactness plus interior buys you both extremes — the boundary $\partial K$ is a closed hypersurface, and the volume $\mathrm{vol}(K)$ is a positive finite number.

Convex bodies are completely determined by their support function:

$h_K(u)=\sup_{x\in K}\,\langle u,x\rangle,\qquad u\in\mathbb{R}^n.$

The function $h_K$ is positively 1-homogeneous and subadditive (hence convex), and the body recovers as

$K=\bigcap_{u\in S^{n-1}}\{x:\langle u,x\rangle\le h_K(u)\}.$

For every unit vector $u$, the hyperplane $H_u=\{x:\langle u,x\rangle=h_K(u)\}$ is a supporting hyperplane: it touches $K$ but leaves all of $K$ on one side. Hahn–Banach in finite dimension (= a one-line separation argument) guarantees that every boundary point of $K$ has a supporting hyperplane through it. At smooth boundary points the hyperplane is unique and is the tangent space; at corners (e.g. vertices of a polytope) there is a whole fan of supporting hyperplanes.

2. Minkowski sum & mixed volumes

The Minkowski sum of two convex sets is

$A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,\ b\in B\}.$

It is again convex; for scalars $\lambda\ge0$ one writes $\lambda A=\{\lambda a:a\in A\}$. The two operations endow the set of convex bodies with the structure of a positive cone, and the support function turns Minkowski sum into pointwise addition: $h_{A+B}=h_A+h_B$.

The deep observation (Minkowski 1903) is that volume is a polynomial under Minkowski combination. For convex bodies $K_1,\ldots,K_m\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\lambda_i\ge0$,

$\mathrm{vol}(\lambda_1 K_1+\cdots+\lambda_m K_m)=\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n=1}^m V(K_{i_1},\ldots,K_{i_n})\,\lambda_{i_1}\cdots\lambda_{i_n},$

where $V$ is symmetric in its arguments, multilinear with respect to Minkowski sum, and normalised so that $V(K,\ldots,K)=\mathrm{vol}(K)$. The coefficient $V(K_1,\ldots,K_n)$ is the mixed volume — the central numerical invariant of convex geometry.

Mixed volumes specialise to many familiar quantities. With $B$ the unit ball, the Steiner formula

$\mathrm{vol}(K+\varepsilon B)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom{n}{k}\varepsilon^k\,W_k(K)$

has coefficients $W_k(K)=V(K,\ldots,K,\underbrace{B,\ldots,B}_{k})$ — the quermassintegrals. The first non-trivial coefficient $W_1(K)=\mathrm{surf}(K)/n$ recovers the surface area; the top coefficient $W_n=\mathrm{vol}(B)$.

3. Brunn–Minkowski inequality

The single inequality that organises the whole subject is

$\boxed{\;\mathrm{vol}(A+B)^{1/n}\ge\mathrm{vol}(A)^{1/n}+\mathrm{vol}(B)^{1/n}\;}$

for convex bodies (or, in the Prékopa–Leindler form, for arbitrary measurable sets). Equality holds iff $A$ and $B$ are homothetic: $B=\lambda A+v$ for some $\lambda>0$ and $v\in\mathbb{R}^n$.

The functional form (Prékopa–Leindler, 1971/1972) is even stronger:

$\Bigl(\int h\Bigr)\ge\Bigl(\int f\Bigr)^{1-t}\Bigl(\int g\Bigr)^t,$

whenever $h\bigl((1-t)x+ty\bigr)\ge f(x)^{1-t}g(y)^t$ for all $x,y$ and $t\in[0,1]$. Applied to indicator functions $f=\mathbf{1}_A$, $g=\mathbf{1}_B$, $h=\mathbf{1}_{(1-t)A+tB}$ one recovers Brunn–Minkowski via $\log$-concavity of $t\mapsto\mathrm{vol}((1-t)A+tB)^{1/n}$.

Consequence: isoperimetric inequality. Taking $B=\varepsilon B^n$ and differentiating Brunn–Minkowski at $\varepsilon=0$ yields $\mathrm{surf}(K)\ge n\,\omega_n^{1/n}\,\mathrm{vol}(K)^{(n-1)/n}$, with equality iff $K$ is a Euclidean ball. The same one-line argument also produces the Aleksandrov–Fenchel inequality, the master inequality on mixed volumes.

4. Polytopes & the f-vector

A polytope is a convex body with finitely many extreme points. Equivalently — and this equivalence is the Minkowski–Weyl theorem — it is a bounded intersection of finitely many closed half-spaces. The two descriptions are dual:

  • V-representation: $P=\mathrm{conv}(v_1,\ldots,v_N)$ — the convex hull of vertices.
  • H-representation: $P=\{x:a_i^\top x\le b_i,\ i=1,\ldots,m\}$ — the intersection of half-spaces.

Converting between them is, in general, hard (the convex hull problem has output-sensitive complexity $O(n\log n+f)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ but blows up combinatorially in higher dimension).

The combinatorial skeleton of a polytope $P\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ is its face lattice. Each face is itself a polytope obtained by setting some linear functionals equal to their support values; faces are ordered by inclusion and graded by dimension. The number of $i$-dimensional faces is $f_i(P)$; the tuple $f(P)=(f_0,f_1,\ldots,f_{n-1})$ is the $f$-vector.

Euler's relation generalises the formula $V-E+F=2$ for $3$-polytopes:

$\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(-1)^i f_i(P)=1-(-1)^n.$

The upper-bound theorem (McMullen 1970) says that, among simplicial $n$-polytopes with $f_0=N$ vertices, the cyclic polytope $C(N,n)$ maximises every $f_i$. A polytope is simple if every vertex meets exactly $n$ facets; simplicial if every facet is a simplex. Polar duality (next section) exchanges simple and simplicial.

5. John's ellipsoid & Banach–Mazur distance

Among all ellipsoids contained in a centred convex body $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$, there is a unique one of maximal volume — the John ellipsoid $E=E(K)$. Dually, the unique minimal-volume ellipsoid containing $K$ is the Löwner ellipsoid, related by polarity to $E(K^\circ)$.

John's theorem (1948). For centrally symmetric $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$,

$E\subseteq K\subseteq\sqrt n\,E,$

and the factor $\sqrt n$ is best possible — saturated by the cube and the cross-polytope. For general (not necessarily symmetric) $K$, the factor is $n$ instead of $\sqrt n$ (saturated by the simplex).

The John condition for optimality is striking: when $E$ is the unit ball $B$, optimality holds iff there exist contact points $u_1,\ldots,u_m\in K\cap\partial B$ and weights $c_i>0$ with

$\sum_i c_i\,u_i u_i^\top=\mathrm{Id},\qquad\sum_i c_i\,u_i=0.$

That is, the contact points form a tight frame. This is exactly the condition for a finite "Parseval-style" expansion of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and gives John's theorem a representation-theoretic flavour. Note: the second equation $\sum_i c_i u_i=0$ is the optimality condition for the general (possibly non-symmetric) version. For centrally symmetric $K$ the contact set is itself $\pm$-symmetric, so $\sum_i c_i u_i=0$ holds automatically and only the first (frame) equation is a constraint.

John's inclusion translates into a bound on the Banach–Mazur distance

$d(K,L)=\inf\{r\ge1:K\subseteq TL\subseteq rK\text{ for some invertible affine }T\},$

which is a complete metric (after $\log$) on the set of $n$-dim normed spaces modulo isometry. John gives $d(K,\ell^2_n)\le\sqrt n$ for symmetric bodies, so the Banach–Mazur compactum has radius (around $\ell^2_n$) at most $\sqrt n$; the multiplicative diameter is then $\le n$ via the triangle inequality $d(K,L)\le d(K,\ell^2_n)\,d(\ell^2_n,L)$ (and $\le n^2$ in the general non-symmetric case). Determining the exact diameter is a long-standing open problem.

6. Polar duality & the Mahler conjecture

Define the polar body of $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ (closed convex, $0\in\mathrm{int}\,K$) as

$K^\circ=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^n:\langle y,x\rangle\le 1\text{ for all }x\in K\}.$

Polarity is order-reversing ($K\subseteq L\Rightarrow K^\circ\supseteq L^\circ$) and an involution: $(K^\circ)^\circ=K$ (this is the bipolar theorem). In the language of support functions, polarity exchanges $h_K$ and the gauge / Minkowski functional $\|x\|_K=\inf\{t>0:x\in tK\}$; in the language of polytopes, polarity exchanges the $V$- and $H$-representations (vertices of $K$ ↔ facets of $K^\circ$).

The most striking open question on the product $\mathrm{vol}(K)\cdot\mathrm{vol}(K^\circ)$ is the Mahler conjecture: for centrally symmetric convex bodies $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$,

$\mathrm{vol}(K)\,\mathrm{vol}(K^\circ)\ge\frac{4^n}{n!}.$

Equality should be achieved by the cube–cross-polytope (and the Hanner polytopes more generally). The conjecture is known in dimensions 2 (Mahler 1939) and 3 (Iriyeh–Shibata 2020); the symmetric case in arbitrary dimension is still open. The non-symmetric version (with bound $(n+1)^{n+1}/(n!)^2$) is also open beyond $n=2$.

Dual to Mahler is the hyperplane (slicing) conjecture of Bourgain: there exists a universal $c>0$ such that every centrally symmetric convex body $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ with $\mathrm{vol}(K)=1$ has a hyperplane section of volume $\ge c$. Klartag–Lehec (2022) proved the isotropic-constant bound $L_K=O(\sqrt{\log n})$, giving $c=\Omega(1/\sqrt{\log n})$, and Klartag (2023) further improved this to $L_K=O((\log n)^{1/4})$ — so the slicing conjecture is now resolved up to polylog factors, well past Bourgain's original $\Omega(n^{-1/4})$.

The volume product is scale-invariant by design: scaling $K\mapsto sK$ scales $K^\circ\mapsto K^\circ/s$, leaving $\mathrm{vol}(K)\cdot\mathrm{vol}(K^\circ)$ fixed. The square saturates Mahler in 2D; sliding the scale doesn't move the product off $8$. Switch shapes and the product rises — the worst case is the cube–cross-polytope pair.

7. Spherical sections & Dvoretzky's theorem

The crown of finite-dimensional convex geometry, where the subject reconnects with high-dimensional probability:

Dvoretzky's theorem (1961). Let $K\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ be a centred convex body with associated norm $\|\cdot\|_K$. For every $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ there is a subspace $E\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ of dimension $k\ge c\,\varepsilon^2\log n$ and a number $r>0$ such that $$r\,|x|\le\|x\|_K\le(1+\varepsilon)\,r\,|x|\qquad\forall x\in E.$$ That is, every high-dimensional convex body hides an almost-Euclidean section of dimension $\Omega(\log n)$.

The exponent on $\log n$ is sharp: cubes and cross-polytopes only have logarithmic-dimensional Euclidean sections. Milman's proof (1971) uses Lévy concentration on $S^{n-1}$ — the unit sphere concentrates around any $1$-Lipschitz function, and the norm $\|\cdot\|_K$ restricted to a random $k$-subspace fluctuates by less than $\varepsilon$ when $k\le c\,\varepsilon^2\log n$. The Lipschitz constant is controlled by the John ellipsoid (§5).

The theorem is the geometric heart of high-dimensional probability: it says that every normed space, no matter how exotic, eventually behaves like Hilbert space on a small subspace, and the dimension grows only logarithmically in the ambient size. For applications in machine learning (Johnson–Lindenstrauss, random projections), this is the structural reason random sketches preserve geometry.

8. Connections

Convex geometry is a hub: it inherits its analytic muscle from functional analysis and high-dimensional probability, it borrows from the combinatorics of polytopes for its discrete face, and it loops back into algebraic geometry (toric varieties, Newton polytopes), optimisation (LP polyhedra, semidefinite programming), and the asymptotic theory of normed spaces. Many central problems — Mahler, the slicing conjecture, the variance / KLS conjecture — remain open in arbitrary dimension; the strongest current advances mix concentration of measure with stochastic localisation, which is where the next decade of progress will likely come from.

  • High-dimensional geometry — Dvoretzky's theorem — the convex-geometry view of concentration of measure; this page's §7 is its companion.
  • Combinatorial optimization — Polyhedral combinatorics — LP feasibility regions and integer hulls are the bridge to the optimisation side of polytopes.
  • Toric varieties — Cone–variety dictionary — lattice polytopes and Newton polytopes recast convex bodies as algebro-geometric data.
  • Differential geometry — First and second fundamental forms — the Gauss curvature of $\partial K$ for a smooth convex body $K$ feeds into Aleksandrov–Fenchel and the volume polynomial.
  • Geometric measure theory — Sets of finite perimeter — Brunn–Minkowski and the isoperimetric inequality generalise far beyond convex bodies, with the relevant notion of "boundary" supplied by GMT.
