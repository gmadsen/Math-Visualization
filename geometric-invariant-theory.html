Geometric invariant theory

Hilbert's finiteness theorem turned invariant rings into geometry: the GIT quotient $X/\!/G$ takes a reductive group action on a projective variety and produces a moduli space. The Hilbert–Mumford criterion reduces stability to a one-parameter test, Kempf–Ness identifies the algebraic quotient with a symplectic reduction $\mu^{-1}(0)/K$, and the framework underwrites the construction of $M_g$, quiver moduli, and $\mathrm{Bun}_G$.

1. Invariants of group actions

Classical invariant theory begins with a linear action: a group $G$ acts on a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, hence on the polynomial ring $R=\operatorname{Sym}(V^*)=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ by $(g\cdot f)(v)=f(g^{-1}v)$. An invariant is a polynomial fixed by every group element:

$R^G\;=\;\{f\in R\;:\;g\cdot f=f\text{ for every }g\in G\}.$

The invariant subring $R^G$ is the natural candidate for the coordinate ring of the orbit space $V/G$ — its functions descend to orbits by construction. Three classical examples set the tone:

group $G$actioninvariant ringgenerators
$\mathfrak{S}_n$permute $x_1,\ldots,x_n$symmetric polynomials$e_1,\ldots,e_n$ (elementary)
$\mathbb{Z}/n=\langle\zeta\rangle$$x\mapsto\zeta x$$k[x^n]$$x^n$
$\mathrm{SL}_2$binary $d$-forms$k[\text{discriminant},\ldots]$finitely many (Hilbert)

The third row is the deep one. A binary $d$-form is a degree-$d$ homogeneous polynomial $f(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^d a_i\binom{d}{i}x^iy^{d-i}$, on which $\mathrm{SL}_2$ acts via change of variables. For $d=2$ the only invariant up to scalar is the discriminant $a_0a_2-a_1^2$; for $d=3$ the cubic discriminant $a_0^2a_3^2-\cdots$; for $d=4$ Cayley found two algebraically independent invariants $I_2$ and $I_3$, and Sylvester's catalogue ran for hundreds of pages enumerating them in higher degrees. Hilbert's 1890 theorem — proved with the Hilbert basis theorem he invented for the purpose — said that no matter how many invariants there were, they were always finitely many.

Hilbert's finiteness theorem

If $G$ is a reductive algebraic group acting linearly on a finitely generated $k$-algebra $R$, then $R^G$ is itself a finitely generated $k$-algebra. The proof in modern language: the inclusion $R^G\hookrightarrow R$ splits as $G$-modules (Reynolds operator, next section), so the ideal $R\cdot R^G_+\subset R$ contracts to the maximal ideal $R^G_+\subset R^G$; pick finitely many generators of the former by Hilbert basis, project them with the Reynolds operator, and you have finitely many generators of $R^G_+$ as an ideal — which by graded Nakayama is finitely many algebra generators of $R^G$.

The hypothesis "reductive" is sharp. Nagata (1959) built a 14-dimensional unipotent subgroup $G\subset\mathrm{GL}_{32}$ whose action on $k[x_1,\ldots,x_{32}]$ has $R^G$ not finitely generated — disproving Hilbert's 14th problem in full generality. Unipotent groups have no Reynolds operator, and the proof breaks at the splitting step. So GIT lives where the splitting exists: in the reductive world.

Why finiteness is hard. The Reynolds operator $R\colon R\to R^G$ averages a polynomial against $G$ to produce its invariant projection. For a finite group, $R(f)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g} g\cdot f$ — visibly bounded. For a positive-dimensional reductive group, the average is over Haar measure on the maximal compact, and finiteness of the output is exactly Hilbert's theorem.

2. Reductive groups and the Reynolds operator

The dividing line between "GIT works" and "GIT fails" is reductivity. A linear algebraic group $G$ over $\mathbb{C}$ is reductive if its unipotent radical (the maximal connected normal unipotent subgroup) is trivial. Equivalently — and this is the working definition — every finite-dimensional rational representation of $G$ is semisimple: a direct sum of irreducibles.

The Reynolds operator

Reductivity gives a canonical $G$-equivariant projection $R\colon V\twoheadrightarrow V^G$ for every rational $G$-module $V$: split $V=V^G\oplus W$ as $G$-modules (possible because every rep is semisimple) and project onto $V^G$. The map $R$ is linear, $G$-equivariant ($R(g\cdot v)=R(v)$), idempotent ($R^2=R$), and the identity on invariants.

Two incarnations of the same map:

  • Finite group, average: $R(v)=\dfrac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G} g\cdot v.$
  • Compact (or general reductive complexified), Haar integral: $R(v)=\int_{K} k\cdot v\,dk$ where $K\subset G_\mathbb{C}$ is a maximal compact and $dk$ is normalised Haar measure.

For an algebraic-geometric version that works over any field of characteristic zero, one uses the Weyl unitary trick: the algebraic averaging is the unique $G$-equivariant projection, characterised entirely by these properties — no integral required.

Why the splitting matters. Hilbert's finiteness proof projects a finite set of generators of an ideal in $R$ onto invariants via the Reynolds operator $R$, then shows the projected set generates $R^G_+$ as an ideal of $R^G$. Without $R$, the projection step is impossible — unipotent groups fail exactly because their orbits can't be averaged.

3. GIT quotients and stability

For an affine variety $X=\operatorname{Spec} R$ with reductive $G$-action, Hilbert's theorem gives the affine quotient

$X/\!/G\;=\;\operatorname{Spec} R^G.$

This works categorically — $\operatorname{Spec} R^G$ is the categorical quotient in the category of affine schemes — but it identifies any two orbits whose closures meet. Two points $x,y\in X$ map to the same point of $X/\!/G$ iff $\overline{G\cdot x}\cap\overline{G\cdot y}\ne\emptyset$, which is strictly weaker than "lie in the same orbit". A typical pathology: $\mathbb{G}_m$ on $\mathbb{A}^2$ with weights $(1,-1)$ has the hyperbola $xy=c$ as one orbit for each $c\ne 0$, but the union of the two coordinate axes degenerates: the origin sits in the closure of every nonzero orbit, so all axis points collapse to one quotient point. The affine GIT quotient is $\operatorname{Spec} k[xy]=\mathbb{A}^1$.

For projective $X$, GIT does something more interesting: it uses a linearization to filter which orbits enter the quotient.

Mumford's projective construction

Fix a $G$-equivariant ample line bundle $L$ on $X$ (a linearization). The graded ring of invariant sections is

$R(X,L)^G\;=\;\bigoplus_{n\ge 0} H^0(X,L^{\otimes n})^G,$

a finitely generated graded $k$-algebra by Hilbert's theorem applied to each piece (and a graded analogue of the Reynolds projection). The GIT quotient is

$X/\!/_L G\;=\;\operatorname{Proj}\bigl(R(X,L)^G\bigr).$

Concretely: invariant sections separate the orbits they don't vanish on. A point $x$ is semistable ($x\in X^{ss}(L)$) if some invariant section $s\in H^0(X,L^{\otimes n})^G$ has $s(x)\ne 0$. The semistable locus $X^{ss}$ is an open subvariety, and the GIT quotient is the image of $X^{ss}$ in $\operatorname{Proj}$.

The set $X^s\subset X^{ss}$ of stable points is open. On $X^s$ the GIT quotient is a geometric quotient — $X^s\to X^s/G$ is a principal $G$-bundle (étale-locally) and the fibres are exactly $G$-orbits. Off the stable locus, several orbits can collapse to a single quotient point.

Four ordered points $p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4\in\mathbb{P}^1$ under the diagonal $\mathrm{SL}_2$ action: the GIT quotient is $\mathbb{P}^1$ parametrised by the cross-ratio. Stable = no point coincides with $\ge 2$ others (= no point of multiplicity $\ge 2$). Strictly semistable = exactly two coincide (cross-ratio $0$, $1$, or $\infty$). The threshold "multiplicity $>n/2$" generalises: for $n$ points on $\mathbb{P}^1$, semistable iff no point repeats more than $n/2$ times, stable iff no point repeats $\ge n/2$ times.

Configuration ≠ moduli unless stable. The classical moduli of ordered 4-tuples on $\mathbb{P}^1$ modulo $\mathrm{SL}_2$ is the cross-ratio $\lambda\in\mathbb{P}^1\setminus\{0,1,\infty\}$. GIT compactifies by adding back the three special values — points where exactly two of the four coincide — and discards "three or more coincide" as unstable. The GIT quotient is exactly $\mathbb{P}^1$, with $\{0,1,\infty\}$ representing the strictly semistable boundary.

4. The Hilbert–Mumford numerical criterion

Testing stability from the definition — "does some invariant section avoid $x$?" — is impractical: invariant rings are huge and rarely have an explicit generating set. Mumford's 1965 numerical criterion reduces the test to a one-parameter check.

A 1-parameter subgroup (1-PS) is a homomorphism $\lambda\colon\mathbb{G}_m\to G$. For a reductive group, every 1-PS is conjugate to one landing in a maximal torus, so the test parameter is essentially a tuple of integers (the weights of the 1-PS on a maximal torus). For each 1-PS, the action of $\lambda(t)$ on the ambient representation $V=H^0(X,L)^\vee$ decomposes into weight spaces:

$V\;=\;\bigoplus_w V_w,\qquad \lambda(t)\cdot v_w\;=\;t^w v_w.$

Write $x=\sum_w x_w$ with $x_w\in V_w$, and define the Hilbert–Mumford weight

$\mu(x,\lambda)\;=\;\min\{w\,:\,x_w\ne 0\}.$

This is the smallest weight at which $x$ has a nonzero component, equivalently the order of vanishing of $\lambda(t)\cdot x$ at $t=0$ measured by $L$.

The criterion

A point $x\in X$ is GIT-semistable for $L$ iff $\mu(x,\lambda)\le 0$ for every nontrivial 1-PS $\lambda$, and stable iff $\mu(x,\lambda)<0$ for every nontrivial $\lambda$.

The intuition is clean. If $\mu(x,\lambda)>0$ for some $\lambda$, then $\lim_{t\to 0}\lambda(t)\cdot x$ exists in $V$ and lies in the lower weight subspace — that limit is $G$-fixed (or fixed by a smaller torus) and any invariant section vanishes there, hence also at $x$ by continuity along the orbit. Conversely, if every 1-PS gives $\mu\le 0$, the orbit closure stays away from the "vanishing direction" of every invariant section, so some section survives.

Worked example: binary $n$-forms

Take $V=k[x,y]_n$, the binary forms of degree $n$, with $\mathrm{SL}_2$ acting by change of variables. The natural 1-PS $\lambda(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t,t^{-1})$ acts on the monomial $x^iy^{n-i}$ by $\lambda(t)\cdot x^iy^{n-i}=t^i\cdot t^{-(n-i)}\cdot x^iy^{n-i}=t^{2i-n}\cdot x^iy^{n-i}$. So:

$\text{weight of }a_i x^iy^{n-i}\;=\;2i-n.$

Write $f(x,y)=\sum a_i x^iy^{n-i}$ with $a_i\ne 0$ for some set $S\subset\{0,1,\ldots,n\}$; then $\mu(f,\lambda)=\min_{i\in S}(2i-n)$. Iterating over all 1-PSs conjugate to this one (i.e.\ all choices of basis pinning the root) gives: $f$ is semistable iff no root of $f$ has multiplicity $> n/2$; stable iff no root has multiplicity $\ge n/2$. The walls are exactly the configurations with a root of multiplicity exactly $n/2$.

The criterion as a slope. $\mu(x,\lambda)$ is the negative of the leading-order vanishing of $\lambda(t)\cdot x$ at $t=0$. The 1-PS $\lambda$ "destabilises" $x$ iff its action drives $x$ to a $G$-fixed limit while invariant sections vanish there. The criterion is a tropical / valuation-theoretic statement: stability is dictated by Newton polytopes (the set of weights at which $x$ has support).

5. Kempf–Ness and symplectic reduction

Atiyah, Bott, Guillemin and Sternberg observed in the late 1970s that GIT has a symplectic twin. The starting data: a compact connected Lie group $K$ acts on a Kähler manifold $X$ preserving the symplectic form $\omega$; let $G_\mathbb{C}=K_\mathbb{C}$ be the complexification.

A moment map $\mu\colon X\to\mathfrak{k}^*$ is a $K$-equivariant map such that for every $\xi\in\mathfrak{k}$, the function $\langle\mu,\xi\rangle\colon X\to\mathbb{R}$ is a Hamiltonian for the vector field $\xi_X$ induced by $\xi$. When it exists, the moment map encodes the $K$-action as a Hamiltonian flow on $X$. The Marsden–Weinstein reduction at level $0$ is

$X/\!/K\;:=\;\mu^{-1}(0)/K.$

Generically this is a smooth Kähler manifold of dimension $\dim X - 2\dim K$ (twice the loss because each Hamiltonian eats one symplectic direction and the quotient eats another).

The Kempf–Ness theorem (1979)

Let $X\subset\mathbb{P}^N$ be a smooth projective variety with a linear action of a reductive group $G_\mathbb{C}$ (so $K\subset G_\mathbb{C}$ acts on $X$ preserving the Fubini–Study Kähler form). Then:

  1. A point $x\in X$ is polystable iff $\overline{G_\mathbb{C}\cdot x}$ contains a zero of $\mu$.
  2. If $x$ is polystable, the set $\mu^{-1}(0)\cap(G_\mathbb{C}\cdot x)$ is a single $K$-orbit (so the moment-map zero is unique up to compact symmetry).
  3. The GIT quotient coincides with the symplectic reduction:

$X/\!/_{L}G_\mathbb{C}\;\cong\;\mu^{-1}(0)/K.$

The proof flows through the Kempf–Ness function $\Phi_x(g)=\log\|g\cdot \tilde x\|^2$ on $G_\mathbb{C}/K$, where $\tilde x\in V\setminus 0$ is any lift. $\Phi_x$ is strictly convex along geodesics, and its critical points project to the zeros of $\mu$ on the $G_\mathbb{C}$-orbit. Polystability is exactly "$\Phi_x$ achieves its minimum"; instability is "$\Phi_x$ has no minimum on the orbit", which happens precisely when the orbit closure contains $0$.

$\mathbb{C}^\times$ acts on $\mathbb{C}^2$ by scalar multiplication (the complexification of the diagonal $\mathrm{U}(1)$). The moment map is $\mu(v)=\tfrac{1}{2}(|v|^2-1)$ for the level-shifted choice; the zero locus is the unit sphere $|v|=1$. Polystable orbits ($v\ne 0$) all meet $\mu^{-1}(0)$ in a unique $\mathrm{U}(1)$-orbit (a circle), and the GIT quotient $\mathbb{C}^2/\!/_1\mathbb{C}^\times = \mu^{-1}(0)/\mathrm{U}(1) = \mathbb{P}^1$. The unstable orbit ($v=0$) is the origin alone; the moment-map condition throws it away.

Convexity in disguise. The Kempf–Ness function $\Phi_x(g)=\log\|g\cdot\tilde x\|^2$ on $G_\mathbb{C}/K$ is geodesically convex (in the natural symmetric-space metric on $G_\mathbb{C}/K$). Its gradient is $-2\mu(g\cdot x)$. So minimising $\Phi_x$ along the orbit = solving $\mu=0$ = finding the polystable representative. Convexity is what makes Kempf–Ness so usable algorithmically (gradient descent really does converge).

6. Variation of GIT and wall-crossing

The GIT quotient depends on a choice of linearization $L$. Two different linearizations $L_+,L_-$ on the same action can give two different quotients $X/\!/_{L_\pm}G$, and the relationship between them is one of the cleanest natural sources of birational geometry.

The $G$-ample cone

For a $G$-action on a projective $X$ with $G$ reductive, the $G$-equivariant Néron–Severi group $\mathrm{NS}^G(X)_\mathbb{Q}$ has a cone of $G$-ample classes — fractional linearizations under which $X^{ss}$ is non-empty. Inside this cone, the set $\{L\,:\,X^{ss}(L)=X^{ss}(L_0)\}$ is open: as you wiggle $L$, the semistable locus is constant. Outside this open set, you've crossed a wall: some orbit that was stable becomes strictly semistable, or vice versa.

Thaddeus (1996) and Dolgachev–Hu (1998) gave the structure theorem: the $G$-ample cone is partitioned into finitely many open chambers, separated by codimension-one walls. Inside each chamber the quotient $X/\!/_L G$ is constant. Across a wall, $X/\!/_{L_+} G$ and $X/\!/_{L_-} G$ are connected by a small birational modification — usually a flip in the sense of the minimal model program.

Wall-crossing as MMP

The picture across a wall: there is a "master" quotient $X/\!/_{L_0} G$ at the wall itself (using the boundary linearization $L_0$). Both $X/\!/_{L_\pm}G$ admit birational morphisms to $X/\!/_{L_0}G$ contracting different loci. When both maps are small (i.e.\ contract loci of codimension $\ge 2$), the diagram is

$X/\!/_{L_-}G\;\dashrightarrow\;X/\!/_{L_+}G$

a flip across $X/\!/_{L_0}G$. The two sides are birational, share a common open subset, but differ in the codimension-2 strata being contracted.

This isn't just a curiosity: it's a tool. VGIT is the cleanest source of explicit flips, has been used by Hu–Keel to attack the Mori dream space program, and powers Bridgeland stability where the "$L$" becomes a continuous parameter and the wall-and-chamber structure organises moduli problems on derived categories.

Thaddeus' moduli of stable pairs and the cone of Mori dream spaces both use VGIT to manufacture flips on demand.

7. Moduli applications: $M_g$, quivers, $\mathrm{Bun}_G$

GIT's reason to exist is moduli construction. Three load-bearing examples:

Mumford's $M_g$ (1965)

For a smooth projective curve $C$ of genus $g\ge 2$, the tricanonical embedding $|3K_C|\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{5g-6}$ realises $C$ as a curve of degree $6g-6$ in projective space. The Hilbert scheme $H$ of subschemes of $\mathbb{P}^{5g-6}$ with the right Hilbert polynomial parametrises all such embeddings, and $\mathrm{PGL}_{5g-5}$ acts on $H$ by change of coordinates. Mumford's result: a Hilbert point is GIT-stable iff the corresponding curve is Deligne–Mumford stable (smooth + at worst nodal + finite automorphism group), and

$\overline{M}_g\;=\;H^{ss}/\!/\mathrm{PGL}.$

This was the first construction of $\overline{M}_g$ as a projective scheme. The same machine gives $\overline{M}_{g,n}$ (pointed curves) and $A_g$ (principally polarized abelian varieties).

King's quiver moduli (1994)

Fix a finite quiver $Q$ (a directed graph) and a dimension vector $\mathbf{d}=(d_i)_{i\in Q_0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^{Q_0}$. A representation of $Q$ assigns a $d_i$-dimensional vector space to each vertex and a linear map to each arrow:

$\mathrm{Rep}(Q,\mathbf{d})\;=\;\bigoplus_{a\colon i\to j}\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{C}^{d_i},\mathbb{C}^{d_j}),$

with $\mathrm{GL}(\mathbf{d})=\prod_i\mathrm{GL}(d_i)$ acting by simultaneous basis change. A stability character $\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_0}$ with $\sum\theta_i d_i=0$ defines a character of $\mathrm{GL}(\mathbf{d})$ via $g\mapsto\prod_i\det(g_i)^{\theta_i}$, linearising the trivial line bundle. King's theorem: a representation is GIT-(semi)stable under this linearization iff it is $\theta$-(semi)stable in the abstract sense (every nonzero subrepresentation $W$ has $\theta\cdot\dim W \le 0$). The GIT quotient is the moduli space of $\theta$-stable representations.

Bundles on a curve: $\mathrm{Bun}_G$

For a smooth projective curve $C$ and a reductive group $G$, the moduli of principal $G$-bundles on $C$ is the stack $\mathrm{Bun}_G$. Its semistable locus (Mumford / Narasimhan–Seshadri) is a finite-type scheme, constructed via GIT on a Quot scheme: parametrise pairs (bundle, generating sections), take the locus where the bundle is semistable, mod out by $\mathrm{GL}$. The result is a projective coarse moduli space $\mathcal{N}_G(C)$, and for $G=\mathrm{GL}_n$ it equals the Narasimhan–Seshadri space of polystable degree-$0$ bundles, which under Kempf–Ness identifies with the space of irreducible unitary representations $\pi_1(C)\to U(n)$.

The pattern

Across all three examples, GIT does exactly one thing: it cuts a "raw" parameter space (Hilbert points, representations, Quot points) by a reductive group action, and selects the semistable orbits whose closure-equivalences are the morphisms a moduli space should record. The fine moduli problem might be obstructed by automorphisms, but the GIT coarse quotient always exists and is projective — that's the payoff.

Kronecker quiver $K_2$: two arrows $a_1,a_2\colon 1\to 2$, dimension vector $\mathbf{d}=(1,1)$. A representation is $(c_1,c_2)\in\mathbb{C}^2$ (the two arrow values). The 1-dimensional subrepresentations are: $W=(0,\mathbb{C})$ at vertex 2 ($\dim W=(0,1)$, $\theta\cdot\dim W=\theta_2=-\theta_1$); when $c_1=c_2=0$, also $W=(\mathbb{C},0)$ at vertex 1 ($\dim W=(1,0)$, $\theta\cdot\dim W=\theta_1$). $\theta$-semistable iff every subrep $W$ has $\theta\cdot\dim W\le 0$. The wall is $\theta_1=0$ (trivial stability).

Stacks vs GIT. The moduli stack $\mathcal{M}_g=[\,\text{curves}\,]$ remembers automorphisms of each curve; the GIT quotient $\overline{M}_g$ is the coarse moduli space — it forgets them. Both objects are useful: stacks for functorial questions (deformations, virtual classes, derived category), coarse moduli for "Is this a projective scheme I can do intersection theory on?" GIT is the bridge that turns the stack into a scheme.

8. Connections

GIT is the algebraic-geometric incarnation of stability theory. Its tendrils:

  • Moduli spaces — the entire moduli-construction story is half about functors of points and half about GIT cutting raw parameter spaces. $\overline{M}_g$, quiver moduli, and $\mathrm{Bun}_G$ are GIT outputs.
  • Symplectic manifolds — Kempf–Ness identifies GIT with Marsden–Weinstein reduction. The two languages describe the same moduli space; one is algebraic (rings of invariants), one is symplectic (zero locus of the moment map).
  • MMP & birational geometry — VGIT manufactures explicit flips. Thaddeus / Dolgachev–Hu's wall-crossing structure is one of the cleanest naturally occurring sources of small modifications.
  • Higher-genus curves — Mumford's original GIT construction of $\overline{M}_g$ used tricanonical Hilbert points and reduced Deligne–Mumford stability to GIT stability.
  • Stacks — $[X/G]$ is the stack quotient; $X/\!/G$ is the coarse moduli space. GIT remembers less (loses automorphism information) but produces an actual scheme. Both are needed.
  • Group actions and orbits — quiver moduli (King) extends GIT to representation moduli of any finite quiver, recovering many classical moduli spaces (vector bundles, Higgs bundles) as quiver moduli with relations.

Frontier: Bridgeland stability conditions on derived categories $D^b(X)$ generalise GIT stability to a continuous parameter space $\mathrm{Stab}(X)$, with a wall-and-chamber structure refining VGIT and a conjectural action of the mapping class group / braid group. The Bayer–Macrì programme uses Bridgeland walls to run the MMP on moduli of sheaves on K3 surfaces, producing all known minimal models from a single derived-category computation. The interplay between GIT, Bridgeland, and quantum cohomology is one of the active modern research areas in algebraic geometry.
