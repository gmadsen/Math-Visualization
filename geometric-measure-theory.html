Geometric measure theory

Measure theory with geometric content: BV functions, sets of finite perimeter, rectifiable sets, currents, and the existence theorems for minimal surfaces and image segmentation that fall out of one compactness machine.

1. Functions of bounded variation

A function $u\in L^1(\Omega)$ on an open set $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ is of bounded variation when its distributional gradient is a finite vector-valued Radon measure: there exists $Du=(D_1u,\dots,D_nu)$, finite signed Borel measures, with

$$\int_\Omega u\,\partial_i\phi\,dx \;=\; -\int_\Omega \phi\,d(D_iu)\qquad\text{for every }\phi\in C^1_c(\Omega).$$

The total variation $|Du|(\Omega)$ — the variation measure of the vector measure $Du$ — equals the dual norm

$$|Du|(\Omega)\;=\;\sup\Bigl\{\,\int_\Omega u\,\mathrm{div}\,\phi\,dx \,:\, \phi\in C^1_c(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^n),\,|\phi(x)|\le 1\,\Bigr\}.$$

Why widen $W^{1,1}$ to $\mathrm{BV}$? Because $W^{1,1}$ requires the gradient measure to be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, ruling out jumps. The Heaviside on $\mathbb{R}$ has $u'=\delta_0$ — a finite Radon measure but not in $L^1$ — so $H\in\mathrm{BV}_{\mathrm{loc}}\setminus W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}$. Every BV function admits a Radon-Nikodym-style decomposition

$$Du \;=\; \nabla u\,\mathcal{L}^n \;+\; (u^+-u^-)\,\nu_u\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\restriction J_u \;+\; D^c u,$$

splitting the gradient measure into an absolutely continuous part (the classical $\nabla u\in L^1$), a jump part concentrated on the rectifiable jump set $J_u$ with two-sided traces $u^\pm$ and unit normal $\nu_u$, and a Cantor part $D^c u$ — singular with respect to Lebesgue measure but vanishing on every $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$-finite set. The Cantor staircase $u(x)=$ Cantor function has $\nabla u\equiv 0$, no jumps, yet $|Du|([0,1])=1$ — the canonical $\mathrm{BV}\setminus W^{1,1}$ counterexample whose entire variation lives in $D^c u$.

Three properties make $\mathrm{BV}$ the natural setting for variational problems with discontinuities. Lower semicontinuity: if $u_k\to u$ in $L^1$ then $|Du|(\Omega)\le\liminf_k|Du_k|(\Omega)$. Compactness: bounded sequences in $\mathrm{BV}(\Omega)$ have $L^1$-convergent subsequences (a Rellich-type embedding). Coarea: $|Du|(\Omega)=\int_\mathbb{R}|D\chi_{\{u>t\}}|(\Omega)\,dt$ — total variation as integrated level-set perimeter, which is the bridge to §2 and §4.

2. Sets of finite perimeter and reduced boundary

A measurable set $E\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ has finite perimeter when $\chi_E\in\mathrm{BV}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, and its perimeter is

$$P(E)\;=\;|D\chi_E|(\mathbb{R}^n)\;=\;\sup\Bigl\{\,\int_E\mathrm{div}\,\phi\,dx \,:\, \phi\in C^1_c(\mathbb{R}^n;\mathbb{R}^n),\,|\phi|\le 1\,\Bigr\}.$$

For $E$ with smooth boundary the divergence theorem gives $P(E)=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)$, recovering classical surface area. The point of GMT is that this dual definition makes sense — and is finite — for sets whose topological boundary is fractal, has corners, or is wildly non-smooth, while the surface area $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial E)$ is sharp only on the reduced part of the boundary.

The reduced boundary $\partial^*E$ is the set of points $x$ where the outward unit normal exists in the measure-theoretic sense:

$$\nu_E(x)\;=\;-\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{D\chi_E(B_r(x))}{|D\chi_E|(B_r(x))} \;\in\; S^{n-1},\qquad |D\chi_E|(B_r(x))>0\text{ for all }r>0.$$

De Giorgi's structure theorem is the central result: if $P(E)<\infty$ then $\partial^*E$ is countably $(n-1)$-rectifiable (a countable union of $C^1$-hypersurface pieces, modulo $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$-null), $\nu_E$ is a Borel measurable unit-vector field, $|D\chi_E|=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\restriction\partial^*E$, and

$$P(E)\;=\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^*E),\qquad D\chi_E\;=\;-\nu_E\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\restriction\partial^*E.$$

This is the divergence theorem extended to corner-y, fractal, and disconnected sets — with the caveat that the topological boundary may be larger than $\partial^*E$. The Koch snowflake is the textbook bad case: it has finite Lebesgue measure but infinite perimeter ($P(E)=\infty$, the limit of perimeters of the pre-fractal approximants), so finite-perimeter regularity forbids it. A square has $\partial^*E$ = the four open edges (the four corners drop out as $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$-null — they are the genuinely non-smooth points). The whole isoperimetric story — $P(E)\ge n\omega_n^{1/n}|E|^{(n-1)/n}$ with equality only on balls — lives natively in this class.

3. Rectifiable sets and Hausdorff measure

A subset $S\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ is countably $k$-rectifiable when $\mathcal{H}^k$-almost all of $S$ is contained in a countable union of Lipschitz images of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^k$:

$$S\;\subset\;S_0\,\cup\,\bigcup_{j=1}^\infty f_j(A_j),\qquad \mathcal{H}^k(S_0)=0,\;f_j:A_j\subset\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}^n\text{ Lipschitz}.$$

This is the geometric measure-theoretic upgrade of "smooth $k$-submanifold up to a measure-zero exceptional set". Three structural facts pin down the class:

Approximate tangent planes. At $\mathcal{H}^k$-almost every $x\in S$ there is a unique $k$-plane $T_xS\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ such that $S$ has density $1$ in $T_xS$ near $x$ (in the precise $r\to 0$ blow-up sense). So rectifiable sets carry a measurable tangent-plane field even when no parameterization is differentiable in the classical sense — a corner point of a Lipschitz curve has tangent direction defined $\mathcal{H}^1$-a.e., just not at the corner itself.

Besicovitch–Federer projection theorem. A purely $k$-unrectifiable set $S$ (no $\mathcal{H}^k$-positive piece is rectifiable) projects to a set of $\mathcal{L}^k$-measure zero on $\mathcal{H}^{k\cdot(n-k)}$-almost every $k$-plane. Rectifiable sets project to positive measure on most planes; purely unrectifiable sets are invisible to most projections. The 2D test: a smooth curve has positive projected length onto almost every line, while the 4-corner Cantor set in the plane projects to a Lebesgue-null set on every line except a Hausdorff-dimension-1 sparse family.

Densities classify. The $k$-dimensional density $\Theta^k(S,x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\mathcal{H}^k(S\cap B_r(x))/(\omega_k r^k)$ exists and equals $1$ at $\mathcal{H}^k$-a.e. point of a rectifiable $S$ (Besicovitch, Marstrand, Mattila). Conversely, $\mathcal{H}^k$-positive sets where the density exists and is positive a.e. are rectifiable. So rectifiability $\Leftrightarrow$ existence of a unit density a.e. — measure-theoretic smoothness reduces to a one-dimensional limit at almost every point.

De Giorgi's structure theorem for finite-perimeter sets is exactly: the reduced boundary is countably $(n-1)$-rectifiable. So §2's $\partial^*E$ is the canonical example of a rectifiable set, and the "surface area equals $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$" identity is a consequence of the density theorem applied to it.

4. Area and coarea formulas

Two formulas tie together Lipschitz maps, Hausdorff measures, and integration on rectifiable sets. Both hold for Lipschitz $f$ — far broader than $C^1$ — because Rademacher's theorem gives differentiability $\mathcal{L}^k$-almost everywhere.

Area formula. For Lipschitz $f:\mathbb{R}^k\to\mathbb{R}^n$ with $k\le n$ and Borel $A\subset\mathbb{R}^k$:

$$\int_A J_f(x)\,d\mathcal{L}^k(x) \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \mathcal{H}^0\bigl(A\cap f^{-1}(y)\bigr)\,d\mathcal{H}^k(y),$$

where $J_f(x)=\sqrt{\det(Df(x)^T Df(x))}$ is the $k$-Jacobian. Read right-to-left: integrate the cardinality of preimage fibres against the $k$-dimensional Hausdorff measure on the image, and you recover the Jacobian-weighted Lebesgue integral on the source. When $f$ is injective and $A$ is a parameter region, the right side reduces to $\mathcal{H}^k(f(A))$ and the area formula becomes the classical change-of-variables / surface-area formula: $\mathcal{H}^k(f(A))=\int_A J_f\,d\mathcal{L}^k$. Non-injective $f$ multiply-covers parts of the image; the multiplicity factor $\mathcal{H}^0(A\cap f^{-1}(y))$ is exactly the correction.

Coarea formula. For Lipschitz $u:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ with $m\le n$ and integrable $g$:

$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} g(x)\,J_u(x)\,d\mathcal{L}^n(x) \;=\; \int_{\mathbb{R}^m}\Bigl(\int_{u^{-1}(y)} g\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-m}\Bigr)\,d\mathcal{L}^m(y).$$

The level set $u^{-1}(y)$ is $(n-m)$-rectifiable for $\mathcal{L}^m$-a.e. $y$ (the GMT-form of Sard's theorem), and the formula slices a top-dimensional integral by integrating against level-set integrals. The most-cited special case is $m=1$, $g=1$:

$$\int_\Omega |\nabla u|\,dx \;=\; \int_{-\infty}^\infty \mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Omega\cap\{u=t\})\,dt.$$

Total variation of a smooth $u$ as integrated level-set perimeter — the bridge that, extended to $u\in\mathrm{BV}$, identifies $|Du|(\Omega)=\int|D\chi_{\{u>t\}}|(\Omega)\,dt$ with the integrated perimeter of superlevel sets. Read this as: every BV function is a continuous combination of indicator functions of finite-perimeter sets, weighted by the height of the level. This decomposition is what makes the BV-perimeter equivalence (a Cavalieri-style integration) the operational core of GMT — minimizing $\int|\nabla u|$ over $u$ reduces to simultaneously minimizing $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial^*\{u>t\})$ for almost every $t$.

5. Currents and Federer–Fleming compactness

De Rham's currents generalize oriented submanifolds the way distributions generalize functions: a $k$-current $T$ is a continuous linear functional on the space $\mathcal{D}^k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ of smooth, compactly supported $k$-forms. An oriented $k$-submanifold $M$ defines its integration current $[M]$ by

$$\langle [M],\omega\rangle \;=\; \int_M\omega,\qquad\omega\in\mathcal{D}^k(\mathbb{R}^n).$$

Currents have a boundary: $\partial T$ is the $(k-1)$-current with $\langle\partial T,\eta\rangle=\langle T,d\eta\rangle$. For an integration current $[M]$ this recovers Stokes' theorem: $\partial[M]=[\partial M]$. Currents have a mass

$$\mathbf{M}(T)\;=\;\sup\bigl\{\,\langle T,\omega\rangle\,:\,\omega\in\mathcal{D}^k,\,\|\omega\|_\infty\le 1\,\bigr\},$$

which generalizes $\mathcal{H}^k$-measure: $\mathbf{M}([M])=\mathcal{H}^k(M)$ for an oriented submanifold. The class of integral currents $\mathbf{I}_k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ consists of locally rectifiable $k$-currents $T$ (carried by an $\mathcal{H}^k$-rectifiable set with integer-valued multiplicity and $\mathcal{H}^k$-measurable orientation) for which $\partial T$ is itself locally rectifiable. They are the GMT replacement for oriented Lipschitz chains.

Federer–Fleming compactness theorem. Let $\{T_j\}\subset\mathbf{I}_k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ be a sequence with $\mathrm{spt}(T_j)\subset K$ (a fixed compact set) and uniform bounds $\mathbf{M}(T_j)+\mathbf{M}(\partial T_j)\le C$. Then there is a subsequence $T_{j_l}$ converging weakly (i.e., $\langle T_{j_l},\omega\rangle\to\langle T,\omega\rangle$ for every $\omega\in\mathcal{D}^k$) to an integral current $T\in\mathbf{I}_k(\mathbb{R}^n)$, with $\mathbf{M}(T)\le\liminf\mathbf{M}(T_{j_l})$ and $\mathbf{M}(\partial T)\le\liminf\mathbf{M}(\partial T_{j_l})$.

This is the engine of GMT existence theory. Compactness in classical function spaces (Arzelà-Ascoli on $C^0$, Rellich on $W^{1,p}$) cannot extract a limit from a minimizing sequence of surfaces: equiboundedness in $\mathcal{H}^k$ alone is too weak, and the limit can fail to be a manifold. Integral currents — measure-theoretic generalized surfaces with quantized multiplicity — form a class large enough to be closed under weak limits of mass-bounded minimizing sequences and small enough to retain a coherent boundary calculus. The Plateau-problem proof is now mechanical: take a minimizing sequence of currents with prescribed boundary, extract a weakly convergent subsequence by Federer-Fleming, and the limit minimizes mass.

6. Applications: Plateau, minimal surfaces, and Mumford–Shah

Three flagship arenas where GMT replaces ad-hoc PDE / variational tools with a uniform existence theorem:

Plateau's problem. Given a closed $(k-1)$-current $\Gamma$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with finite mass, find a $k$-current $T$ with $\partial T=\Gamma$ minimizing mass — the higher-dimensional soap-film problem. Federer-Fleming (§5) does the heavy lifting: a minimizing sequence $T_j$ has uniformly bounded mass (any competitor is an upper bound) and fixed boundary mass $\mathbf{M}(\Gamma)$; pass to a weakly convergent subsequence with limit $T$ satisfying $\partial T=\Gamma$ and $\mathbf{M}(T)=\inf$. Existence is mechanical.

Regularity is delicate, and clean statements need a slightly larger object than a current. A varifold is a Radon measure on the Grassmannian bundle $\mathbb{R}^n\times G(n,k)$ — informally, a current with orientation forgotten but tangent-plane data retained. Currents lose embedded-vs-immersed information (a soap film that self-touches has $\partial T=\Gamma$ degenerate to nothing meaningful at the contact); varifolds keep the unsigned mass and the tangent-plane field. An integral varifold is the rectifiable, integer-multiplicity sub-class. A varifold is stationary when the first variation of mass under any compactly-supported diffeomorphism vanishes — the variational analogue of vanishing mean curvature. Allard's regularity theorem says the support of a stationary integral varifold is a $C^{1,\alpha}$-submanifold $\mathcal{H}^k$-almost everywhere. A celebrated chain of results (Fleming, De Giorgi, Almgren, Simons) shows that codimension-1 minimizers are $C^\infty$ in dimensions $n\le 7$, with the Simons cone $\{x_1^2+\cdots+x_4^2=x_5^2+\cdots+x_8^2\}$ giving the first singular minimizer in $\mathbb{R}^8$.

Mumford-Shah image segmentation. The functional

$$\mathcal{E}(u,K)\;=\;\int_{\Omega\setminus K}(u-g)^2\,dx \;+\;\alpha\int_{\Omega\setminus K}|\nabla u|^2\,dx\;+\;\beta\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(K)$$

is minimized over pairs $(u,K)$ with $K\subset\Omega$ a closed singular set and $u\in C^1(\Omega\setminus K)$: a piecewise-smooth approximation $u$ to the noisy data $g$, paired with an edge set $K$ whose total $(n-1)$-measure is penalized. Direct minimization on closed-set pairs fails because the obvious topology on closed sets is bad — Hausdorff convergence does not control $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}$. The fix is to lift $K$ into the BV decomposition. Special BV functions $\mathrm{SBV}(\Omega)$ are the subspace of $\mathrm{BV}(\Omega)$ cut out by $D^c u=0$: only the absolutely-continuous and jump parts survive, with $K=J_u$ then a rectifiable jump set. Ambrosio's SBV compactness theorem (1989) is the version of Federer-Fleming for this class: bounded sequences in SBV with bounded $\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(J_u)$ have $L^1$-convergent subsequences whose limit lies in SBV with the diffuse Cantor singularities zero'd out — exactly what makes Mumford-Shah well-posed. De Giorgi-Carriero-Leaci then close the singular set $K=\overline{J_u}$ via density estimates: GMT making a computer-vision functional rigorous.

Mean-curvature flow and Cheeger inequalities. A Brakke flow is a weak solution of mean-curvature flow defined as a one-parameter family $\{V_t\}$ of varifolds whose mass decreases at the squared mean curvature: $\frac{d}{dt}\mathbf{M}(V_t)\le -\int|H|^2\,dV_t$. Past pinch-off / topology-change singularities the smooth flow ceases to make sense; the varifold formulation continues. The Cheeger constant $h(\Omega)=\inf_E P(E)/\min(|E|,|\Omega\setminus E|)$ — a perimeter-volume isoperimetric quotient — gives the Cheeger inequality $\lambda_1(\Omega)\ge h(\Omega)^2/4$, an isoperimetric lower bound on the Laplacian spectral gap. Cheeger uses the coarea bridge directly (level-set perimeter argument); Brakke flow uses varifold tangent-measure rectifiability and Allard-style regularity rather than coarea.

Connections

Geometric measure theory is the analytic toolkit for problems where the natural class of competitors is rougher than smooth submanifolds: minimal surfaces with corners, BV functions with jumps, edge sets in noisy images, fractal boundary phenomena. Open frontiers include the regularity of solutions to higher-codimension Plateau problems (Almgren's $Q$-valued functions, the De Lellis-Spadaro program), GMT on metric measure spaces (Cheeger differentiation, Ambrosio-Kirchheim currents in metric spaces, Heisenberg-group GMT), the Mumford-Shah conjecture on the regularity of singular sets in image segmentation, and Brakke-flow weak solutions for mean-curvature flow past singularities. Below the headline names, the field is still actively producing new compactness theorems for variant function classes (BD, GSBV, GBD).

  • Measure theory — Hausdorff measure & dimension — supplies $\mathcal{H}^k$, the substrate for rectifiability, currents, and the area / coarea formulas. The s-dimensional Hausdorff measure introduced there becomes the unit of mass for integration currents and the way perimeter is even defined.
  • Sobolev spaces & distributions — distributional / weak derivatives — the BV space sits one rung above $W^{1,1}$: the gradient is a Radon measure (allowing jumps and Cantor parts) rather than an $L^1$ function. Section 1's BV decomposition is exactly the Radon-Nikodym refinement of the distributional derivative.
  • Real analysis — bounded variation — the 1D classical $\mathrm{BV}([a,b])$ via Jordan's monotone-difference theorem is the point of departure. GMT's BV is the multivariable measure-valued upgrade, and the Cantor function reappears here as the canonical $\mathrm{BV}\setminus\mathrm{AC}$ counterexample.
  • Differential forms — Stokes & de Rham — currents are the distributional dual of compactly-supported smooth forms, and Stokes' theorem $\partial[M]=[\partial M]$ is built into the very definition of $\partial T$ on currents. Federer-Fleming's existence theorem is essentially de Rham's theory done with rectifiable carriers and integer multiplicities.
  • Variational methods — direct method — every existence theorem in §6 (Plateau, Mumford-Shah) is the direct method of the calculus of variations carried out in a function class (currents, SBV) chosen so that the requisite lower semicontinuity + compactness pair both hold. GMT is what enables the direct method to land outside Sobolev space.
  • Atiyah-Singer index theorem — Allard's regularity theorem and the regularity theory of minimal currents lean on elliptic estimates for the linearization of the area functional; the geometric-analytic regularity story for stationary varifolds is in the same intellectual neighborhood as parametrix-driven regularity in microlocal analysis.
