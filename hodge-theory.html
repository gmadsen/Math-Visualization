Hodge theory

For a smooth projective variety over $\mathbb{C}$, the rational cohomology $H^n(X; \mathbb{Q})$ is not just a vector space — it carries a canonical decomposition $H^n(X; \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} H^{p,q}$ that encodes the complex structure of $X$ and refines every cohomology calculation. Hodge theory is the study of this refinement: what it implies for the geometry of $X$, how it degenerates in families, how it organises invariants of singular and non-compact varieties (Deligne's mixed Hodge structures), and what the still-open Hodge conjecture predicts about which classes are algebraic.

1. Why Hodge structures? — cohomology gets refinement from complex structure

Take a smooth projective variety $X$ over $\mathbb{C}$ and consider its singular cohomology $H^n(X; \mathbb{Q})$ — a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space. As a topologist sees it, that's the whole story: the data is "$X$ has these Betti numbers, here are the cup products." But two facts make this incomplete.

Fact 1. $X(\mathbb{C})$ is also a complex manifold. So $X$ has a sheaf $\Omega^1_X$ of holomorphic 1-forms, and exterior powers $\Omega^p_X$ of holomorphic $p$-forms. Sheaf cohomology $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X)$ is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{C}$-vector space — the Dolbeault cohomology in bidegree $(p, q)$.

Fact 2. A complex projective variety is in particular a Kähler manifold (the Fubini–Study metric on $\mathbb{P}^N$ restricts). On a Kähler manifold the Laplacian $\Delta$ on differential forms commutes with the type decomposition $\Omega^k_\mathbb{C} = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \Omega^{p,q}$, so harmonic forms decompose by type:

$$\mathcal{H}^k(X; \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} \mathcal{H}^{p,q}(X).$$

And by Hodge theory on Riemannian manifolds, harmonic forms compute cohomology: $\mathcal{H}^k \cong H^k(X; \mathbb{C})$. The two facts together force the Hodge decomposition: $H^n(X; \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} H^{p,q}(X)$, with $H^{p,q} = H^q(X, \Omega^p_X)$.

Slogan. A topologist sees a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space; an algebraic geometer sees the same vector space sliced into $(p, q)$ pieces by complex structure; the slicing is forced by Kähler geometry and rigid under continuous variation. That extra rigidity is what makes Hodge theory more than topology with bookkeeping.

The data of "rational vector space + Hodge decomposition of its complexification" is what we abstract into a Hodge structure. The next several sections unpack what's in that data, what's preserved under varying $X$ in a family, and what the open questions are.

2. The Hodge decomposition theorem

The central theorem (Hodge, 1941) says: for $X$ a compact Kähler manifold of complex dimension $d$,

$$H^n(X; \mathbb{C}) \;=\; \bigoplus_{p+q=n} H^{p,q}(X), \qquad H^{p,q}(X) := H^q(X, \Omega^p_X),$$

and the conjugation symmetry $\overline{H^{p,q}} = H^{q,p}$ holds (where the bar is complex conjugation on $H^n(X; \mathbb{C})$ relative to the real subspace $H^n(X; \mathbb{R})$).

The numbers $h^{p,q}(X) := \dim_\mathbb{C} H^{p,q}(X)$ are the Hodge numbers. They sit in the Hodge diamond:

For a surface $X$ (so $d = 2$), the Hodge numbers fill out a $3 \times 3$ array $h^{p,q}$ with $0 \le p, q \le 2$ — the Hodge diamond — with two axes of symmetry: Hodge symmetry $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$ (from complex conjugation) and Poincaré duality $h^{p,q} = h^{d-p, d-q}$. So a surface has at most three independent Hodge numbers: $h^{1,0} = q$ (the irregularity), $h^{2,0} = p_g$ (the geometric genus), and $h^{1,1}$.

The Betti numbers fall out of the diamond by row sums: $b_n = \sum_{p+q=n} h^{p,q}$.

3. The Hodge filtration $F^\bullet$ — algebraic incarnation

The Hodge decomposition is gorgeous over $\mathbb{C}$ but uses the Kähler metric and complex conjugation; neither is purely algebraic. The Hodge filtration packages the same data using only algebraic input. Define

$$F^p H^n(X; \mathbb{C}) := \bigoplus_{p' \ge p} H^{p', n-p'}(X) \;=\; H^{p,n-p} \oplus H^{p+1,n-p-1} \oplus \cdots \oplus H^{n,0}.$$

Then $F^p$ is a decreasing filtration: $F^0 \supset F^1 \supset \cdots \supset F^n \supset F^{n+1} = 0$, and the Hodge decomposition is recovered by the relation

$$H^n(X; \mathbb{C}) = F^p \oplus \overline{F^{n-p+1}} \quad \text{for every } p.$$

So filtration + complex conjugation = decomposition. The pair $(H^n(X; \mathbb{Q}), F^\bullet H^n(X; \mathbb{C}))$ is the Hodge data without invoking harmonic forms.

Why this matters algebraically. The de Rham complex $\Omega^\bullet_X$ has a "stupid" filtration $\sigma^{\ge p} \Omega^\bullet_X = (0 \to \cdots \to 0 \to \Omega^p \to \Omega^{p+1} \to \cdots)$ — kill everything in degree $< p$. Taking hypercohomology gives a spectral sequence:

$$E_1^{p,q} = H^q(X, \Omega^p_X) \;\Rightarrow\; H^{p+q}_\dR(X).$$

This is the Hodge-to-de-Rham spectral sequence. The miracle for smooth projective $X$ over $\mathbb{C}$ is that it degenerates at $E_1$: $E_\infty^{p,q} = E_1^{p,q}$. Equivalently, the Hodge filtration $F^p H^n_\dR(X) := \mathrm{im}(\mathbb{H}^n(X, \sigma^{\ge p} \Omega^\bullet_X) \to H^n_\dR(X))$ is well-defined, and the associated graded $\mathrm{gr}^p_F H^n_\dR(X) = H^{n-p}(X, \Omega^p_X)$.

So algebraic de Rham cohomology with the Hodge filtration recovers the entire Dolbeault decomposition. Defined purely algebraically over the field $X$ is defined over (the filtration is rational; the conjugation-symmetric splitting is not).

4. Pure Hodge structures as a category

Strip away the variety: a pure $\mathbb{Q}$-Hodge structure of weight $n$ is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space $V_\mathbb{Q}$ together with a decomposition

$$V_\mathbb{C} := V_\mathbb{Q} \otimes_\mathbb{Q} \mathbb{C} \;=\; \bigoplus_{p+q=n} V^{p,q}, \qquad \overline{V^{p,q}} = V^{q,p}.$$

Equivalently, it is $V_\mathbb{Q}$ with a finite decreasing filtration $F^\bullet V_\mathbb{C}$ on the complexification, satisfying $V_\mathbb{C} = F^p \oplus \overline{F^{n-p+1}}$ for every $p$. The cohomology of a smooth projective variety is the canonical example, with $H^n(X; \mathbb{Q})$ as $V_\mathbb{Q}$.

Pure Hodge structures form a category $\HS_\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{pure}}$ with morphisms that respect the decomposition (equivalently, the filtration). Tensor products and duals of Hodge structures stay pure. The unit $\mathbb{Q}(0)$ has weight $0$ and type $(0,0)$. The Tate twist $\mathbb{Q}(1)$ has weight $-2$ and type $(-1,-1)$ — same Tate twist that organises the motivic story; the Hodge realization of $\mathbb{Q}(1)$ is $2\pi i \cdot \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{C}$.

Polarisation upgrades a pure Hodge structure to a "polarised" one: a $(-1)^n$-symmetric bilinear form $V_\mathbb{Q} \times V_\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Q}$ that is positive on the primitive part. Cup product against the Kähler class gives polarisation on $H^n(X; \mathbb{Q})$ for compact Kähler $X$. The category $\HS_\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{pol}}$ of polarisable pure Hodge structures is semisimple — every short exact sequence splits.

Tannakian frame. $\HS_\mathbb{Q}$ is a neutral Tannakian category over $\mathbb{Q}$ (with the forgetful $V \mapsto V_\mathbb{Q}$ as fiber functor), so it has a Tannakian fundamental group — the Mumford-Tate group of all Hodge structures. The Mumford-Tate group of a single Hodge structure $V$ is the Tannakian group of the smallest sub-Tannakian category containing $V$.

5. Mixed Hodge structures (Deligne)

The Hodge decomposition fails for non-compact or singular varieties. The cohomology of $\mathbb{C}^* = \mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ is $H^1(\mathbb{C}^*; \mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Q}$, but neither weight-$0$ nor weight-$1$ Hodge structure fits — the answer is "extension of weight 2 by weight 0" (or, after a Tate twist, weight $-2$ by weight $0$).

Deligne's mixed Hodge structures (1971-74) repair the failure. A mixed $\mathbb{Q}$-Hodge structure is the data of:

  • a $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space $V_\mathbb{Q}$,
  • an increasing weight filtration $W_\bullet$ on $V_\mathbb{Q}$,
  • a decreasing Hodge filtration $F^\bullet$ on $V_\mathbb{C}$,

such that the induced filtrations make each graded piece $\mathrm{gr}^W_n V := W_n / W_{n-1}$ a pure Hodge structure of weight $n$ (with the induced Hodge filtration).

So a mixed Hodge structure is "pure Hodge structures glued by extensions across weights."

Deligne's theorem: every complex variety $X$ — smooth or singular, compact or open — has a canonical mixed Hodge structure on $H^n(X; \mathbb{Q})$, functorial in $X$. Smoothness controls the Hodge filtration; properness controls the weight filtration. The pure case (smooth + projective) collapses the weight filtration to a single weight $n$ — recovering the Hodge decomposition.

The category $\MHS_\mathbb{Q}$ of mixed Hodge structures is abelian (not semisimple — extensions are nontrivial), Tannakian, and contains $\HS_\mathbb{Q}^{\mathrm{pure}}$ as the full subcategory of pure objects.

6. The Hodge conjecture

For $X$ smooth projective over $\mathbb{C}$ and $0 \le p \le \dim X$, the cycle-class map sends a codimension-$p$ algebraic cycle to a class in $H^{2p}(X; \mathbb{Q})$:

$$\mathrm{cl}\colon \CH^p(X) \otimes \mathbb{Q} \;\longrightarrow\; H^{2p}(X; \mathbb{Q}).$$

The image lands inside the Hodge classes: the rational classes of pure Hodge type $(p, p)$,

$$\Hdg^p(X) := H^{2p}(X; \mathbb{Q}) \cap H^{p,p}(X) \;\subset\; H^{2p}(X; \mathbb{Q}).$$

The Hodge conjecture (1950) predicts that this is all of $\Hdg^p(X)$ — every rational class of type $(p, p)$ comes from an algebraic cycle.

Open since 1950. A Clay Millennium prize problem. Known for $p = 1$ (the Lefschetz $(1,1)$-theorem: every integral $(1,1)$-class is the cycle class of a divisor — comes from the exponential sequence and the de Rham comparison). Known for abelian varieties of dimension $\le 4$ (André, Deligne for general absolute Hodge classes), for low-dimensional moduli, for Calabi-Yau threefolds in many cases — but in general, we don't know how to produce algebraic cycles to match every Hodge class. Counterexamples to the integral version are known (Atiyah-Hirzebruch, Totaro); the rational version remains the live problem.

Equivalent reformulations make the conjecture more tractable in special cases:

  • Tannakian. The Hodge realization $G_{\mathrm{mot}}(\mathbb{Q}) \twoheadrightarrow \MT(X)$ should be surjective — every Mumford-Tate-group symmetry of cohomology comes from a motivic symmetry. (This is the formulation that makes contact with motives.)
  • Absolute. Deligne's "absolute Hodge classes" — those that remain Hodge under all conjugates by automorphisms of $\mathbb{C}$ — are conjecturally exactly the algebraic ones. Known for abelian varieties (Deligne).

7. Period domains and variations of Hodge structure

A family of varieties $\pi\colon \mathcal{X} \to S$ over a base $S$ produces, for each $s \in S$, a Hodge structure on $H^n(\mathcal{X}_s; \mathbb{Q})$. As $s$ varies, the $\mathbb{Q}$-vector space stays constant locally (over a simply-connected base) but the Hodge filtration $F^\bullet$ moves. The resulting object is a variation of Hodge structure (VHS) — a flat $\mathbb{Q}$-bundle plus a holomorphically-varying Hodge filtration.

The receiving space is the period domain $D$ — a complex manifold parametrising all polarised Hodge structures of the given type (weight, dimension, polarisation). For weight-$1$ Hodge structures of dimension $2g$ with the standard symplectic polarisation, $D$ is the Siegel upper half-space $\mathfrak{H}_g$ — and the period domain for an abelian variety's $H^1$ is exactly this. For higher weight the period domain is no longer Hermitian symmetric in general, which is a major source of the difficulty.

Griffiths transversality. The derivative of the period map $S \to D$ is constrained: $\nabla F^p \subset F^{p-1} \otimes \Omega^1_S$. So the period map cannot move freely — its differential drops at most one step in the Hodge filtration per direction. This is what makes the period map locally injective on most families, and what makes the global period map (after taking a quotient by the monodromy) often algebraic.

Key examples. The period map for elliptic curves $\mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{M}_{1,1}$ recovers the classical j-line via the Weierstrass ℘-function. The period map for K3 surfaces lands in a 20-dimensional period domain and is generically a local isomorphism (Torelli theorem for K3s). For a generic Calabi-Yau threefold the period map is dominant onto its image but with Griffiths transversality as the binding constraint.

8. Hodge as a motivic realization

The Hodge realization functor $R_\Hdg\colon \mathsf{Mot}_\mathbb{Q} \to \HS_\mathbb{Q}$ sends a motive to its Hodge structure. It is one of the four classical realizations from §7 of motives; the others are Betti, $\ell$-adic étale, and crystalline.

The Hodge-realization quotient $G_{\mathrm{mot}}(\mathbb{Q}) \twoheadrightarrow \MT$ is exactly what the Hodge conjecture asks to be surjective. So the conjecture is the statement: every Hodge-theoretic symmetry of cohomology lifts to a motivic symmetry. Modulo the standard conjectures + Hodge, motives over $\mathbb{Q}$ would be entirely determined by their Hodge realizations — the Tannakian formalism would collapse to "studying motives is studying their Hodge structures."

The unconditional bridge is more modest but already powerful: the category $\MHS_\mathbb{Q}$ of mixed Hodge structures is unconditionally Tannakian, and Voevodsky's $\mathsf{DM}(\mathbb{Q})$ has a Hodge realization $\mathsf{DM}(\mathbb{Q}) \to D^b(\MHS_\mathbb{Q})$ (Huber, Bondarko). So mixed motives — at least in their triangulated avatar — already factor through mixed Hodge structures, even without the standard conjectures.

Mixed Tate Hodge. The Hodge realization restricts to a fully faithful functor on mixed Tate motives over $\mathbb{Z}$ (Deligne–Goncharov, Brown). This is the unconditionally-true motivic Galois group story: the category of mixed Tate Hodge structures captures multiple zeta values, and the explicit motivic Galois group acting on them is computed.

Connections

Hodge theory is one of the great unifying threads of modern algebraic geometry. It cleans up cohomology with extra structure forced by complex geometry, packages that structure into a Tannakian category, and ties into nearly every current frontier — the Hodge conjecture remains a Clay Millennium problem, the period map underwrites Calabi-Yau enumerative geometry and mirror symmetry, mixed Hodge modules (Saito's massive 1980s extension) provide the right framework for $\mathcal{D}$-modules with extra structure on singular varieties, and $p$-adic Hodge theory (a different beast — see Fontaine) does the analogous work over $p$-adic fields.

  • Algebraic de Rham cohomology — the algebraic foundation; the Hodge filtration is defined here. The Hodge-to-de-Rham spectral sequence's $E_1$-degeneration is the algebraic mechanism behind §3.
  • Motives — the Hodge realization is one of the four classical realizations; the Hodge conjecture rephrases as surjectivity of $G_{\mathrm{mot}} \twoheadrightarrow \MT$.
  • Calabi-Yau manifolds — the period map is the central calculational tool; mirror symmetry exchanges Hodge numbers $h^{p,q} \leftrightarrow h^{d-p, q}$.
  • Moduli spaces — variations of Hodge structure are the cohomological invariants of families; Torelli-type theorems say "moduli is determined by periods" for many classes of varieties.
  • Mirror symmetry — the celebrated $h^{p,q}(X) = h^{d-p, q}(X^\vee)$ exchange between mirror Calabi-Yau pairs is a Hodge-numerical statement; the categorical lift sits in $\mathsf{DM}$ + mixed Hodge.
