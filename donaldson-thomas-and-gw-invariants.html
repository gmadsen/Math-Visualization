Donaldson–Thomas & Gromov–Witten invariants

Two ways to count curves on a Calabi–Yau threefold: Donaldson–Thomas as ideal sheaves (algebra), Gromov–Witten as stable maps from curves (geometry), and the MNOP correspondence $q = -e^{iu}$ that unifies them through the combinatorics of 3D partitions and the topological vertex.

1. Moduli of ideal sheaves on a Calabi–Yau threefold

Let $X$ be a smooth projective Calabi–Yau 3-fold over $\mathbb{C}$: complex dimension $3$, trivial canonical bundle $K_X \cong \mathcal{O}_X$, and a nowhere-vanishing holomorphic 3-form $\Omega \in H^{3,0}(X)$. The Donaldson–Thomas (DT) programme asks a simple-sounding question: how many curves and points does $X$ contain, in each fixed homology class? The answer requires a moduli space — and the right one isn't the space of literal curves, but the space of ideal sheaves.

An ideal sheaf $\mathcal{I}_Z \subset \mathcal{O}_X$ is the kernel of a surjection $\mathcal{O}_X \twoheadrightarrow \mathcal{O}_Z$, where $Z \subset X$ is a closed subscheme. Equivalently, $\mathcal{I}_Z$ is a torsion-free sheaf of rank $1$ with $c_1 = 0$, so it differs from $\mathcal{O}_X$ only at higher Chern classes. We fix the topological invariants

$$\mathrm{ch}_2(\mathcal{I}_Z) \;=\; -\beta \in H^4(X,\mathbb{Z}) \;=\; H_2(X,\mathbb{Z}), \qquad \mathrm{ch}_3(\mathcal{I}_Z) \;=\; -n \in H^6(X,\mathbb{Z}) \;=\; \mathbb{Z}.$$

Here $\beta$ is a curve class and $n$ is a point count. The moduli space

$$I_n(X,\beta) \;=\; \{\mathcal{I}_Z \subset \mathcal{O}_X \;:\; \mathrm{ch}_2 = -\beta, \;\mathrm{ch}_3 = -n\}$$

parametrises every ideal sheaf with these invariants. In the simplest case $\beta = 0$, the subscheme $Z$ is zero-dimensional of length $n$, so $I_n(X,0) = \mathrm{Hilb}^n(X)$ is the Hilbert scheme of $n$ points: $n$ points of $X$ that may coincide with multiplicity. For $\beta \ne 0$ the subscheme $Z$ has 1-dimensional components — a curve in class $\beta$ — possibly decorated with embedded points.

As $s \to 0$, $n$ distinct points collide at the origin and the ideal $(x-a_1,y-b_1) \cap \cdots \cap (x-a_n,y-b_n)$ flows toward a non-reduced ideal of colength $n$ — e.g.\ $(x^n, y)$ or $(x^2, xy, y^2)$ for $n = 3$. Distinct points and fat points sit in the same moduli space $\mathrm{Hilb}^n$, which therefore has positive dimension at every fat-point boundary.

The story is more delicate on a 3-fold than on a surface. The Hilbert scheme $\mathrm{Hilb}^n(C^2)$ on a smooth surface is itself smooth of dimension $2n$ (Fogarty 1968). But $\mathrm{Hilb}^n(\mathbb{C}^3)$ is generically singular for $n \ge 4$: the deformation theory acquires obstructions in $\mathrm{Ext}^2(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I})$ and the moduli space has irreducible components of different dimensions. This is the central technical difficulty DT theory must overcome — the moduli space is "too big," and naive integrals on it diverge or give wrong answers.

2. The virtual fundamental class

The fix is the virtual fundamental class $[M]^{\mathrm{vir}}$, introduced by Behrend–Fantechi (1997) and Li–Tian (1998). When a moduli space $M$ is singular, has too high dimension, or both, $[M]^{\mathrm{vir}}$ is a homology class of the expected dimension that the integrals "should" use. The data needed to build it is a perfect obstruction theory: a morphism

$$\phi \colon E^\bullet \;\longrightarrow\; \mathbb{L}_M$$

in the derived category, where $E^\bullet$ is a complex of locally free sheaves supported in degrees $-1$ and $0$, and $\mathbb{L}_M$ is the cotangent complex of $M$. The map must induce an isomorphism on $h^0$ and a surjection on $h^{-1}$. Unpacking: $h^0(E^\bullet)$ is the cotangent / tangent sheaf, while $h^{-1}(E^\bullet)$ is the obstruction sheaf $\mathcal{O}b$ — first-order deformations that fail to lift.

The virtual dimension is the rank:

$$\mathrm{vd} \;=\; \mathrm{rk}\,(E^{-1}) - \mathrm{rk}\,(E^0) \;=\; \dim\,h^0(E^\bullet) \,-\, \dim\,h^{-1}(E^\bullet) \quad \text{(at each point of }M\text{)}.$$

For a smooth moduli space, $\mathrm{Ob} = 0$, $E^\bullet \simeq \Omega_M$ concentrated in degree $0$, and $[M]^{\mathrm{vir}} = [M]$. For a singular moduli space, the construction takes the intrinsic normal cone of $M$ (which lives in the bundle stack of $E^\bullet$), intersects with the zero section, and produces a class of the right dimension.

The CY3 case is special: Serre duality $\mathrm{Ext}^i(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I}) \cong \mathrm{Ext}^{3-i}(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{I})^\vee$ pairs $\mathrm{Ext}^1$ (deformations) with $\mathrm{Ext}^2$ (obstructions). They have the same dimension, so $\mathrm{vd} = 0$ — DT invariants are honest integers.

Behrend's theorem. When the obstruction theory is symmetric (as on a CY3), $[M]^{\mathrm{vir}}$ equals a weighted Euler characteristic $\chi(M, \nu)$, where the Behrend function $\nu \colon M \to \mathbb{Z}$ is the constructible function $\nu(x) = (-1)^{\dim_x M}(1 - \chi(\mathrm{MF}_x))$. The local Chern–Simons potential whose Milnor fibre $\mathrm{MF}_x$ appears here is what makes DT theory "categorified" — the count is geometric, not just an integral.

3. Donaldson–Thomas invariants

With the virtual class in hand, the Donaldson–Thomas invariant is the simplest possible integral:

$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{DT}_{n,\beta}(X) \;=\; \int_{[I_n(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}}} 1 \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}.\;}$$

The virtual dimension is $0$ (CY3 Serre duality), so the integral of $1$ is a number — a signed count. Equivalently, by Behrend, it's $\chi(I_n(X,\beta), \nu)$. Packaging all the integers into a generating series:

$$Z_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,Q) \;=\; \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}, \;\beta \in H_2(X,\mathbb{Z})} \mathrm{DT}_{n,\beta}(X) \, q^n \, Q^\beta.$$

The variable $q$ tracks point count, $Q^\beta$ tracks curve class. The series factors as $Z_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,Q) \;=\; Z_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,0) \cdot Z'_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,Q)$, where the degree-zero piece $Z_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,0)$ collects ideal sheaves of points alone (no curve part) and the reduced quotient $Z'_{\mathrm{DT}}$ carries the curve-counting content.

The MacMahon function

The simplest CY3 is $X = \mathbb{C}^3$ — non-compact, but with an action by the torus $T = (\mathbb{C}^\times)^3$ that lets one apply localisation. The DT count of length-$n$ subschemes of $\mathbb{C}^3$ supported at the origin equals the number of 3D partitions of $n$ (also called plane partitions): ways of stacking $n$ unit cubes in the positive octant such that each cube has support beneath it on all three coordinate planes. The generating function is the MacMahon function:

$$M(q) \;=\; \prod_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{(1-q^k)^k} \;=\; 1 + q + 3q^2 + 6q^3 + 13q^4 + 24q^5 + 48q^6 + 86q^7 + 160q^8 + 282q^9 + \cdots$$

Coefficient of $q^n$ in $M(q)$ is the number of plane partitions of size $n$. $M(q)$ has radius of convergence $1$ but grows extremely fast in the coefficients ($\log p_3(n) \sim c \, n^{2/3}$ as $n \to \infty$).

For a general compact CY3-fold $X$, the degree-zero DT series is itself a universal MacMahon, raised to the Euler characteristic:

$$Z_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,0) \;=\; M(-q)^{\chi(X)} \quad \text{(MNOP conjecture, now theorem on every CY3)}.$$

For the quintic threefold $X \subset \mathbb{P}^4$ with $\chi(X) = -200$, the point-only DT count is $M(-q)^{-200}$ — encoding nothing about the actual quintic geometry beyond its Euler characteristic. All the geometry sits in the reduced part $Z'_{\mathrm{DT}}$.

4. Gromov–Witten invariants

The Gromov–Witten (GW) approach to counting curves on $X$ takes the dual perspective: parametrise maps from curves rather than ideal sheaves. A stable map is data $(C, p_1,\ldots,p_n, f)$ where

  • $C$ is a connected, projective, at-worst-nodal complex curve of arithmetic genus $g$;
  • $p_1,\ldots,p_n \in C^{\mathrm{sm}}$ are distinct smooth marked points;
  • $f \colon C \to X$ is a morphism with $f_*[C] = \beta$;
  • the automorphism group of $(C, p_\bullet, f)$ is finite (the stability condition).

Stability rules out unstable components — e.g.\ $\mathbb{P}^1$-components contracted by $f$ with fewer than 3 special (node or marked) points. The moduli space

$$\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$$

is a proper Deligne–Mumford stack. Its expected dimension is

$$\mathrm{vd} \;=\; \int_\beta c_1(X) \;+\; (\dim X - 3)(1 - g) \;+\; n.$$

On a Calabi–Yau 3-fold both summands vanish — $c_1(X) = 0$ and $\dim X - 3 = 0$ — so $\mathrm{vd}_{g,0}(X,\beta) = 0$ in every genus and every class. The GW invariant is the virtual count:

$$\mathrm{GW}_{g,\beta}(X) \;=\; \int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,0}(X,\beta)]^{\mathrm{vir}}} 1 \;\in\; \mathbb{Q}.$$

Note the rational target: $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$ is a stack with finite automorphism groups, so the virtual class integrates to a rational number weighted by $1/|\mathrm{Aut}|$.

GW invariants $\mathrm{GW}_{g,\beta}$ are bi-indexed by genus $g$ and class $\beta$ — generating variable $u^{2g-2}$. DT invariants $\mathrm{DT}_{n,\beta}$ are bi-indexed by point count $n$ and class $\beta$ — generating variable $q^n$. MNOP says these are the same series after $q = -e^{iu}$.

The Gopakumar–Vafa integrality conjecture

GW counts are rational, which is unsatisfying compared to DT integers. The Gopakumar–Vafa (1998) conjecture explains the rationals as universal contributions from integer BPS-state counts $n^g_\beta \in \mathbb{Z}$. Schematically,

$$\sum_g \mathrm{GW}_{g,\beta}\, u^{2g-2} \;=\; \sum_{g \ge 0} n^g_\beta \sum_{d \ge 1} \frac{1}{d}\left(2 \sin\!\tfrac{du}{2}\right)^{2g-2}\, Q^{d\beta},$$

and $n^g_\beta \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the conjectural BPS count. For the quintic at genus zero, $n^0_d$ recovers the famous Candelas–de la Ossa–Green–Parkes numbers $2{,}875$, $609{,}250$, $317{,}206{,}375$, …

5. The MNOP correspondence

The headline equality of the subject is the Maulik–Nekrasov–Okounkov–Pandharipande conjecture (2003). After dividing out the universal degree-zero MacMahon factor, the reduced DT generating series equals the GW generating series under a substitution:

$$\boxed{\;Z'_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,Q) \;=\; Z_{\mathrm{GW}}(X;u,Q) \quad \text{with} \quad q \;=\; -e^{iu}.\;}$$

The conjecture was proved for all toric CY3-folds by Maulik–Oblomkov–Okounkov–Pandharipande (2011), and for many non-toric examples (the quintic, local curves, products of curves) by various groups. A universal proof for arbitrary smooth projective CY3-folds remains open.

The substitution $q = -e^{iu}$ is where algebra and geometry collide. On the DT side, $q$ is a formal power-series variable tracking integer point counts. On the GW side, $u$ is the genus-expansion parameter — formal in $u^{2g-2}$. The exponential map $q = -e^{iu} = -1 - iu - u^2/2 + \cdots$ converts a finite-genus power series in $u$ into an analytic-looking series in $q$.

Worked on the resolved conifold $X = \mathcal{O}(-1) \oplus \mathcal{O}(-1) \to \mathbb{P}^1$. Genus-zero GW: $\sum_d \mathrm{GW}_{0,d} \, Q^d = -\sum_d Q^d / d^2$. Genus expansion adds higher-genus corrections in $u$. The MNOP substitution converts to a $q$-series that exactly matches the DT count of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules.

Strategic upshot. The MNOP correspondence is the right enumerative-geometry analogue of Hodge-theoretic mirror symmetry: it turns the GW prediction of Candelas–de la Ossa–Green–Parkes for the quintic into a (proven) integer count of ideal sheaves. Every DT invariant computable from the topological vertex on a toric CY3 yields, via MNOP, a Gromov–Witten genus expansion — no integrals over $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$ required.

6. The topological vertex

Toric Calabi–Yau threefolds are open / non-compact CY3-folds with a torus action that lets equivariant localisation reduce everything to combinatorics. Aganagic, Klemm, Mariño, and Vafa (2003) introduced the topological vertex $C_{\lambda\mu\nu}(q)$ as the universal contribution from a single trivalent vertex in the toric diagram. The diagram is a planar trivalent graph; each leg (edge going to $\infty$) carries an asymptotic Young diagram $\lambda$, $\mu$, or $\nu$ that prescribes the boundary partition seen by the count of 3D partitions.

The closed form, in terms of skew Schur functions $s_{\lambda/\mu}$:

$$C_{\lambda\mu\nu}(q) \;=\; q^{\kappa(\lambda)/2}\, s_{\nu^t}(q^\rho) \sum_{\eta} s_{\lambda^t/\eta}(q^{\nu+\rho})\, s_{\mu/\eta}(q^{\nu^t+\rho}),$$

where $\rho = (-\tfrac12, -\tfrac32, -\tfrac52, \ldots)$ is the half-integer shift, $\kappa(\lambda) = \sum_i \lambda_i (\lambda_i - 2i + 1)$, and the variables are evaluated at the principal specialisation. For the trivial-partition input,

$$C_{\emptyset\emptyset\emptyset}(q) \;=\; M(q) \;=\; \prod_{k\ge 1}(1-q^k)^{-k},$$

recovering the MacMahon count of plane partitions in a single corner of $\mathbb{C}^3$.

The toric diagram is a planar trivalent graph: each vertex is a $\mathbb{C}^3$-chart, each internal edge is a gluing $\mathbb{P}^1$ with framing factor $(-1)^{a_1 a_2}q^{-a_1 a_2/2 - \kappa(\eta)/2}$. The partition function $Z_{\mathrm{DT}}(X;q,Q) = \sum_{\eta\text{ on each edge}} \prod_{\text{vertices}} C_{\lambda\mu\nu}(q) \cdot \prod_{\text{edges}} (\text{framing})$.

The topological vertex is more than a calculational tool. Its existence reveals that DT/GW counts on toric CY3-folds are governed by the combinatorics of melting crystal models: the 3D partitions counted by $M(q)$ are configurations of a 3D Ising-style atomic crystal melted at temperature $\log q$. The exact same combinatorial objects appear in the Hilbert series of $\mathrm{Sym}^\bullet(\mathbb{C}^3)$, in the Schur–Weyl duality for $GL_\infty$, and in the operator-algebra approach to Hurwitz numbers — a deep web of unifications hiding under one Schur function.

7. Stability conditions and wall-crossing

The DT invariants we have written down so far depend on a hidden choice — the stability condition defining which ideal sheaves "count." Sheafy stability (Gieseker / slope) parametrises the simplest case, but on derived categories the right framework is Bridgeland stability: a heart of a bounded $t$-structure on $D^b\mathrm{Coh}(X)$ together with a central charge $Z\colon K(X) \to \mathbb{C}$ landing in the upper half-plane.

Stability conditions vary in a complex manifold $\mathrm{Stab}(X)$. As the parameter $\sigma$ moves across a wall — a real codimension-1 locus where some object's phase $\phi(E) = \arg Z(E)/\pi$ coincides with another's — semistable objects switch identity and the DT count $\mathrm{DT}_\sigma(\alpha)$ in a fixed class $\alpha$ jumps.

Across the wall (at $\sigma = 0$ here) the phases swap: $\phi(\alpha_1) > \phi(\alpha_2)$ vs.\ $\phi(\alpha_1) < \phi(\alpha_2)$. Semistability of $E$ in class $\alpha$ flips, and Joyce / KS gives the universal jump formula.

The universal jump formula was found in different forms by Joyce (2008, motivic Hall algebra) and Kontsevich–Soibelman (2008, quantum-torus identity). Both express the product of formal group elements $U_\alpha^{\mathrm{DT}(\alpha)}$ taken with respect to the wall-ordering of phases as a constant of motion: the product is the same on either side of the wall, even though the individual $\mathrm{DT}(\alpha)$ counts jump.

Refinements upgrade integer DT counts to motivic / refined DT invariants: Poincaré polynomials in a formal variable $y$ that recover the integers at $y = -1$ via Behrend's theorem. The wall-crossing identity then involves quantum dilogarithms $\mathbb{E}_q(x) = \prod_{k \ge 0}(1 + q^{k+1/2}x)^{-1}$ in place of plain group elements — a quantum-cluster-algebra structure that connects DT theory to cluster algebras, BPS spectrum computations in $\mathcal{N} = 2$ supersymmetric gauge theories, and stability conditions on the Fukaya category via homological mirror symmetry.

8. Connections

Donaldson–Thomas theory now sits at the intersection of enumerative algebraic geometry, mirror symmetry, BPS state counting in string theory, and quantum-cluster-algebra combinatorics. The frontier questions are sharp and active: a universal MNOP proof for arbitrary CY3-folds is open; the conjectural integer BPS counts of Gopakumar–Vafa have only been pinned down rigorously in genus zero on the quintic; refined DT and its Hodge-theoretic upgrade are still being worked out for higher-dimensional moduli; and the categorification of DT into cohomological Hall algebras (Kontsevich–Soibelman) suggests a 2-categorical structure whose full implications are unclear.

  • Mirror symmetry — A-model GW — the dual story: $Z_{\mathrm{GW}}$ on $X$ is computed from periods on the mirror $Y$, giving genus-zero predictions like the quintic count $2{,}875$ that DT confirms after MNOP.
  • Calabi–Yau manifolds — string compactifications, DT, SYZ — where the CY3 hypothesis comes from physically, and why both DT and GW are computing the same "BPS state index" of a type-II compactification.
  • Moduli spaces — stable curves — the GW side lives on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}(X,\beta)$, a virtual generalisation of Deligne–Mumford's $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$.
  • Gauge theory — Donaldson invariants — the original "DT" name: Donaldson's 4-manifold instanton count, which Thomas categorified to ideal-sheaf counts on a CY3 in his 1998 thesis.
  • Cluster algebras — Kontsevich–Soibelman's wall-crossing identities take the form of quantum-cluster mutation formulas; the entire BPS-spectrum structure factors through cluster combinatorics.
  • Toric varieties — the home of the topological vertex; every toric CY3 admits a vertex computation, and MNOP is a theorem in this setting.
