Information geometry

A family of probability distributions is a curved manifold, and the natural way to measure distance on it — the Fisher information — turns statistics into Riemannian geometry, with KL divergence as its squared infinitesimal length.

1. Statistical manifolds

The founding idea of information geometry: a parametric family of probability distributions is not just a set, it is a manifold. Fix a sample space and a family $\mathcal{M}=\{p_\theta : \theta\in\Theta\}$ smoothly parameterized by $\theta=(\theta^1,\dots,\theta^d)$. Then $\theta$ is a coordinate chart, each distribution is a point, and a smooth curve $\theta(t)$ is a path through distribution space.

What is a tangent vector? Moving infinitesimally along the coordinate $\theta^i$ changes the log-density, so the natural representative of the direction $\partial_i$ is the score

$\partial_i\,\ell_\theta(x)\ =\ \partial_i\log p_\theta(x), \qquad \ell_\theta:=\log p_\theta.$

The score is mean-zero. For any regular family, differentiating the normalization $\int p_\theta\,dx=1$ gives $$\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\ell_\theta]\ =\ \int \frac{\partial_i p_\theta}{p_\theta}\,p_\theta\,dx\ =\ \partial_i\!\int p_\theta\,dx\ =\ 0.$$ So the score lives in the space of mean-zero random variables — and its covariance, computed in the next section, will be the metric.

Identifying tangent vectors with score functions makes the tangent space at $\theta$ an inner-product space inside $L^2(p_\theta)$. That inner product is the Fisher metric, and it is what makes $\mathcal{M}$ Riemannian. The whole subject is the study of this one geometric structure on families of distributions.

Worked mini-example: the Gaussian upper half-plane

Take the one-dimensional Gaussian family $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $p_{(\mu,\sigma)}(x)=\tfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)$. The coordinates are $\mu\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\sigma > 0$, so as a manifold the family is the open upper half-plane $\{(\mu,\sigma):\sigma > 0\}$. The two scores are $\partial_\mu\ell=(x-\mu)/\sigma^2$ and $\partial_\sigma\ell=\big((x-\mu)^2-\sigma^2\big)/\sigma^3$, each manifestly mean-zero under $p_{(\mu,\sigma)}$.

2. The Fisher information metric

The tangent space at $\theta$ is spanned by scores; equip it with the $L^2(p_\theta)$ inner product of scores and you get the Fisher information metric

$g_{ij}(\theta)\ =\ \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\big[\,\partial_i\ell_\theta\,\partial_j\ell_\theta\,\big].$

Differentiating the mean-zero identity $\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_j\ell_\theta]=0$ once more in $\theta^i$ produces a second, equivalent form — the metric is also minus the expected Hessian of the log-likelihood:

Two faces of one matrix. $$g_{ij}(\theta)\ =\ \mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\ell_\theta\,\partial_j\ell_\theta]\ =\ -\,\mathbb{E}_\theta[\partial_i\partial_j\ell_\theta].$$ The first form shows $G(\theta)=(g_{ij})$ is a covariance, hence symmetric positive semidefinite; the second connects it to the curvature of the log-likelihood. It is a genuine $(0,2)$-tensor: under a smooth reparameterization $\theta\mapsto\phi$ with Jacobian $J$, $G_\phi=J^{-\top}G_\theta J^{-1}$, so geodesic distances between distributions are coordinate-free.

Because $G$ transforms tensorially, the length of a curve of distributions $\int\!\sqrt{\dot\theta^\top G(\theta)\,\dot\theta}\,dt$ depends only on the path of distributions, not on how we labeled them. This is the precise sense in which the Fisher metric is the intrinsic geometry of a statistical model — and Cramér–Rao gives it its statistical meaning: the inverse metric $G^{-1}$ lower-bounds the covariance of any unbiased estimator, $\operatorname{Var}_\theta(\hat\theta)\succeq G(\theta)^{-1}$.

Worked mini-example: the Gaussian is hyperbolic

For $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, using the scores from §1: $g_{\mu\mu}=\mathbb{E}[(x-\mu)^2]/\sigma^4=1/\sigma^2$, the cross term $g_{\mu\sigma}=0$ by symmetry, and $g_{\sigma\sigma}=\mathbb{E}[((x-\mu)^2-\sigma^2)^2]/\sigma^6=2/\sigma^2$. So

$G(\mu,\sigma)=\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2},\ \tfrac{2}{\sigma^2}\Big).$

This is (up to the constant $2$) the hyperbolic metric of the upper half-plane: a Fisher ball of fixed radius is small in $(\mu,\sigma)$ coordinates when $\sigma$ is small, because the factor $1/\sigma^2$ blows up there — nearby small-variance Gaussians are easy to tell apart, so they are far in Fisher length.

3. KL divergence & its local metric

The most natural measure of how different two distributions are is the Kullback–Leibler divergence

$\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\ =\ \mathbb{E}_{p}\!\Big[\log\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big]\ \ge\ 0,$

with equality iff $p=q$. But KL is not a distance: it is asymmetric, $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\ne\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)$, and it fails the triangle inequality. So how does it relate to the Riemannian Fisher metric of §2?

KL is locally the Fisher quadratic. Fix $\theta$ and expand $\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\,\|\,p_{\theta+\delta})$ in the small displacement $\delta$. At $\delta=0$ the value is $0$ (a minimum) and the gradient is $0$, so the leading term is the Hessian — and that Hessian is exactly the Fisher matrix: $$\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\,\|\,p_{\theta+\delta})\ =\ \tfrac12\,\delta^\top G(\theta)\,\delta\ +\ O(\|\delta\|^3).$$ The same expansion holds for the reversed $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta+\delta}\,\|\,p_\theta)$ — the asymmetry of KL only appears at third order, so to second order both directions agree and equal the symmetric Fisher form.

This is the bridge between statistics and geometry: KL has no metric structure globally, but its infinitesimal behaviour is a metric, and that metric is Fisher. Squared Riemannian length, in the small, is twice the KL divergence. It is why minimizing KL (as in maximum likelihood) is, locally, steepest descent in the Fisher geometry — the subject of §4.

Worked mini-example: two Bernoullis vs. the Fisher quadratic

For Bernoulli$(p)$ the Fisher information is $g(p)=1/(p(1-p))$. Compare the exact KL of two nearby Bernoullis with the quadratic approximation: at $p=0.5$, $g=4$, so $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,p+\delta)\approx \tfrac12\cdot 4\cdot\delta^2=2\delta^2$. For $\delta=0.02$ this predicts $0.0008$; the exact KL is $0.000800\ldots$ — agreement to the digits shown, with the gap growing only like $\delta^3$.

4. The natural gradient

Gradient descent updates $\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta)$. But $\nabla L$ is a covector — it transforms with the inverse Jacobian under reparameterization — whereas a step in parameter space is a vector. Subtracting a covector from a vector is geometrically illegitimate, and the practical symptom is that ordinary gradient descent depends on how you coordinatize the model: rescale or skew $\theta$ and the trajectory changes.

The fix is to raise the index with the Fisher metric. The natural gradient is

$\tilde\nabla L(\theta)\ =\ G(\theta)^{-1}\,\nabla L(\theta).$

Steepest descent in the Fisher metric. Among all unit-Fisher-length steps $\delta^\top G\,\delta=\varepsilon^2$, the one that decreases $L$ fastest points along $-G^{-1}\nabla L$. Because $G^{-1}$ is exactly the metric raising-of-index, $\tilde\nabla L$ is a true tangent vector, and the flow $\dot\theta=-G^{-1}\nabla L$ traces the same curve of distributions in every parameterization. The ordinary gradient flow does not.

Two consequences. First, invariance: the natural-gradient path is intrinsic to the model, immune to the arbitrary choice of coordinates. Second, conditioning: where the Fisher metric is badly anisotropic — a parameter that the data constrains tightly in one direction and loosely in another — the $G^{-1}$ factor automatically rescales each direction, so natural-gradient descent does not zig-zag down narrow valleys the way vanilla descent does. In maximum likelihood this is exactly Fisher scoring, and it underlies the asymptotic efficiency of the MLE.

Worked mini-example: a skewed reparameterization

Take a likelihood whose level sets are circles in coordinates $\theta$, so vanilla GD heads straight to the optimum. Now reparameterize by stretching one axis by a factor of $10$. In the new coordinates the level sets are thin ellipses; vanilla GD zig-zags badly because it ignores the metric. The natural gradient, using $G^{-1}$, undoes the stretch and again heads straight — its path matches the original, confirming invariance.

5. Exponential families & dual coordinates

Information geometry is sharpest on exponential families, the models

$p_\theta(x)\ =\ \exp\!\big(\theta\cdot T(x)-\psi(\theta)\big),$

where $T(x)$ is the sufficient statistic, $\theta$ the natural parameter, and $\psi(\theta)=\log\!\int e^{\theta\cdot T(x)}\,dx$ the log-partition function (a normalizer). Gaussians, Bernoulli, Poisson, exponential, gamma — all of them.

The log-partition does all the work. $\psi$ is convex, and its derivatives are moments of $T$: $$\nabla\psi(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_\theta[T]=:\eta,\qquad \nabla^2\psi(\theta)=\operatorname{Cov}_\theta[T]=G(\theta).$$ The gradient of $\psi$ defines the dual (expectation) coordinates $\eta$ — the mean of the sufficient statistic — and the Hessian of $\psi$ is the Fisher metric. Convexity of $\psi$ and positive-semidefiniteness of $G$ are the same fact.

Because $\psi$ is convex, its Legendre–Fenchel transform $\psi^*(\eta)=\sup_\theta(\theta\cdot\eta-\psi(\theta))$ exists, and the map $\theta\mapsto\eta=\nabla\psi(\theta)$ is a bijection with inverse $\eta\mapsto\theta=\nabla\psi^*(\eta)$. So an exponential family carries two coordinate systems, dual under Legendre: the natural parameters $\theta$ (in which the family is exponentially “flat”) and the expectation parameters $\eta$ (in which mixtures are flat). This is the dually-flat structure that §6 exploits.

Worked mini-example: Bernoulli is the logit–probability duality

Bernoulli$(p)$ with $T(x)=x\in\{0,1\}$ is exponential-family with natural parameter $\theta=\log\!\frac{p}{1-p}$ (the logit) and log-partition $\psi(\theta)=\log(1+e^\theta)$. Then

$\eta=\psi'(\theta)=\frac{e^\theta}{1+e^\theta}=\sigma(\theta)=p,\qquad \psi''(\theta)=p(1-p)=G(\theta).$

So the natural-to-mean map $\theta\mapsto\eta$ is exactly the logistic sigmoid $\sigma$, i.e. the inverse of the logit — the same logit–probability pair every classifier uses. The dual potential is $\psi^*(\eta)=\eta\log\eta+(1-\eta)\log(1-\eta)$, the negative binary entropy.

6. Dual connections & the Pythagorean theorem

The dually-flat structure of §5 means a statistical manifold carries not one but a pair of flat affine connections. The e-connection (exponential) makes the natural coordinates $\theta$ affine — its geodesics are exponential mixtures $p_t\propto p_0^{1-t}p_1^{t}$. The m-connection (mixture) makes the expectation coordinates $\eta$ affine — its geodesics are ordinary mixtures $p_t=(1-t)p_0+t\,p_1$.

Dual connections. The pair $(\nabla^{(e)},\nabla^{(m)})$ is dual with respect to the Fisher metric $g$: $$X\,g(Y,Z)\ =\ g\big(\nabla^{(e)}_X Y,\,Z\big)\ +\ g\big(Y,\,\nabla^{(m)}_X Z\big).$$ Neither connection alone is the Levi-Civita connection of $g$ (their average is). KL divergence is the canonical (Bregman) divergence of this structure: $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta_1}\,\|\,p_{\theta_2})=B_\psi(\theta_2,\theta_1)=\psi(\theta_2)-\psi(\theta_1)-\nabla\psi(\theta_1)\cdot(\theta_2-\theta_1)$.

The payoff is a genuinely geometric law for KL. Define orthogonality by the Fisher metric. Then:

Information-geometric Pythagorean theorem. If the m-geodesic from $p$ to $q$ meets the e-geodesic from $q$ to $r$ orthogonally at $q$, then $$\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,r)\ =\ \mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\ +\ \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,r).$$

This is the engine behind information projection: projecting a distribution onto a model is dropping a geodesic perpendicular, and the projection is the KL-closest point. It is the geometry of the EM algorithm (alternating e- and m-projections), of maximum-entropy inference, and of mean-field variational methods.

Worked mini-example: projecting onto a 2-D Gaussian submanifold

Take the family of unit-variance bivariate Gaussians $N(\eta, I_2)$, coordinatized by the mean $\eta=(\eta_1,\eta_2)$. Here KL is exactly a squared Euclidean distance, $\mathrm{KL}(N(a)\,\|\,N(b))=\tfrac12\|a-b\|^2$, so the Fisher metric is the flat $I_2$ and the dual connections coincide with ordinary lines. Fix an e-flat submanifold $S$ (a line of means) and a point $p$ off it. Let $q$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $p$ to $S$ — the information (m-)projection — and let $r$ be any other point of $S$. The leg $p\to q$ is perpendicular to the leg $q\to r$ along $S$, so the right triangle gives, exactly, $\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,r)=\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)+\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,r)$ — literally the Pythagorean theorem.

Connections

Information geometry sits at the meeting point of statistics, differential geometry, and machine learning. It is far from closed: the geometry of singular models (neural networks, mixtures) where the Fisher metric degenerates is the subject of Watanabe's singular learning theory, and the right geometry for non-parametric and quantum statistical models is still being worked out. What surprises a beginner is that the “obvious” Euclidean distance between parameters is almost never the right one — the Fisher metric, forced on us by invariance, is.

  • Riemannian geometry — Riemannian metrics — the Fisher metric makes a statistical model a Riemannian manifold, so geodesics, curvature, and volume all carry over.
  • Information theory — Fisher information & Cramér–Rao — the same matrix $G$ that is our metric is the estimation-theoretic information bound $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\succeq G^{-1}$.
  • Information theory — KL divergence — the global divergence whose infinitesimal form is the Fisher metric and whose Bregman identity gives the Pythagorean theorem.
  • Mathematical statistics — maximum likelihood and Cramér–Rao — natural-gradient ascent is Fisher scoring, and asymptotic efficiency of the MLE is the statement that its covariance attains $G^{-1}$.
  • Convex optimization — convex functions & the Legendre transform — the convex log-partition $\psi$ and its Legendre dual $\psi^*$ are exactly the dual coordinates of an exponential family.
  • Deep learning theory — non-convex optimization — natural-gradient and its approximations (K-FAC) are how the Fisher geometry enters the training of large models.
