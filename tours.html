1. The road to BSD

Goal: understand why a Clay-Millennium prize is offered for a single equality between an analytic order of vanishing and an algebraic rank.

  1. Algebraic geometry Elliptic curves and the chord–tangent group law

    Start where every BSD discussion starts: a smooth Weierstrass cubic $E\colon y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, with the marvellous fact that its rational points form an abelian group under chord–tangent addition. The identity is the point at infinity; the inverse of $(x,y)$ is $(x,-y)$; and the sum of two points $P, Q$ is the third intersection of $\overline{PQ}$ with $E$, reflected through the $x$-axis. Mordell's theorem says $E(\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus T$ with $T$ a finite torsion subgroup. The integer $r$ — the rank — is the algebraic side of BSD, and it has no known algorithm: producing a rank-$28$ generator is a hand-tuned hunt. To even formulate the conjecture we need an analytic shadow that we can compute: counting points modulo each prime $p$ and packaging the counts into a generating function.

  2. Modular forms & L-functions Modular forms on the upper half-plane

    Before we can build the L-function of $E$, we need to meet the other side of the modularity coin: holomorphic functions on $\mathbb{H} = \{\tau : \Im\tau > 0\}$ that transform predictably under $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ (or a congruence subgroup like $\Gamma_0(N)$) and are bounded at the cusps. A weight-$2k$ modular form $f$ satisfies $f\bigl(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\bigr) = (c\tau+d)^{2k} f(\tau)$. The Fourier expansion $f(\tau) = \sum a_n q^n$ with $q = e^{2\pi i\tau}$ packages each form into an arithmetic sequence $(a_n)$. The space of forms of given weight and level is finite-dimensional and effectively computable — that's the miracle. That arithmetic sequence is what will eventually meet our point counts on $E$, but first we need to make sense of what an $L$-function is as an object in its own right.

  3. Modular forms & L-functions L-functions: $L(E,s)$ and $L(f,s)$

    An $L$-function is a Dirichlet series $\sum a_n n^{-s}$ with an Euler product $\prod_p L_p(s)^{-1}$ over primes — local data glued into a single complex-analytic object. For an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{Q}$ with good reduction at $p$, the local factor is $L_p(s) = 1 - a_p(E) p^{-s} + p^{1-2s}$ where $a_p(E) = p + 1 - \#E(\mathbb{F}_p)$. For a Hecke eigenform $f$, the local factor is $L_p(s) = 1 - a_p(f) p^{-s} + p^{2k-1-2s}$. Two completely different definitions, two completely different worlds. The Hasse–Weil conjecture predicts that $L(E,s)$ analytically continues to all of $\mathbb{C}$ and satisfies a functional equation $s \leftrightarrow 2 - s$; the only way we know how to prove that for a general $E$ is to identify $L(E,s) = L(f,s)$ for some weight-$2$ newform $f$. That identification is the content of the next stop.

  4. Modular forms & L-functions Modularity: every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ is a modular form

    The Modularity Theorem (Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor) says exactly that: for every $E/\mathbb{Q}$ there is a weight-$2$ newform $f$ of level $N = $ conductor of $E$ such that $a_p(E) = a_p(f)$ for all primes. The conductor records the bad reduction of $E$ — small primes where the Weierstrass model goes singular — and it determines the level of $f$. Modularity is what gives $L(E,s)$ its analytic continuation and functional equation: both are inherited from the modular world, where the Mellin transform $L(f,s) = (2\pi)^s \Gamma(s)^{-1} \int_0^\infty f(it) t^{s-1}\,dt$ makes them automatic from the transformation law of $f$. With analytic continuation in hand, the order of vanishing $\mathrm{ord}_{s=1} L(E,s)$ is now a well-defined non-negative integer, called the analytic rank. Two integers, both attached to $E$, both meaningful — and the conjecture is that they're the same one.

  5. Modular forms & L-functions · capstone Birch & Swinnerton-Dyer: analytic rank = algebraic rank

    The conjecture: $\mathrm{ord}_{s=1} L(E,s) = \mathrm{rank}\, E(\mathbb{Q})$. Equality of two integers, one defined by a Taylor expansion of a complex-analytic function and the other by counting independent rational points on a cubic curve. The refined version pins the leading Taylor coefficient to a product of period $\Omega_E$, regulator $R$, Tamagawa numbers $c_p$, torsion order $|E(\mathbb{Q})_{\mathrm{tors}}|^2$, and the order of the Tate–Shafarevich group $\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})$ — five disparate quantities, four of them computable, $\Sha$ conjecturally finite. Known in rank $0$ and $1$ (Gross–Zagier–Kolyvagin); wide open in higher rank. Every step of this tour was a layer of scaffolding that even states the conjecture rigorously: without modularity, $L(E,s)$ has no value at $s=1$ to take an order of vanishing of.

2. How Fermat's Last Theorem was proved

Goal: trace the chain Frey curve → Ribet's level-lowering → Wiles's modularity → contradiction, the second-most-quoted proof in modern mathematics.

  1. Number theory Galois theory and solvable extensions

    The classical setting: a polynomial $f \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ has a splitting field $L \supset \mathbb{Q}$, the Galois group $\Gal(L/\mathbb{Q})$ acts faithfully on the roots, and "solvable by radicals" translates to a structural condition — solvability of the group. FLT — does $a^p + b^p = c^p$ have nonzero integer solutions for prime $p \geq 3$? — looks like it should belong here, and for $300$ years people tried to settle it with cyclotomic Galois theory (Kummer's regular primes, Iwasawa towers, the Vandiver conjecture). The strategy that finally worked is wildly different: instead of factoring $a^p + b^p$ in a cyclotomic ring of integers, attach a geometric object — an elliptic curve — to a hypothetical solution and study the Galois representations coming from it. Galois theory survives, but it acts on étale cohomology rather than on roots of polynomials.

  2. Modular forms & L-functions Modular forms and newforms of level $N$

    We need to know what we're hunting for. A weight-$2$ newform of level $N$ is a cusp form $f\colon \mathbb{H} \to \mathbb{C}$ transforming under $\Gamma_0(N) \subset \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ that doesn't come from a lower level $N' \mid N$ — a Hecke eigenform that's "primitive" at $N$. The space $S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ of weight-$2$ cusp forms is finite-dimensional with dimension given by an explicit formula in terms of the genus of the modular curve $X_0(N)$ and the number of elliptic and cusp points. In particular $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(2)) = 0$ — there are no nonzero cusp forms at all of level $2$, weight $2$. Hold onto that fact: it will be the wall the entire FLT argument crashes the Frey curve into. The strategy is to derive a newform of level $2$ from a hypothetical solution and let dimension zero do the work.

  3. Modular forms & L-functions Modular curves $X_0(N)$ as moduli of elliptic curves

    Why do modular forms know about elliptic curves? Because $X_0(N) = \overline{\Gamma_0(N) \backslash \mathbb{H}}$ (compactified by adjoining cusps) is the moduli space of pairs $(E, C)$ with $E$ an elliptic curve and $C \subset E$ a cyclic subgroup of order $N$. Every elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{Q}$ of conductor $N$ corresponds to a $\mathbb{Q}$-point on $X_0(N)$, and weight-$2$ cusp forms are exactly holomorphic differentials on $X_0(N)$ — i.e. global sections of $\Omega^1_{X_0(N)}$. The Eichler–Shimura relations then attach to each Hecke eigenform $f$ an abelian variety quotient $A_f$ of the Jacobian $J_0(N) = \mathrm{Pic}^0(X_0(N))$, carrying a compatible system of $\ell$-adic Galois representations. Now the bridge is built: a modular form has a Galois representation, and a (suitable) elliptic curve has a Galois rep on its $\ell$-adic Tate module. Modularity says these two representations are the same.

  4. Number theory Galois representations from the Tate module

    Given $E/\mathbb{Q}$ and a prime $\ell$, the $\ell$-adic Tate module $T_\ell E = \varprojlim E[\ell^n]$ carries a continuous action of $\Gal(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/\mathbb{Q})$, giving a representation $\rho_{E,\ell}\colon G_\mathbb{Q} \to \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)$. Reducing mod $\ell$ gives a residual representation $\bar\rho$ valued in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{F}_\ell)$. Now Frey's idea, sharpened by Ribet: if $a^p + b^p = c^p$ with $p \geq 5$, the curve $E_{a,b,c}\colon y^2 = x(x-a^p)(x+b^p)$ has a residual representation $\bar\rho$ at $\ell = p$ which is unramified outside $2$ and $p$, finite at $p$, and absolutely irreducible. Ribet's level-lowering theorem then says: if $\bar\rho$ is modular of any level, it must be modular of level $2$.

  5. Modular forms & L-functions · capstone Ribet + Wiles ⇒ FLT

    Wiles (with Taylor) proves: every semistable elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ is modular, by way of the now-famous deformation-theoretic identity $R_\Sigma = T_\Sigma$ between a universal deformation ring and a Hecke algebra. The Frey curve $E_{a,b,c}$ is semistable when $p \geq 5$, so its associated $\bar\rho$ is modular by Wiles. Ribet says any modular $\bar\rho$ with the ramification properties of $\bar\rho_{E_{a,b,c}}$ must come from a level-$2$ newform. But $\dim S_2(\Gamma_0(2)) = 0$. Contradiction — so no Frey curve, hence no nontrivial solution to $a^p + b^p = c^p$ for $p \geq 5$. (The cases $p = 3, 4$ are classical Fermat–Euler.) The proof reduces a Diophantine question to an existence statement about modular forms of a particular level, then exhibits a vector space whose dimension you can just compute and find to be zero.

3. What is cohomology?

Goal: see "cohomology" as one idea — the systematic measurement of obstructions — wearing five very different costumes.

  1. Algebra & homological Group cohomology and $H^2$ as central extensions

    The cleanest first sighting of cohomology. Given a group $G$ and a $G$-module $M$, the bar cochain complex $C^n(G, M) = \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathsf{Set}}(G^n, M)$ with differential $(d\varphi)(g_0, \ldots, g_n) = g_0 \varphi(g_1, \ldots, g_n) - \varphi(g_0 g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_n) + \cdots$ has cohomology $H^n(G, M)$. The interpretations are concrete: $H^0(G,M) = M^G$ is invariants, $H^1$ classifies crossed homomorphisms (derivations) modulo principal ones — Galois-cohomologically, this is torsors for $M$. $H^2$ classifies central extensions $1 \to M \to \tilde G \to G \to 1$ up to equivalence. Cohomology measures things that almost split but don't. Hold onto that phrase — it survives every change of context in this tour, even when "split" turns into "is locally trivial" or "lifts to characteristic zero."

  2. Geometry & topology Singular homology and cohomology of a space

    Same idea, geometric incarnation: assign to a space $X$ the chain complex of singular simplices $C_n(X) = \mathbb{Z}\langle \sigma\colon \Delta^n \to X\rangle$ with boundary $\partial\sigma = \sum (-1)^i \sigma|_{\text{face}_i}$, and dualize to get cochains. $H^n(X; \mathbb{Z})$ is a topological invariant — homotopy-invariant, functorial in $X$, computable from a CW structure or a Mayer–Vietoris cover. Now the obstructions are topological: $H^1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ detects the loop that can't be contracted; $H^2(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ detects the sphere that can't be filled. Künneth, Poincaré duality, and the cup product $\smile\colon H^p \otimes H^q \to H^{p+q}$ turn this into a genuine graded-commutative ring, not just a graded abelian group. The same complex pattern $\partial^2 = 0$ as group cohomology, the same "obstruction" intuition, but the underlying combinatorics now records geometric content.

  3. Algebraic geometry Sheaf cohomology via Čech and derived functors

    For coherent sheaves on a scheme, "global sections" $H^0(X, \mathcal{F}) = \Gamma(X, \mathcal{F})$ is the friendly part; the obstruction to lifting local sections to global sections lives in the higher right-derived functors $H^i(X, \mathcal{F}) = R^i\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F})$. Concretely you compute by Čech cohomology over an affine cover $\mathfrak{U} = \{U_\alpha\}$: alternating sums on $\check{C}^p(\mathfrak{U}, \mathcal{F}) = \prod \mathcal{F}(U_{\alpha_0\cdots\alpha_p})$. This is where geometry meets homological algebra. The cohomology of line bundles on $\mathbb{P}^n$, computable from the Koszul complex, controls everything downstream — Riemann–Roch on a curve, Serre duality $H^i(X, \mathcal{F}) \cong H^{n-i}(X, \mathcal{F}^\vee \otimes \omega_X)^\vee$, the entire dictionary between projective varieties and graded modules. Same complex pattern, but the "module" is now a sheaf and the "group" is now an open cover.

  4. Algebraic geometry Algebraic de Rham cohomology and the Hodge filtration

    Replace the sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ by the de Rham complex $\Omega^\bullet_{X/k} = (\mathcal{O}_X \to \Omega^1_{X/k} \to \Omega^2_{X/k} \to \cdots)$ of algebraic Kähler differential forms with the exterior derivative $d$, and take hypercohomology: $H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X/k) = \mathbb{H}^*(X, \Omega^\bullet_{X/k})$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ this matches singular cohomology with $\mathbb{C}$ coefficients (Grothendieck's comparison theorem), but it's defined purely algebraically — no transcendental input, no choice of metric. The Hodge filtration $F^p H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}$ comes from the "stupid" filtration $\sigma^{\geq p}$ on $\Omega^\bullet$ and gives the Hodge-to-de Rham spectral sequence. For smooth projective $X$ over $\mathbb{C}$, this degenerates at $E_1$ and yields the Hodge decomposition $H^n(X, \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=n} H^q(X, \Omega^p)$. Now we have an algebraic invariant that recovers a topological one — and a finer structure besides.

  5. Algebraic geometry · capstone Étale cohomology and the Weil conjectures

    In characteristic $p$ there's no ambient $\mathbb{C}$ to compare to, and Zariski-open covers are too coarse to see torsion phenomena like $\ell$-th roots of unity (any constant sheaf $\underline{\mathbb{Z}/\ell}$ is acyclic on an irreducible variety). Grothendieck's solution: replace "open cover" by "étale cover" — a flat unramified cover, the algebraic analogue of a local homeomorphism — and define $H^i_{\mathrm{\acute{e}t}}(X, \mathbb{Z}_\ell)$ via the same derived-functor pattern on the new site. The payoff is enormous. Étale cohomology is a Weil cohomology theory for varieties over $\mathbb{F}_q$: a Lefschetz fixed-point formula computes $\#X(\mathbb{F}_{q^n})$ as alternating Frobenius traces, and Deligne's Riemann hypothesis pins the eigenvalues to absolute value $q^{i/2}$. Cohomology was the obstruction-counting machine the whole time; étale cohomology is what happens when you're allowed to count obstructions in a Galois-equivariant way.

4. From naive sets to ∞-topoi

Goal: walk from the membership relation $\in$ to the categorical universes that house all of derived algebraic geometry, in six honest steps.

  1. Logic & foundations Sets, functions, products, and equivalence relations

    Begin where every working mathematician begins: a set is a "collection of elements" and a function $f\colon A \to B$ is a rule sending each element of $A$ to one in $B$. Cartesian products $A \times B$, disjoint unions $A \sqcup B$, equivalence classes $A/\!\sim$, function spaces $B^A$ — the four constructions you actually use a hundred times a day. The catch: in this naive picture, the operations are all about elements, and the moment you ask "what's the universal property of a product?" — i.e., the moment you describe $A \times B$ as the unique object equipped with projections $\pi_A, \pi_B$ such that any pair $(f, g)$ factors uniquely through it — you've left set theory and entered category theory. The leap from "membership" to "universal property" is the entire conceptual jump this tour follows up.

  2. Algebra & homological Categories, functors, Yoneda

    A category is what you get when you keep track of arrows between things rather than elements within things. Sets and functions, groups and homomorphisms, topological spaces and continuous maps — all categories. A functor preserves the arrow structure; a natural transformation is an arrow between functors. The whole subject is the disciplined unfolding of these three definitions. Yoneda's lemma is the punchline: an object $X$ is completely determined by the functor $\Hom(-, X)$, i.e. by the pattern of arrows into it from every other object. The functor $X \mapsto \Hom(-,X)$ embeds $\mathcal{C}$ fully faithfully into the functor category $[\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathsf{Set}]$. This is the philosophical lever that makes the rest of the tour possible — every later "topos" will be a category of sheaves, i.e. a particular kind of functor category, and Yoneda is what makes that reduction bite.

  3. Higher categories & toposes Elementary toposes and the subobject classifier

    An elementary topos is a category that "behaves like $\mathsf{Set}$": finite limits, exponentials $B^A$ (right adjoint to $-\times A$), and a subobject classifier $\Omega$ playing the role of $\{\mathrm{true}, \mathrm{false}\}$ — i.e. an object with a universal monomorphism $\mathrm{true}\colon 1 \to \Omega$ such that every monomorphism is a unique pullback of it. The slogan: a topos is a place where you can do mathematics. Sheaves on a topological space form a topos $\mathrm{Sh}(X)$; the topos of $G$-sets $[\mathcal{B}G, \mathsf{Set}]$ is another; the effective topos encodes computability. The internal logic is intuitionistic — excluded middle is not free, since $\Omega$ has more truth values than $\{0,1\}$ — and that's a feature: it lets you reason about variation as if it were membership. The forced-set-theoretic models of Cohen, viewed correctly, are toposes too.

  4. Higher categories & toposes Simplicial sets and the nerve of a category

    To go higher we need a combinatorial model of "category up to coherent homotopy." Simplicial sets are presheaves on the simplex category $\Delta$ (objects $[n] = \{0 < 1 < \cdots < n\}$, morphisms order-preserving maps) — combinatorial gadgets that simultaneously model topological spaces (via geometric realization $|\cdot|\colon \mathsf{sSet} \to \mathsf{Top}$, a Quillen equivalence) and small categories (via the nerve $N(\mathcal{C})$). The point: $N(\mathcal{C})_n$ is the set of length-$n$ composable chains of arrows in $\mathcal{C}$; degeneracies insert identities; faces compose adjacent arrows or drop endpoints. So a category is a particular kind of simplicial set — one in which every "inner horn" $\Lambda^n_k$ ($0 < k < n$) admits a unique filler. Relax "unique" into "exists" and you get the next stop.

  5. Higher categories & toposes $\infty$-categories as quasi-categories

    A quasi-category is a simplicial set in which every inner horn $\Lambda^n_k$ ($0 < k < n$) admits a filler — but not necessarily a unique one. The fillers play the role of "compositions defined up to coherent homotopy" — there's no chosen composite of $f$ and $g$, just a contractible space of choices. This is enough to do all of category theory: Joyal and Lurie show that limits, colimits, adjunctions, Kan extensions, and the Yoneda lemma all generalize. The whole point is that "homotopy types" — the $\infty$-groupoid version of sets — replace "sets" as the basic kind of mapping space. What was $\Hom(X,Y) \in \mathsf{Set}$ becomes $\mathrm{Map}(X,Y) \in \mathcal{S}$, the $\infty$-category of spaces. Mathematics with mapping spaces in $\mathcal{S}$ is called higher category theory.

  6. Higher categories & toposes · capstone $\infty$-topoi and Lurie's higher topos theory

    An $\infty$-topos is an accessible left-exact localization of the presheaf $\infty$-category $\mathcal{P}(C) = \mathrm{Fun}(C^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathcal{S})$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the $\infty$-category of spaces. The $\infty$-Giraud axioms characterize them intrinsically: small colimits exist, are universal, sums are disjoint, and groupoid objects are effective. Their internal logic is homotopy type theory (Voevodsky's univalence axiom is the topos-theoretic statement that equivalence equals identity in the universe). Their geometric examples — the étale, pro-étale, fppf $\infty$-topoi of a scheme — are the natural home of derived algebraic geometry, where descent for sheaves of spectra is automatic and cohomology is just hom. We started by asking what a set was; we end with the universe in which every modern descent and gluing statement becomes a one-line triviality.

5. From sound to wavelets

Goal: understand why decomposing a signal into frequencies is sometimes the wrong thing to do, and what to use instead.

  1. Analysis Fourier series and the Fourier transform

    A periodic signal is a sum of sines and cosines (Fourier series); an aperiodic signal on $\mathbb{R}$ is an integral over a continuum of frequencies. The Fourier transform $\hat f(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}\,dx$ exchanges convolution $f * g$ for multiplication $\hat f \cdot \hat g$, derivative $\partial_j$ for multiplication by $2\pi i \xi_j$, translation for modulation, and the Gaussian for the Gaussian. It's the right basis for any system whose dynamics commute with translation — the heat equation, the wave equation, the Laplacian on $\mathbb{R}^n$, anything diagonalized by plane waves $e^{2\pi i x\cdot\xi}$. Two limitations show up immediately: Fourier doesn't see where a feature lives in time (only its frequency content), and many functions of physical interest — $\delta$, the constant $1$, $H(x)$ — aren't even integrable. The next stop fixes the second problem.

  2. Analysis Distributions and weak derivatives

    Fourier needs a function class roomy enough to hold $\delta$, the constant function $1$, plane waves $e^{ix\cdot\xi}$, and step functions — none of which are classically integrable. Schwartz's distributions $\mathcal{D}'(\Omega)$ are the right abstraction: continuous linear functionals on test functions $C^\infty_c(\Omega)$. Differentiation extends to all of $\mathcal{D}'$ by transposition: $\langle \partial_j u, \varphi\rangle = -\langle u, \partial_j\varphi\rangle$. The tempered distributions $\mathcal{S}'(\mathbb{R}^n)$ are the dual of Schwartz space and form the natural domain for the Fourier transform. Sobolev spaces $H^s(\mathbb{R}^n)$ then arise as the natural domains for elliptic operators: $H^s = \{u \in \mathcal{S}' : (1+|\xi|^2)^{s/2}\hat u \in L^2\}$, the Fourier-side definition that makes derivatives just look like multiplication. Every estimate in PDE is at heart a statement about which Sobolev space some operator maps to which.

  3. Analysis Spectral theory of self-adjoint operators

    Why is Fourier the "right basis" for the Laplacian? Because $-\Delta$ is self-adjoint on a suitable domain in $L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$, and the spectral theorem says every self-adjoint operator on a Hilbert space is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator $M_\varphi$ on $L^2$ of some measure space — i.e. it has a coordinate system in which it's just "multiply by the real-valued function $\varphi$." Fourier is exactly that coordinate system for $-\Delta$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, with $\varphi(\xi) = 4\pi^2|\xi|^2$. For a compact self-adjoint operator like the inverse Laplacian on a bounded domain, the spectrum is discrete and we get an honest orthonormal eigenbasis. The spectral theorem reframes "diagonalize an operator" as a universal procedure, not a clever trick — and it tells us when a smarter coordinate system might work better than Fourier.

  4. Analysis Wavelets and multiresolution analysis

    For signals with sharp edges or transients — images, speech, financial time series — Fourier is wasteful: a localized feature spreads across all frequencies, since a delta has constant transform. A wavelet basis is built from a single mother wavelet $\psi$ via dyadic dilations and integer translations $\psi_{j,k}(x) = 2^{j/2}\psi(2^j x - k)$. Each basis element is localized in both time and frequency, at a resolution that scales with $j$. Multiresolution analysis builds these from a nested family $V_0 \subset V_1 \subset V_2 \subset \cdots$ of approximation spaces with $V_{j+1} = V_j \oplus W_j$. The Haar wavelet is the simplest example; Daubechies's compactly-supported orthonormal wavelets came later and changed signal processing — JPEG 2000 is a wavelet codec. Same Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, smarter basis, chosen to match the actual structure of the data.

6. The local–global principle

Goal: see why "$x \in \mathbb{Q}$" is best understood as "$x \in \mathbb{Q}_p$ for every $p$, and $x \in \mathbb{R}$, simultaneously."

  1. Number theory Number fields, rings of integers, Dedekind domains

    The starting object: a number field $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ — a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ — and its ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$, the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$. Unique factorization can fail in $\mathcal{O}_K$ (the famous example: $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$) — but it always succeeds at the level of ideals, which factor uniquely into prime ideals. That's the content of "$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a Dedekind domain." The class group $\mathrm{Cl}(K) = $ ideals modulo principal ideals measures the failure of element-level unique factorization; Minkowski's geometry-of-numbers bound proves $|\mathrm{Cl}(K)| < \infty$. We have global arithmetic — but most concrete questions ("does $x^3 - 5y^3 = 7$ have a solution in $\mathbb{Q}$?") are still hopeless from this height. We need to localize.

  2. Number theory $p$-adic numbers and Hensel's lemma

    Localize. The completion $\mathbb{Q}_p$ at each prime $p$ — built from $\mathbb{Q}$ the way $\mathbb{R}$ is built, but with the absolute value $|p^k(a/b)|_p = p^{-k}$ in place of the usual one — is a complete field with its own non-archimedean topology. Inside it sits the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$, the closed unit ball. Hensel's lemma turns approximate solutions into exact ones the same way Newton's method does over $\mathbb{R}$: if $f(\bar a) \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ and $f'(\bar a) \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}$, then $\bar a$ lifts uniquely to a root in $\mathbb{Z}_p$. So solving an equation in $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is genuinely easier than in $\mathbb{Q}$. Hasse's local–global principle says: for a quadratic form $Q$, the equation $Q = 0$ has a nontrivial solution in $\mathbb{Q}$ iff it has one in $\mathbb{R}$ and in every $\mathbb{Q}_p$. The principle fails for cubics and higher, but the failures themselves become arithmetic invariants — the Tate–Shafarevich group $\Sha(E/\mathbb{Q})$ is precisely the failure for elliptic curves.

  3. Number theory Adèles and idèles: all places at once

    Rather than juggle each $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and $\mathbb{R}$ separately, package them into the adèle ring $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{R} \times \prod_p' \mathbb{Q}_p$, the restricted product in which almost every component sits in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ (else the product wouldn't be locally compact). The diagonal embedding $\mathbb{Q} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q}$ is discrete with compact quotient $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Q}$ — the structural fact, an analogue of $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$ being discrete with compact quotient $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$. This is what powers Tate's thesis: integration over $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q}$ gives a single zeta integral whose decomposition place-by-place reproduces the Euler product. The idèles $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q}^\times$ are the multiplicative version; their quotient $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q}^\times/\mathbb{Q}^\times$ is the idèle class group, and its continuous characters are exactly Hecke characters — the right linear coefficients for $L$-functions of arithmetic type.

  4. Number theory Frobenius elements and Chebotarev density

    For a Galois extension $L/K$ of number fields, every unramified prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of $K$ determines a Frobenius conjugacy class $\mathrm{Frob}_\mathfrak{p} \subset \Gal(L/K)$ — the local data at $\mathfrak{p}$, defined as the lift of $x \mapsto x^{N\mathfrak{p}}$ on the residue field. Chebotarev's density theorem says these classes equidistribute over $\Gal(L/K)$ as $\mathfrak{p}$ varies, with density equal to the relative size of each conjugacy class. In particular every conjugacy class is hit infinitely often, and the union of all $\mathrm{Frob}_\mathfrak{p}$ generates the Galois group topologically. The global Galois group is reconstructed from its local pieces — a non-abelian, statistical version of the local–global principle. Reciprocity laws (quadratic, Artin, modular) are all statements about how the conjugacy class $\mathrm{Frob}_\mathfrak{p}$ is predictable from local data — which residues $\mathfrak{p}$ falls into, modulo something computable.

  5. Number theory · capstone Heights, Northcott, and the canonical height

    A height function $h\colon \mathbb{P}^n(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}) \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ measures the arithmetic complexity of a point — built place-by-place from local contributions and then summed. The naive height on $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})$ is $h(a/b) = \log\max(|a|, |b|)$ for coprime integers $a,b$; the Weil height generalizes to algebraic points by averaging over Galois conjugates. Northcott's finiteness theorem says there are only finitely many points of bounded height and bounded degree — the structural result that makes ranks and Mordell–Weil generators effective in principle. The canonical height $\hat h$ on an elliptic curve is the unique quadratic refinement of the Weil height that makes the group law isometric, $\hat h(nP) = n^2 \hat h(P)$, and it's the regulator quantity that appears in BSD. Each place — every $\mathbb{Q}_p$, $\mathbb{R}$, every closed point of an arithmetic scheme — contributes one term, and the global statement is the sum: the local–global principle, fully grown into Arakelov geometry.
