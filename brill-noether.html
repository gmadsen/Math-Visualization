Brill–Noether theory

When does a smooth projective curve of genus $g$ admit a non-degenerate map to $\mathbb{P}^r$ of degree $d$? Riemann–Roch gives a lower bound on $h^0$; the Brill–Noether number $\rho(g,r,d)=g-(r+1)(g-d+r)$ predicts the expected dimension of the variety $W^r_d(C)$ of such linear series. The Brill–Noether theorem (Kempf, Kleiman–Laksov) says $W^r_d$ is non-empty of dimension $\rho$ on every curve when $\rho\ge 0$; Petri's theorem says the locus is smooth — and the prediction sharp — on a general curve. Together they describe how special divisors are arranged on a general curve, and how the geometry of $\mathcal{M}_g$ stratifies by the existence of unusual linear systems.

1. Special divisors and linear series

Let $C$ be a smooth projective curve of genus $g$ over an algebraically closed field $k$. A divisor $D=\sum n_p\,[p]$ is a finite formal $\mathbb{Z}$-sum of points; its degree is $\deg D=\sum n_p$. The complete linear series attached to a divisor is the projective space

$|D|\;=\;\mathbb{P}(H^0(C,\mathcal{O}_C(D))),$

parametrizing effective divisors $D'\ge 0$ linearly equivalent to $D$. Equivalently, a section $s\in H^0(C,\mathcal{O}(D))$ has a zero locus $\mathrm{div}(s)+D\sim D$; the choice of $s$ up to scalar is the choice of an effective representative.

A $g^r_d$ on $C$ is a pair $(L,V)$ where $L$ is a line bundle of degree $d$ and $V\subset H^0(C,L)$ is an $(r+1)$-dimensional subspace. When $V=H^0(C,L)$ it is complete; in general it is a sublinear series. A $g^r_d$ defines a rational map $C\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^r=\mathbb{P}(V^*)$; the map is a morphism whenever $V$ has no base points. The question "does $C$ embed in $\mathbb{P}^r$ as a degree-$d$ curve" is the question "does $C$ carry a very ample $g^r_d$".

Special vs. non-special

A divisor $D$ is special if $h^1(D):=\dim H^1(C,\mathcal{O}(D))>0$, equivalently (Serre duality) if $h^0(K-D)>0$ where $K$ is the canonical divisor of degree $2g-2$. By Riemann–Roch (next section), $h^0(D)-h^1(D)=d-g+1$, so when $D$ is non-special, $h^0(D)=d-g+1$ is determined by $d$ and $g$ alone. Specialness — extra sections beyond what Riemann–Roch forces — is exactly the phenomenon Brill–Noether theory measures.

2. Riemann–Roch as the dimension-counting tool

The single most important constraint on $|D|$ is the Riemann–Roch formula. For any divisor $D$ on a smooth projective curve $C$ of genus $g$, with canonical divisor $K$ of degree $2g-2$:

$h^0(C,\mathcal{O}(D)) - h^0(C,\mathcal{O}(K-D))\;=\;\deg D - g + 1.$

Equivalently $h^0(D)-h^1(D)=d-g+1$ via Serre duality $h^1(D)=h^0(K-D)$. The formula is the engine: it converts the abstract question "how many sections does $\mathcal{O}(D)$ have?" into a concrete count, modulo the correction term $h^0(K-D)$, which vanishes once $D$ is sufficiently positive.

Brill–Noether theory lives entirely in the special range: when $D$ is special, $h^1(D)>0$ can be anything from $1$ to $g$, and the question of how special $D$ can be — how large $h^0$ can grow above $d-g+1$ — is precisely the question of the existence of a $g^r_d$ with $r$ above the Riemann–Roch baseline.

Serre duality $H^1(C,\mathcal{O}(D))\cong H^0(C,\Omega^1_C(-D))^*$ gives a clean reading of $h^1$: an element of $H^1$ is a meromorphic 1-form with prescribed poles. So $h^1(D)$ counts holomorphic differentials on $C$ that vanish along $D$; equivalently, hyperplanes in $\mathbb{P}^{g-1}$ (the canonical embedding) that contain the image of $D$. This geometrical reading is the bridge to the Petri map (section 6).

3. The Brill–Noether number $\rho(g,r,d)$

The expected dimension of the locus of $g^r_d$'s on a smooth projective curve of genus $g$ is the Brill–Noether number

$\rho(g,r,d)\;=\;g\;-\;(r+1)(g-d+r).$

The derivation is a parameter count. Working on the Jacobian $J(C)=\Pic^d(C)$, line bundles of degree $d$ form a $g$-dimensional family. The condition "$h^0(L)\ge r+1$" is the rank-condition that an evaluation map of $L$ have a kernel of dimension $\ge r+1$. By Porteous-type expected codimension, the locus where a generic linear map between bundles of ranks $a\le b$ has kernel of dimension $\ge a-c$ has codimension $c(b-c)$ inside the parameter space. Applied here: $h^0(L)\ge r+1$ is codimension $(r+1)(g-d+r)$ (the "Brill–Noether codimension"). Subtracting from $g=\dim\Pic^d$ gives $\rho$.

4. $W^r_d(C)$ — the Brill–Noether locus

For a fixed smooth projective curve $C$, the Brill–Noether locus $W^r_d(C)\subseteq\Pic^d(C)$ is the closed subset parametrizing line bundles with at least $r+1$ sections:

$W^r_d(C)\;=\;\{\,L\in\Pic^d(C)\;:\;h^0(C,L)\ge r+1\,\}.$

It has a natural scheme structure as a determinantal variety. The Abel–Jacobi map $C^{(d)}\to\Pic^d(C)$ from the $d$-th symmetric product factors through $W^r_d$ in the right way; concretely, one realizes $W^r_d$ as the locus where the rank of a universal evaluation map of bundles drops. Choose a Poincaré bundle on $C\times\Pic^d(C)$ and form, for a sufficiently general effective divisor $E$ on $C$,

$\gamma\colon\;\mathcal{F}\;\longrightarrow\;\mathcal{G}$

a map of vector bundles on $\Pic^d(C)$ whose kernel rank at $L$ jumps by $h^0(L)$. Then $W^r_d(C)$ is the locus where $\operatorname{rk}\gamma\le \operatorname{rk}\mathcal{F}-(r+1)$. The codimension of such a determinantal locus, by Macaulay/Porteous, has expected value $(r+1)(g-d+r)$ — which is exactly $g-\rho$.

The Brill–Noether loci form a chain $\Pic^d(C)=W^{-1}\supseteq W^0_d\supseteq W^1_d\supseteq W^2_d\supseteq\cdots$. Each inclusion is a determinantal cut by one extra rank condition; the expected codimension increases by $g-d+r$ at each step. When $\rho(g,r,d)\ge 0$, the locus is non-empty of expected dimension; when negative, it's empty (generic curve).

Scheme structure matters. $W^r_d(C)$ is not just a set; it's a determinantal scheme, and the "expected dimension" $\rho$ is the dimension of the determinantal locus when the rank conditions are imposed generically. The Brill–Noether and Petri theorems are precisely the statements that this expected scheme structure is the actual scheme structure on a general curve.

5. The Brill–Noether theorem (existence + dimension)

The classical existence statement was conjectured by Brill and Noether in the late 19th century and proved rigorously a century later:

Brill–Noether theorem (Kempf 1971, Kleiman–Laksov 1972, 1974). Let $C$ be any smooth projective curve of genus $g$. If $\rho(g,r,d)\ge 0$, then $W^r_d(C)$ is non-empty, and every irreducible component has dimension $\ge\rho(g,r,d)$.

The non-emptiness half is unconditional — every curve, not just a generic one. The proof is a Schubert-calculus / Chern-class computation on a projective bundle. Build the Picard variety $\Pic^d(C)$ and a Poincaré bundle $\mathcal{P}$, push it forward to $\Pic^d$ to get sheaves $\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{G}$ whose kernel-rank-jumping locus is $W^r_d$. The class of $W^r_d$ in cohomology of $\Pic^d$ is a Schubert-style polynomial in the universal Chern classes, and its non-vanishing for $\rho\ge 0$ is a direct combinatorial check on Young diagrams.

For the second half — dimension equals $\rho$ on a general curve — one needs:

Existence + sharp dimension (Griffiths–Harris 1980). On a general smooth projective curve of genus $g$, $\dim W^r_d(C)=\rho(g,r,d)$ whenever $\rho\ge 0$ — the determinantal upper bound on codimension is sharp.

"General" means: in a Zariski-open dense subset of $\mathcal{M}_g$. The non-general curves where $W^r_d$ has dimension above $\rho$ are special — they live in proper subvarieties of $\mathcal{M}_g$. The hyperelliptic curves $\mathcal{M}^1_{g,2}\subset\mathcal{M}_g$ are the simplest example.

A miniature of the Kempf / Kleiman–Laksov proof: $\Pic^d(C)$ carries a universal Poincaré bundle $\mathcal{P}$; pushing it forward along the second projection $C\times\Pic^d\to\Pic^d$ produces locally free sheaves whose evaluation map has rank-drop locus = $W^r_d$. The Chern class of $W^r_d$ is the determinant of a matrix of universal classes — a Schubert-type polynomial. Non-vanishing for $\rho\ge 0$ is a combinatorial check.

Why this is hard. The non-vanishing of $[W^r_d]$ in cohomology is "obvious by Schubert calculus" — once you know it's the right polynomial to compute. The subtle part is making this rigorous: setting up the universal Poincaré bundle, the pushforward sheaves, and the determinantal scheme structure carefully enough that the Chern-class computation actually measures geometry, not just an abstract cohomology number. This is the work of Kempf 1971 (algebraic-cycle argument), Kleiman–Laksov 1972 (Schubert), and a stream of refinements.

6. Petri's theorem (smoothness)

Existence is the easy half. The hard half is smoothness: on a general curve, $W^r_d(C)$ is not merely non-empty of expected dimension — it's smooth as a scheme at every point of the open stratum. The obstruction is the Petri map.

Fix a line bundle $L\in\Pic^d(C)$ of degree $d$ with $h^0(L)=r+1$. The Petri map is the cup-product

$\mu_0(L)\colon\;H^0(C,L)\otimes H^0(C,K\otimes L^{-1})\;\longrightarrow\;H^0(C,K),$

multiplying a section of $L$ by a section of $K\otimes L^{-1}$ to get a holomorphic differential. The Zariski tangent space to $W^r_d$ at $L$ is the dual of $\operatorname{coker}\mu_0(L)$. So $W^r_d$ has the expected dimension $\rho$ at $L$ iff $\mu_0(L)$ has full rank — iff $\mu_0(L)$ is injective.

Petri's theorem (Gieseker 1982, Eisenbud–Harris 1986). On a general smooth projective curve $C$ of genus $g$, for every line bundle $L\in W^r_d(C)\setminus W^{r+1}_d(C)$, the Petri map $\mu_0(L)$ is injective. Consequently, $W^r_d(C)$ is smooth of dimension exactly $\rho(g,r,d)$ along its open stratum.

The statement was conjectured by Petri (1923, in his Pisa thesis under Severi) and proved by Gieseker via degeneration to a flag curve, then re-proved more cleanly by Eisenbud–Harris using limit linear series (next section). The injectivity is open and closed in the smooth locus of $\mathcal{M}_g$: it's a Zariski-open property (the rank of $\mu_0$ is upper-semicontinuous), and it holds for at least one curve (a chain of elliptic curves) — so it holds on a Zariski-open dense set.

Tangent space: $T_L W^r_d=\operatorname{coker}(\mu_0)^*$, of dimension $g-\operatorname{rk}\mu_0$. Petri injectivity means $\operatorname{rk}\mu_0$ is maximal $=(r+1)(g-d+r)$, so $\dim T_L W^r_d=g-(r+1)(g-d+r)=\rho$ — the expected dimension. Failure of injectivity at $L$ means $W^r_d$ has an obstruction at $L$; on a non-general curve, failures live on a closed subset.

Petri's geometric reading. An element of $H^0(L)\otimes H^0(K\otimes L^{-1})$ that maps to zero under $\mu_0$ is a tensor $\sum s_i\otimes t_i$ with $\sum s_i\,t_i=0$ in $H^0(K)$ — a non-trivial linear relation among the products of the two factor systems. Geometrically, the canonical map $C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{g-1}$ has a quadric containing it that splits as a sum of products of an $|L|$-element and a $|K-L|$-element. Petri injectivity says no such "split" quadric exists on a general $C$ — the canonical image is not on a sum-of-two-lines quadric for any divisor splitting.

7. Brill–Noether loci in $\mathcal{M}_g$

Brill–Noether theory is about a fixed curve $C$. But it has a second life: as one varies $C$ over the moduli space $\mathcal{M}_g$, the loci where $W^r_d(C)$ is bigger than expected — where the Petri map degenerates — cut out interesting subvarieties of $\mathcal{M}_g$.

When $\rho(g,r,d)=-1$, $W^r_d(C)$ is generically empty but the locus

$\mathcal{M}^r_{g,d}\;=\;\{\,[C]\in\mathcal{M}_g\;:\;W^r_d(C)\ne\emptyset\,\}$

is a divisor in $\mathcal{M}_g$ — the Brill–Noether divisor. Its class in $\Pic(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g)\otimes\mathbb{Q}$ is computable in terms of the standard generators $\lambda$ (Hodge class) and $\delta_i$ (boundary classes). Eisenbud–Harris (1987) gave the formula explicitly using their machinery of limit linear series, computing these divisor classes for every $g,r,d$ with $\rho=-1$.

This is the bridge between Brill–Noether theory and the birational geometry of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$: the Brill–Noether divisors are exactly the divisors needed to compute the canonical class of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$. The Harris–Mumford theorem on the Kodaira dimension of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$ uses Brill–Noether divisors as the central technical input.

Limit linear series

The Eisenbud–Harris construction handles degenerate (nodal) curves at the boundary of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$. A limit linear series on a chain of components $C_0\cup C_1\cup\cdots\cup C_n$ is a coordinated choice of linear series on each component, with vanishing-order conditions at the nodes that match up. The total "degree" and "rank" of a limit linear series can be specified, and the limit is the right notion of a $g^r_d$ on a stable curve.

A chain of $g$ elliptic curves $E_1\cup E_2\cup\cdots\cup E_g$ is a "flag curve" in $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$. A limit $g^r_d$ assigns to each $E_i$ a $g^r_d$-like datum (a line bundle of degree $d$ and an $(r+1)$-dim subspace of sections) with compatibility at each node: the vanishing sequences must add correctly. Eisenbud–Harris showed limit linear series on flag curves smooth out to $g^r_d$'s on a nearby smooth curve, and this is the engine for proving Petri injectivity (by degeneration) and computing $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$ divisor classes.

8. Connections

Brill–Noether theory sits at the centre of curve theory: it is the bridge between the cohomology of a fixed curve (Riemann–Roch and its refinements), the geometry of its Picard variety, and the global geometry of $\mathcal{M}_g$. Where it leads:

  • Higher-genus curves — every result here is downstream of Riemann–Roch on a curve; Brill–Noether is "Riemann–Roch made geometric" by reading $h^0$ as a dimension of a variety, not just a single number.
  • Moduli of curves — the Brill–Noether divisors stratify $\mathcal{M}_g$ and supply the divisor classes needed to compute the Kodaira dimension of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g$.
  • Canonical embedding — Petri injectivity is a statement about quadrics through the canonical image: no quadric is a "sum of two divisors". This is Noether–Enriques–Petri in its modern form.
  • Abelian varieties — $W^r_d(C)\subset\Pic^d(C)\cong J(C)$ are special subvarieties of the Jacobian; their class as algebraic cycles is a Schubert polynomial in $\theta$.
  • Deformation theory — the Petri map computes the tangent space to $W^r_d$. The general framework is deformations of pairs $(C, L)$ with $L$ a line bundle on $C$.
  • Schubert calculus — Kempf's existence proof is a Chern-class / Schubert computation on the Picard variety. The Brill–Noether class polynomial is the Schubert class of a determinantal locus.

Frontier: Brill–Noether theory for vector bundles (rank $\ge 2$) replaces line bundles by higher-rank bundles, and the corresponding "moduli of vector bundles with prescribed cohomology" is much less understood — Mercat's conjecture and its counterexamples are open battlegrounds. The analogue of $\rho$ for vector bundles has been computed in some cases but not in general. A parallel frontier is tropical Brill–Noether theory (Cools–Draisma–Payne–Robeva, Jensen–Payne) which gives a chip-firing-style proof of Brill–Noether and Gieseker–Petri using tropical curves and parking functions on graphs — and produces uniform bounds matching the classical results.
