Optimal transport

Moving mass at least cost — from Monge’s 1781 earthworks to Kantorovich duality, Wasserstein geometry, Brenier maps, and the gradient-flow view of diffusion.

1. The Monge and Kantorovich problems

You have a pile of sand shaped like a measure $\mu$ and a hole shaped like $\nu$, both of unit total mass. Moving a grain from $x$ to $y$ costs $c(x,y)$. Monge’s problem (1781) seeks a map $T$ with $T_\#\mu=\nu$ minimizing $\int c(x,T(x))\,d\mu(x)$ — every grain at $x$ goes to the single location $T(x)$.

The trouble is that a map cannot split mass: if $\mu=\delta_0$ but $\nu=\tfrac12\delta_{-1}+\tfrac12\delta_{1}$, no $T$ exists. Kantorovich (1942) relaxes the map to a coupling (transport plan) $\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)$ — a measure on $X\times Y$ whose marginals are $\mu$ and $\nu$ — and minimizes the linear objective $\int c\,d\pi$:

$$\mathrm{(K)}\qquad \min_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\ \int_{X\times Y} c(x,y)\,d\pi(x,y).$$

This is a linear program over the (compact, convex) transportation polytope, so a minimizer always exists. On a finite ground set it is exactly the classical transportation / assignment problem, and — for a convex cost on the line — the optimal plan is the monotone one, which you can read off by the north-west-corner rule.

2. Kantorovich duality

Every linear program has a dual, and transport is no exception. The dual of $\mathrm{(K)}$ maximizes over pairs of potentials $(\varphi,\psi)$:

$$\mathrm{(D)}\qquad \max_{\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\,\le\,c(x,y)}\ \int \varphi\,d\mu + \int \psi\,d\nu.$$

Strong duality holds: $\min\mathrm{(K)}=\max\mathrm{(D)}$. At the optimum $\psi=\varphi^{c}$ is the $c$-transform of $\varphi$, and complementary slackness forces the inequality $\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\le c(x,y)$ to be an equality precisely on the support of the optimal plan — which is how the dual locates where mass actually moves.

For the distance cost $c(x,y)=|x-y|$ the dual collapses to a single $1$-Lipschitz function, giving Kantorovich–Rubinstein:

$$W_1(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(f)\le 1}\ \int f\,d\mu-\int f\,d\nu.$$

In one dimension this has the clean closed form $W_1=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F_\mu(x)-F_\nu(x)|\,dx$, the area between the two cumulative distribution functions — which is exactly what the widget shades.

3. Wasserstein distances

Fixing the cost $c(x,y)=|x-y|^p$ turns the optimal value into a distance between measures:

$$W_p(\mu,\nu)=\Big(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int |x-y|^p\,d\pi\Big)^{1/p}.$$

On the space $\mathcal{P}_p$ of measures with finite $p$-th moment, $W_p$ is a genuine metric: symmetric, zero only when $\mu=\nu$, and the triangle inequality follows from a gluing lemma for couplings. Crucially it metrizes weak convergence (together with convergence of $p$-th moments): $W_p(\mu_n,\mu)\to0$ iff $\mu_n\rightharpoonup\mu$ and moments converge.

Contrast total variation, which is blind to geometry: $\mathrm{TV}(\delta_0,\delta_\varepsilon)=1$ for every $\varepsilon>0$, while $W_2(\delta_0,\delta_\varepsilon)=\varepsilon\to0$. For two one-dimensional Gaussians there is a closed form, $W_2^2=|m_1-m_2|^2+(\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2$, separating a translation part from a stretch part.

4. Brenier’s theorem and Monge maps

For the quadratic cost $c(x,y)=\tfrac12|x-y|^2$ and an absolutely continuous source $\mu$, the Kantorovich relaxation does not actually need to split anything: Brenier’s theorem says the optimal coupling is induced by a unique map

$$T=\nabla\varphi,\qquad \varphi\ \text{convex},$$

so the optimum is a genuine Monge map after all. The potential solves a Monge–Ampère equation $\det D^2\varphi(x)=\rho_\mu(x)/\rho_\nu(\nabla\varphi(x))$, expressing conservation of mass under the change of variables $y=\nabla\varphi(x)$.

In one dimension a convex $\varphi$ has a non-decreasing derivative, so $T$ is the monotone rearrangement $T=F_\nu^{-1}\circ F_\mu$: it sends the $q$-quantile of $\mu$ to the $q$-quantile of $\nu$. The local stretching $T'=\rho_\mu/\rho_\nu$ is the 1-D Monge–Ampère relation — the map compresses where the target is dense and stretches where it is sparse.

5. Wasserstein geodesics and displacement interpolation

Because $(\mathcal{P}_2,W_2)$ is a metric space, one can ask what its geodesics look like. The answer is McCann’s displacement interpolation: with $T$ the Brenier map from $\mu$ to $\nu$, the constant-speed geodesic is

$$\mu_t=\big((1-t)\,\mathrm{id}+t\,T\big)_\#\mu,\qquad t\in[0,1],$$

which slides each particle along the straight segment from $x$ to $T(x)$. This is utterly different from the linear interpolation $(1-t)\mu+t\nu$, which merely dims one bump while brightening another and is the geodesic for the flat $L^2$ geometry, not for $W_2$.

Functionals that are convex along these geodesics — displacement convexity, due to McCann — include the entropy, potential, and interaction energies. Displacement convexity is the lever behind uniqueness of minimizers and a whole family of functional inequalities (Brunn–Minkowski, log-Sobolev, HWI).

6. Otto calculus and JKO gradient flows

Otto observed that $(\mathcal{P}_2,W_2)$ behaves like an infinite-dimensional Riemannian manifold, with tangent vectors represented by velocity potentials and a continuity-equation metric. On this manifold many diffusion PDEs turn out to be gradient flows of an energy $F$:

$$\partial_t\rho=\nabla\!\cdot\!\big(\rho\,\nabla F'(\rho)\big).$$

The Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto scheme makes this rigorous as a minimizing-movement (implicit Euler) iteration in the Wasserstein metric:

$$\mu_{k+1}=\arg\min_{\rho}\ \frac{1}{2\tau}\,W_2^2(\rho,\mu_k)+F(\rho),$$

and as $\tau\to0$ the interpolated steps converge to the flow. For the Boltzmann entropy $F(\rho)=\int\rho\log\rho$ this is exactly the heat equation; adding a confining potential $\int V\rho$ gives the Fokker–Planck equation, whose stationary state is the Gibbs measure $\rho_\infty\propto e^{-V}$. The free energy $F(\rho_t)$ decreases monotonically — steepest descent in $W_2$.

Connections

Optimal transport has become a connective tissue across analysis, geometry, probability, and data science. The geometric side feeds curvature bounds on metric-measure spaces (Lott–Villani–Sturm); the variational side underlies a large family of nonlinear PDEs as gradient flows; and the computational side (entropic regularization, Sinkhorn) made $W_2$ a practical loss in machine learning. The hard frontiers are higher-dimensional regularity of Brenier maps, the analysis of multi-marginal and unbalanced transport, and quantitative stability.

  • Measure theory — push-forward measures — the change-of-variables $T_\#\mu$ that defines what a transport map even means.
  • Convex optimization — Lagrangian duality — Kantorovich duality is LP duality on the transportation polytope.
  • PDE — the heat equation — revealed here as steepest entropy descent in $W_2$.
  • Large deviations — Sanov’s theorem — entropy as the rate function dovetails with the entropy functional driving the flow.
  • Riemannian geometry — Ricci curvature — synthetic lower Ricci bounds via displacement convexity of entropy.
