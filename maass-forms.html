Maass forms

The non-holomorphic cousin of a modular form: a $\Gamma$-invariant Laplacian eigenfunction on the upper half-plane. They fill in the spectrum that holomorphic forms miss, supply the continuous part of $L^2(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})$ via Eisenstein series, and carry $L$-functions whose Ramanujan bound is still wide open.

Prerequisites. The geometric stage is the upper half-plane with its hyperbolic metric. The holomorphic counterpart lives in modular forms; the spectral viewpoint is dressed up adelically in automorphic forms; the analytic-number-theory payoff plugs into $L$-functions.

1. Definition and the hyperbolic Laplacian

A Maass form for a discrete group $\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ is a smooth function $f:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}$ that is

  1. automorphic: $f(\gamma z)=f(z)$ for every $\gamma\in\Gamma$;
  2. an eigenfunction of the hyperbolic Laplacian: $$\Delta f \;=\; \lambda f,\qquad \Delta \;=\; -y^2\!\left(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2\right);$$
  3. of moderate growth at every cusp: $f(z) = O(y^N)$ as $y\to\infty$ for some $N$.

Two definitional choices deserve attention.

The "$\tfrac14+r^2$" parameterisation

Eigenvalues are written $$\lambda \;=\; \tfrac14+r^2$$ where $r$ is the spectral parameter. This shift is not cosmetic: it is the value of $s(1-s)$ at the symmetry centre $s=\tfrac12+ir$. The Selberg eigenvalue conjecture (§3) becomes the simple statement "$r$ is real". Eigenvalues with $r\in i\mathbb{R}$ — equivalently, $\lambda<\tfrac14$ — are called exceptional.

Compare with holomorphic modular forms

A weight-$k$ holomorphic modular form satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equation $\partial_{\bar z}f=0$ and the modular transformation law $f(\gamma z)=(c z+d)^k f(z)$. A Maass form (weight zero, the standard setting) drops $\partial_{\bar z}f=0$ and replaces it with the second-order PDE $\Delta f=\lambda f$. Holomorphic forms are holomorphic but not Laplacian eigenfunctions in the Maass sense (they are in a weighted sense for a different operator $\Delta_k$); Maass forms are real-analytic but not holomorphic. They live in disjoint corners of $C^\infty(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})$.

2. Spectral decomposition of $L^2(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})$

The space $L^2(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})$ — square-integrable functions on the modular surface, with respect to the hyperbolic measure $\mathrm{d}\mu = y^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y$ — decomposes orthogonally with respect to $\Delta$ into three pieces:

$$L^2(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}) \;=\; L^2_{\mathrm{cusp}} \;\oplus\; L^2_{\mathrm{res}} \;\oplus\; L^2_{\mathrm{Eis}}.$$

Each piece has a different flavour.

Cusp forms — the discrete spectrum

$L^2_{\mathrm{cusp}}$ is the closure of the span of Maass cusp forms: $L^2$ Maass forms whose constant Fourier coefficient at every cusp is zero. They form a countable orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions $\{u_j\}$ with $\Delta u_j = \lambda_j u_j$. Their eigenvalues are isolated, and their density (§6) follows the Weyl law.

Eisenstein series — the continuous spectrum

For each cusp $\mathfrak{a}$ of $\Gamma$ there is a family of Eisenstein series $E_\mathfrak{a}(z,s)$, defined for $\Re s>1$ and continued meromorphically to all of $\mathbb{C}$ (§4). The values on the critical line $\Re s = \tfrac12$ provide a direct integral $$L^2_{\mathrm{Eis}} \;=\; \bigoplus_{\mathfrak{a}} \int_{\Re s = 1/2}^\oplus \mathbb{C}\cdot E_\mathfrak{a}(\,\cdot\,, s)\,\mathrm{d}s.$$ This is the analog of the Plancherel decomposition $L^2(\mathbb{R}) = \int^\oplus e^{i\xi x}\,\mathrm{d}\xi$ on the line.

Residual spectrum

$L^2_{\mathrm{res}}$ is finite-dimensional, spanned by residues of $E_\mathfrak{a}(\cdot,s)$ at simple poles in $(\tfrac12,1]$. For $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, $E(z,s)$ has a single such pole at $s=1$ with constant residue $3/\pi$, so $L^2_{\mathrm{res}}$ is just the line of constants. Higher-rank groups (e.g. $\mathrm{GL}_n$ for $n\ge 3$) develop richer residual spectra — the Speh representations are the famous $\mathrm{GL}_4$ example.

3. Selberg's eigenvalue conjecture

For a congruence subgroup $\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ — that is, $\Gamma\supseteq\Gamma(N)=\ker(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\to\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/N))$ for some $N$ — Selberg conjectured in 1965 that the smallest non-zero Laplacian eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ on cusp forms satisfies $$\lambda_1 \;\ge\; \tfrac14.$$ Equivalently, no exceptional eigenvalues exist: every Maass spectral parameter $r$ is real.

Why is this hard?

The trivial lower bound is $\lambda_1>0$ (cuspidal eigenvalues are bounded away from zero, and the constant function is the only zero-eigenvalue $L^2$ function — that's the residual spectrum). Tightening it to $\tfrac14$ amounts to ruling out Maass forms that grow at the cusp slower than the constant function. Selberg himself proved $\lambda_1\ge 3/16$ via a Kloosterman-sum estimate (the "$1/4$ minus epsilon" argument); the bound has been improved a handful of times since.

Non-congruence groups: counterexamples exist

The "congruence" hypothesis is essential. Sarnak's PhD thesis and subsequent numerical work showed that for non-congruence subgroups (e.g. those obtained by deforming a Fuchsian group through Teichmüller space) exceptional Maass forms with $\lambda<\tfrac14$ do occur. The conjecture is therefore a deep arithmetic property of the congruence world — closely tied to functoriality on $\mathrm{GL}_2$.

4. Eisenstein series and meromorphic continuation

For $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ — the simplest case, one cusp at $\infty$ with stabiliser $\Gamma_\infty=\langle T\rangle$ where $T:z\mapsto z+1$ — the real-analytic Eisenstein series is $$E(z,s) \;=\; \sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma_\infty\backslash\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})} (\Im\gamma z)^s \;=\; \tfrac12\!\!\sum_{\substack{(c,d)\in\mathbb{Z}^2\\ \gcd(c,d)=1}} \frac{y^s}{|cz+d|^{2s}},\qquad \Re s>1.$$ The series is not holomorphic — the variable $s$ is a complex parameter, but $E(z,s)$ as a function of $z$ depends on both $z$ and $\bar z$ through $|cz+d|^{2s}$.

It is a Laplacian eigenfunction

A direct computation gives $\Delta y^s = -y^2\partial_y^2(y^s) = s(1-s)\,y^s$, and Möbius invariance of $\Delta$ propagates this through the sum: $$\Delta E(\cdot,s) \;=\; s(1-s)\,E(\cdot,s).$$ Substituting $s=\tfrac12+ir$ recovers the Maass eigenvalue $\tfrac14+r^2$ — the Eisenstein series at $\Re s=\tfrac12$ are generalised eigenfunctions of $\Delta$ in the same sense that $e^{i\xi x}$ is for $-\partial_x^2$ on the line.

Selberg's meromorphic continuation

The series only converges for $\Re s>1$, but Selberg proved that $E(\cdot,s)$ extends meromorphically to all $s\in\mathbb{C}$ with a functional equation $$E(z,s) \;=\; \phi(s)\,E(z,1-s),\qquad \phi(s) \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,\Gamma(s-\tfrac12)\,\zeta(2s-1)}{\Gamma(s)\,\zeta(2s)}.$$ Crucially, $\phi(s)$ is an explicit rational expression in completed $\zeta$ values; the functional equation $E(z,s)=\phi(s)E(z,1-s)$ contains the functional equation of $\zeta(s)$. This was Selberg's spectral re-derivation of Riemann's continuation.

The Mellin transform of the constant term against $y^{-s}$ extracts $\zeta(2s)$ — analytic continuation of the Eisenstein series therefore gives, as a free byproduct, the analytic continuation and functional equation of $\zeta$.

5. $L$-functions and the Ramanujan conjecture

Let $f$ be a Maass cusp form on $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ with spectral parameter $r$. Solving $\Delta f=(\tfrac14+r^2)f$ by separation of variables in $(x,y)$ gives the Fourier–Whittaker expansion $$f(x+iy) \;=\; \sum_{n\ne 0} a_f(n)\,\sqrt{y}\,K_{ir}(2\pi|n|y)\,e^{2\pi i n x},$$ where $K_{ir}$ is the modified Bessel function — the unique solution of the radial $\Delta$-equation that decays at $y\to\infty$. The Fourier coefficients $a_f(n)$ are real numbers (after a normalisation); they encode the arithmetic content of $f$.

The $L$-function

If $f$ is additionally a Hecke eigenform — joint eigenfunction of all the Hecke operators $T_p$ on the modular surface — then the Dirichlet series $$L(s,f) \;=\; \sum_{n\ge 1} \frac{a_f(n)}{n^s}$$ has an Euler product, an analytic continuation, and a functional equation. Concretely:

  • Euler product: $L(s,f) = \prod_p (1 - a_f(p) p^{-s} + p^{-2s})^{-1}$.
  • Functional equation: $\Lambda(s,f) = \pi^{-s}\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{s+\epsilon+ir}{2}\right)\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{s+\epsilon-ir}{2}\right)L(s,f)$ satisfies $\Lambda(s,f) = (-1)^\epsilon \Lambda(1-s,f)$, where $\epsilon\in\{0,1\}$ is the parity of $f$ ($f(-\bar z)=(-1)^\epsilon f(z)$).
  • Analytic continuation: $\Lambda(s,f)$ is entire — there is no pole at $s=1$ (cusp form, not Eisenstein) and no pole at $s=0$.

Ramanujan–Petersson

The conjecture says $$|a_f(p)| \;\le\; 2 \qquad \text{for every unramified prime }p.$$ For the holomorphic-form version this was proved by Deligne via étale cohomology of Kuga–Sato varieties (a corollary of the Weil conjectures). For Maass forms there is no such geometric handle and the conjecture is open.

6. The Selberg trace formula and the Weyl law

The deepest tool in the Maass-form toolkit is the Selberg trace formula: an identity between a sum over Laplacian eigenvalues (the spectral side) and a sum over conjugacy classes of $\Gamma$ — equivalently, closed geodesics on $\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}$ (the geometric side). Schematically, for a suitable test function $h$ with Fourier transform $\hat h$: $$\sum_{j\ge 0} h(r_j) + \frac{1}{4\pi}\!\int_{-\infty}^\infty h(r)\,\frac{\phi'}{\phi}(\tfrac12+ir)\,\mathrm{d}r \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}{4\pi}\!\int r\tanh(\pi r)\,h(r)\,\mathrm{d}r + \!\!\!\sum_{\{\gamma\}\,\mathrm{hyp}}\!\!\frac{\log N(\gamma_0)}{|N(\gamma)^{1/2}-N(\gamma)^{-1/2}|}\hat h(\log N(\gamma)) + \cdots$$ The left side runs over the spectrum (cuspidal eigenvalues $r_j$ + an Eisenstein integral); the right side has an identity term, an elliptic correction, and a hyperbolic sum where $N(\gamma)$ is the norm of the hyperbolic element $\gamma$ — equivalently, $e^{\ell(\gamma)}$ for the length of the corresponding closed geodesic.

The Weyl law

Choosing $h$ to be a bump function localised near eigenvalue $T$ extracts the leading-order eigenvalue density. The result is the Weyl law: $$N(T) \;:=\; \#\{j : \lambda_j \le T\} \;\sim\; \frac{\mathrm{vol}(\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H})}{4\pi}\,T \qquad (T\to\infty).$$ For $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ this gives $N(T)\sim T/12$ — the cusp form spectrum has the same first-order density as the eigenvalues of $\Delta$ on a compact hyperbolic surface of equal volume.

Prime geodesic theorem

A different choice of test function extracts the geometric side: the asymptotic density of primitive closed geodesics. The result is the prime geodesic theorem $$\pi_{\mathrm{geod}}(X) \;:=\; \#\{\gamma : \ell(\gamma)\le X\} \;\sim\; \frac{e^X}{X}\qquad (X\to\infty),$$ the exact analog of the prime number theorem under the substitution $N=e^X$. Closed geodesics on $\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}$ play the role of primes; the trace formula is the spectral lever, and exceptional eigenvalues control the size of the error term — in close parallel to how the Riemann hypothesis controls the error in $\pi(N)$.

Connections

Maass forms sit at the intersection of analysis (spectral theory of $\Delta$ on a non-compact surface), number theory (Hecke operators, $L$-functions, Ramanujan), and representation theory (the principal series of $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$). The two open frontiers a beginner is most likely to meet are Selberg's eigenvalue conjecture itself — open since 1965, currently $975/4096$ vs. the conjectural $1/4$ — and the Maass form Ramanujan–Petersson conjecture, where the absence of a geometric model (compare Deligne's holomorphic case via Weil) leaves only the slow-grinding $\mathrm{Sym}^k$-functoriality program. On the deeper side, exceptional eigenvalues control the error term in the prime geodesic theorem, link to expander graphs (Ramanujan graphs), and feed into quantum-chaos questions about eigenfunction equidistribution (the "quantum unique ergodicity" theorem of Lindenstrauss).

  • Modular forms — definition — Maass forms are the non-holomorphic counterpart, sharing $\Gamma$-invariance but solving an elliptic PDE instead of $\partial_{\bar z}f=0$.
  • Upper half-plane — hyperbolic metric — supplies the hyperbolic Laplacian $\Delta=-y^2(\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2)$ that defines the eigenvalue problem.
  • Automorphic forms — spectral decomposition — the global adelic version: cuspidal $\oplus$ residual $\oplus$ continuous on $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Q})\backslash\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{A})$.
  • $L$-functions — unified picture — Maass $L$-functions slot into the same template (Euler product, completion, functional equation, Ramanujan-style coefficient bound).
  • Spectral theory — the analyst's view: $\Delta$ on a non-compact finite-volume surface has discrete + continuous spectrum, and Maass forms are exactly the discrete-cuspidal eigenfunctions.
  • Expanders — Selberg's $\lambda_1\ge 3/16$ on $\Gamma_0(N)\backslash\mathbb{H}$ is the analytic prototype of an explicit spectral gap, foreshadowing the Ramanujan-graph constructions of Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak.
